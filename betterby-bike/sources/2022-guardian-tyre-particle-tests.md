# Guardian: car tyre particle pollution versus exhausts (2022)

## Reporting and evidence

Damian Carrington's 3 June 2022 article reports Emissions Analytics tests and interviews its
Nick Molden and University of Leeds researcher James Tate. Read the full four-page capture,
including the p. 2 graphic visually; substantive reporting is on pp. 1–3, followed by a
“Most viewed” page. This is secondary reporting, not a reviewed technical test report.[^article]

Tyre tests used 14 brands on a Mercedes C-Class driven normally on roads, with some tyres
followed through their lifetime. Scales measured mass loss; a behind-tyre sampling system
measured particle mass, number and size down to 6 nanometres. Exhaust measurements came from
four petrol SUVs, model years 2019 and 2020, rather than the same car. The article separately
links an [Emissions Analytics tyre-database release](https://www.emissionsanalytics.com/news/press-release-emissions-analytics-launches-tyre-database)
and reports chemical analysis of 250 tyre types. That release and the underlying test data
were not independently reviewed.

## Main reporting

The article reports used-tyre emissions of 36 mg/km and exhaust emissions averaging 0.02 mg/km,
calling the contrast 1,850-fold. Its graphic gives used tyres as 36.5 mg/km, new tyres as
73.0 mg/km and the exhaust legal limit as 4.5 mg/km. These displayed rounded values do not
exactly reproduce the quoted ratio. Aggressive but legal driving reportedly raised tyre
emissions to 5,760 mg/km; this is not the normal-driving estimate.[^article]

The article reports more than one trillion particles smaller than 23 nm per kilometre and
says airborne-sized particles dominate the count but represent only 11% of tyre-particle mass.
The headline comparison therefore is not a 1,850-fold comparison of airborne mass or health
harm. Molden argues for regulating high-wear/toxic tyres; those recommendations and his
expectation of slightly greater average BEV tyre emissions are not measured policy or
fleet-comparison results. Tate emphasizes expected reductions in BEV weight. Both EV weight
predictions and regulatory statements are historical claims, not verified current conditions.

## Limits and connections

No uncertainty intervals, detailed driving cycle, representative fleet weighting or health-risk
estimate are supplied. The comparison is tyre mass versus exhaust particle mass, not all vehicle
pollutants, ambient exposure or PM10 measured under a common protocol. The article's salmon
and ocean-microplastic references add context, not independently reviewed evidence.
