# Measuring the Street: New Metrics for 21st Century Streets

New York City Department of Transportation (NYCDOT), 2012.
[Original report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2012-10-measuring-the-street.pdf). The supplied filename and
PDF creation metadata place this edition in October 2012; the text does not state a publication date.

## Scope and reporting basis

This 16-page agency report illustrates how NYC evaluates street projects across safety, public space, bus service,
traffic flow, and parking/loading. It presents selected project-level percentage changes and some borough-wide economic
comparisons, not a fully documented research study. Its proposed metrics include crashes and injuries, travel volumes
and speeds, economic vitality, user satisfaction, and environmental/public-health benefits.[^report]

## Reported results

- **Safety:** injuries to all street users decreased 35% on 8th Avenue and 58% on 9th Avenue. Other outcomes are not
  interchangeable: injury crashes decreased 26% at Union Square North and 37% on First/Second Avenues; pedestrian
  crashes decreased 67% on East 180th Street.
- **Business and public space:** locally based 9th Avenue businesses between 23rd and 31st Streets had retail-sales
  growth of up to 49%, versus 3% borough-wide. Brooklyn's Pearl Street plaza reported 172% sales growth versus 18%
  borough-wide, while Manhattan's Pearl Street curbside seating project reported 14% growth at fronting businesses and
  77% more seated pedestrians. Union Square North reported 49% fewer commercial vacancies versus 5% more borough-wide,
  and 74% user preference for the new layout.
- **Transit and cycling:** Fordham Road reported 20% higher bus speeds, 10% higher bus ridership and 71% higher local
  retail sales versus 23% borough-wide. First/Second Avenues reported 18% higher bus speeds, 12% higher bus ridership,
  up to 177% higher bicycle volumes and 47% fewer commercial vacancies versus 2% more borough-wide.
- **Traffic and curb management:** Hoyt Avenue at the RFK Bridge reported a 51% improvement in northbound travel times,
  21% fewer crashes and 37% higher weekend bicycle volumes. Union Square North reported 16% less speeding alongside 14%
  higher median speeds. Midtown signal management reported 10% higher travel speeds and 9% lower peak-hour
  greenhouse-gas emissions. Park Slope parking changes reported 20% shorter parking duration, 18% more unique visitors
  finding parking and 7% lower traffic volumes; Church Avenue loading changes reported 21% higher peak-hour travel
  speeds and 19% higher travel-speed reliability.[^report]

## Interpretation and limitations

NYCDOT presents these cases as evidence that streets can serve more users, improve safety and public space, and maintain
traffic flow. The reported changes are favorable, but the report does not provide a systematic sample, detailed data
collection or analysis, observation dates, baseline counts, uncertainty estimates, or matched control analyses. It does
not establish the distribution of results across all NYC projects or document null/adverse outcomes.[^report]

Projects combine bicycle or bus lanes with signals, turn management, pedestrian refuges, plazas or other changes. Their
results cannot isolate a bike-lane effect. Borough-wide economic trends supply context, not proof that local redesign
caused the difference. “Up to” values are not established as corridor-wide averages; injuries, injury crashes and
crashes are different measures; bicycle volumes do not establish net new cycling or mode share. Underlying project
evaluations were not independently reviewed.
