# Salt Lake City Cuts Car Parking, Adds Bike Lanes, Sees Retail Boost

Michael Andersen, Streetsblog USA, October 6, 2015.
[Original article][original] · [Local PDF](../../raw-sources/news/2015-streetsblog-slc.pdf).

[original]: https://usa.streetsblog.org/2015/10/06/salt-lake-city-cuts-car-parking-adds-bike-lanes-sees-retail-boost

## Reporting and evidence

Andersen, introduced as a writer for PeopleForBikes' Green Lane Project, covers Salt Lake City's downtown 300 South
(Broadway) redesign through an [in-house municipal study][city], reproduced charts and local accounts. Six blocks
of diagonal parking became parallel parking, and parking moved away from the curb on three more blocks, creating
nine blocks of protected lanes. The project cut total auto parking by 30%, reduced part of the street from five
travel lanes to three, and added planters, crosswalk improvements, public art and colored pavement.[^streetsblog2015]

[city]: https://drive.google.com/file/d/0B8tOk7_upXv5djhCajg1Z0I3bmhTVTIxWldwRzA0YjJWNW9R/view?usp=sharing

Using sales-tax data, the city reportedly compared the first halves of **2013 and 2015**: retail sales rose **8.8%
along the project versus 7% citywide**. This is the same municipal comparison later summarized by
[ULI](/sources/2016-active-transportation-real-estate.md), not independent confirmation. Streetsblog's dates differ
from ULI's year-over-year wording; the underlying analysis has not been reviewed to resolve that discrepancy.[^streetsblog2015]

The city's business ombudsman reached 90% of 90 retail, restaurant and service storefronts in door-to-door manager
surveys. Charts show mostly supportive or neutral opinions, but some opposition and reported sales declines.
The sales-response chart's largest category is labeled “good,” not “unchanged.” The city also reportedly found a
**30% increase in bicycle traffic** after lane installation, without count dates or methods in the article.[^streetsblog2015]

## Accounts, interpretation and limitations

Copper Onion manager Jeff Telicson praised the lanes and requested more bicycle parking. Paradise Palm owner John
Mueller reported business up 20% since the previous year and attributed benefits to calmer traffic and more pedestrians;
this is one merchant's account, not the corridor's tax-record result. Bike Utah's Phil Sarnoff described slower traffic
and more yielding; city coordinator Becka Roolf emphasized a more pleasant walking environment. Andersen proposes
that lingering and street quality, rather than bicycle traffic alone, explain the sales gain. These are accounts and
hypotheses, not measured mechanisms or an isolated lane effect.[^streetsblog2015]

The article supplies no baseline sales, business panel details, uncertainty, matched controls or bicycle-count protocol.
Citywide growth cannot control for every local difference; the combined redesign cannot identify the effects of parking
removal or bike lanes separately. Survey coding, per-question denominators and the meaning of “good” are not explained.
The linked municipal study remains unreviewed. Repeated NYC sales and customer-spending references are context, not
new studies conducted for this article.

All eight PDF pages were read as text, and the three charts on pp. 3–4 were inspected visually. Substantive reporting
ends on p. 6; the July 2024 print date and related stories are not the article's publication date or evidence.
