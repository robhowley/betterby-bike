# Americans’ love affair with big cars is killing them

The Economist, August 31, 2024; supplied browser PDF captured September 3, 2024. No individual byline appears.
[Original article](https://www.economist.com/interactive/united-states/2024/08/31/americans-love-affair-with-big-cars-is-killing-them).

## Reporting and analysis basis

This U.S. data-journalism article combines an original crash analysis, earlier research, manufacturer responses and
safety-expert interviews. Its opening Grand Forks fatal rear-end collision illustrates vehicle mismatch, but also
involved a driver reported to have been drinking; it is not a controlled test of weight.[^economist2024]

The Economist matched police crash reports from 14 states in 2013–2023 to VinAudit curb weights using vehicle
identification numbers. Roughly 10 million matched crashes became about 7.5 million two-vehicle crashes after
missing-data exclusions. States were selected for detailed, accessible records; they covered more than one-third of
the U.S. population. The article calls the sample representative but supplies no weighting or representativeness test.
Its regression adjusts for own-vehicle weight, driver age/gender, location population density and seatbelt use.[^economist2024]

## Main evidence and competing accounts

- Heavier vehicles generally had fewer own-vehicle deaths but more deaths in the other vehicle. An additional
  1,000 lb in the collision partner was associated with a 0.06-percentage-point higher fatality probability after
  adjustment, about 66% of the reported 0.09% average. This is conditional on a recorded crash, not travel risk.
- The top weight decile (at least 5,000 lb) had 5.9 own-vehicle and 20.2 partner-vehicle deaths per 10,000 crashes,
  versus 5.4 and 10.3 in the next decile (4,500–5,000 lb). The article estimates 12% fewer national multi-car crash
  deaths, about 2,300 using 2023 totals, if the heaviest tenth were downsized. It labels this back-of-the-envelope,
  not an observed policy effect.[^economist2024]
- Historical research is not uniformly a case for lighter cars: the article reports earlier estimates of increased
  fatalities following 1970s–1980s downsizing, alongside later work on harm to other road users and diminishing
  occupant protection. It identifies White (2004), Li (2012), Anderson and Auffhammer (2014), and O’Neill and
  Kyrychenko (2004); these papers were not reviewed or counted as additional ingested evidence.
- Ford emphasized crash-avoidance technology and factors beyond weight; GM cited compatibility improvements;
  Stellantis cited compliance with federal standards. Researchers and safety advocates described incentives to buy
  heavier vehicles and urged regulation. The article criticizes occupant-focused ratings and tax/fuel-economy
  incentives; these are reporting and policy arguments, not evaluated remedies. It discloses that Stellantis's
  biggest shareholder part-owns The Economist's parent company.[^economist2024]

## Access and inference limits

Read text from all 12 PDF pages; substantive reporting occupies pp. 1–9. Recovered the original URL from embedded
links and visually checked p. 7's regression and downsizing paragraphs. A repeated interactive chart overlays some
prose in the print rendering; extracted text recovers the weight-group figures underneath. Retained numbers come
from prose, not inferred chart coordinates. The live interactive, crash records, code and cited primary studies were
not reviewed; no separate technical report or primary-study URLs were available in the PDF's embedded links.

The article does not provide uncertainty intervals, full model specifications, state-by-state coverage or a detailed
missing-data assessment. Listed controls do not establish a causal weight effect or account explicitly for impact
speed and vehicle geometry. Its two-vehicle occupant outcomes do not quantify pedestrian/cyclist risk, crash
frequency per mile, single-vehicle outcomes or the effects of bicycle lanes.[^economist2024]
