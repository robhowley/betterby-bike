# Low-Income Americans Walk and Bike to Work the Most

Tanya Snyder, Streetsblog USA, May 8, 2014.
[Original article][original] · [Local PDF](../../raw-sources/news/2014-streetsblog-income-biking.pdf).

[original]: https://usa.streetsblog.org/2014/05/08/low-income-americans-walk-and-bike-to-work-the-most

## Reporting and evidence

Snyder covers a newly released [U.S. Census Bureau report by Brian McKenzie][census] on walking and biking to work,
using American Community Survey (ACS) data. The reproduced Census figures identify **2008–2012** data. This is
secondary reporting of national commuting patterns, not a new survey or an evaluation of bicycle infrastructure.[^streetsblog2014]

[census]: http://www.census.gov/prod/2014pubs/acs-25.pdf

Workers in households with annual income below $10,000 had reported bicycle and walking commute rates of **1.5%**
and **8.2%**. Rates declined through much of the income range, then rose somewhat at the highest levels. Although
Snyder writes of what people earn, the reproduced chart labels the measure **household income**. Workers with no
available vehicle walked at four times and biked at three-and-a-half times the rates of those with one available
vehicle; rates declined with additional vehicles.[^streetsblog2014]

The article also reports differences by education, race/ethnicity, age, children, gender and geography. Education
does not follow a simple income gradient: graduate/professional degree holders had the highest bicycle commute
rate (0.9%), while people without a high-school diploma had the highest walking rate (3.7%). Younger workers had
higher rates, households raising children had lower rates, and women's bicycle commute rate was 0.3% versus men's
0.8%, despite similar walking rates. City and metropolitan-area estimates differed substantially.[^streetsblog2014]

## Interpretation and limitations

Snyder argues that safe walking and cycling infrastructure is not primarily a concern of affluent recreational
riders. Her explanations involving licensing, fitness, parenting and changing culture are interpretations, not
causal effects measured by these comparisons. The article does not estimate infrastructure benefits by income or
show that low income or vehicle availability causes a particular travel choice.[^streetsblog2014]

ACS measures usual travel to work in the previous week and records only the mode covering the greatest distance
when several modes are used. It therefore misses walking or cycling access legs to transit and does not describe
all-purpose travel. The article says work journeys account for less than 20% of all trips. It supplies no sample
sizes, confidence intervals or adjusted demographic comparisons; the underlying Census report and the separately
mentioned National Household Travel Survey were not reviewed.[^streetsblog2014]

All nine PDF pages were read as extracted text, and the Census charts on pp. 1, 3 and 5 were inspected visually.
The substantive article ends on p. 6; the August 2024 print date, related stories and footer are not publication dates
or evidence. Exact values were taken from prose, not estimated from unlabeled bars.
