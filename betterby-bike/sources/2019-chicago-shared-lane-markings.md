# Advancing healthy cities through safer cycling: An examination of shared lane markings

Nicholas N. Ferenchak and Wesley E. Marshall. _International Journal of Transportation Science and Technology_
8 (2019):136–145. [Publication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijtst.2018.12.003) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2019-sharrows-are-harmful.pdf).

## Study and scope

The study compares police-recorded Chicago bicyclist injuries, including dooring, in 2011–2012 and 2013–2014.
Groups were classified by treatments installed in 2012 or 2013: 42 census block groups added only sharrows, 149 added
only bicycle lanes (standard, buffered and/or protected), and 1,948 added no treatments. Nineteen mixed-treatment
and 20 lane-upgrade groups were excluded. “None” means no new treatment, not necessarily no existing facilities.[^paper]

Injuries were assigned spatially with a 50-foot block-group buffer, allowing border injuries to count in both groups.
ACS five-year bicycle-commuter estimates supplied an exposure proxy. Negative binomial models examined injury-count
changes; Kruskal–Wallis tests compared changes in commuter-weighted annual injuries per 100 bicycle commuters.
The authors substituted one rider for null commuter values when calculating rates.[^paper]

## Results

Sharrow groups had the largest increase in mean resident bicycle commuters: 50.3%, versus 20.9% with no new treatment
and 13.8% with lanes; only the first two within-group changes reached p < 0.05. This does not measure use of the
installed facilities or isolate induced cycling.[^paper]

Weighted total injury rates rose 103.8% in sharrow groups, compared with 12.1% in no-new-treatment groups and 10.2%
in lane groups. The sharrow within-group change had p = 0.085; between-group changes differed from no-new-treatment
groups (p = 0.004) and lane groups (p = 0.050). Lanes versus no new treatment did not differ (p = 0.779).
Dooring rates rose with sharrows and fell in the other groups, but none of these within-group dooring-rate changes
reached p < 0.05.[^paper]

The count models give a different picture: the authors report significant decreases in total, dooring and non-dooring
injuries for sharrow and no-new-treatment groups. Lane groups had a significant total-injury increase and dooring
decrease, with an inconclusive non-dooring change. The paper attributes the contrast with weighted rates to where
injury changes occurred relative to commuter concentrations. These results must not be collapsed into a claim that
sharrow injury counts doubled.[^paper]

## Interpretation and limitations

This is an observational block-group comparison, not a corridor-level causal estimate. Resident commuters do not
measure all cycling, trips, distance or riding through a particular block group. Aggregation assumes treatment effects
throughout the block group; roadway conditions, treatment quantity, demographics and socioeconomic differences were
not resolved by the analysis. Injury severity was unavailable. The small sharrow group and pooled lane types limit
generalization and do not identify a protected-lane-specific effect.[^paper]

The authors question sharrows' safety effectiveness in this Chicago setting and call for further research. A proposed
“false sense of security” is a hypothesis, not a measured mechanism. The paper does not test every sharrow function,
road context or marking variant. The supplied filename is stronger than the study's conclusions.[^paper]

All ten PDF pages were read as extracted text; Tables 3–5 on p. 142 were also checked visually. Table 4's baseline
total rates do not equal its displayed dooring plus non-dooring rates; this remains unexplained. Values are retained
as published, not silently recomputed. Supplementary data and cited studies were not independently reviewed.
