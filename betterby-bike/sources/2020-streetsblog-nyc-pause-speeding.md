# By the Way, NYPD, the Speeding Epidemic is Certainly Not Over

## Publication and coverage

Gersh Kuntzman, Streetsblog NYC, May 4, 2020. The [captured publication](../../raw-sources/news/by-the-way-nypd-the-speeding-epidemic-is-certainly-not-over-streetsblog-new-york-city.pdf)
was read in full, including the chart and caption on p. 2; substantive reporting occupies pp. 1–4 of six.
This is original data reporting and NYPD comment with an argumentative framing, not a controlled safety study.
The underlying city open-data records, interactive chart and StreetLight analysis were not independently reviewed.

## Reporting and evidence

At an April 24 City Council hearing, NYPD Transportation Bureau Chief Mike Pilecki described speeding as
trending down. Kuntzman contrasts a short-term retreat from the peak with camera-issued summonses reportedly
83% higher during the 22 weekdays beginning with the March 23 shutdown than in a comparable January–February
period. The text reports 25,541 summonses on April 21, versus more than 26,000 at the earlier peak. The chart
plots the two periods by ordinal weekday, not individual calendar dates. The article also reports a 99% ticket
increase for March 23–April 3 and more than 71% thereafter, relative to earlier comparison periods.[^article]

NYPD told Streetsblog that Highway Patrol issued 1,524 speeding tickets during April 14–20 versus 1,174 in the
same period of 2019. On April 20, the reported counts were 263 Highway Division tickets and 17,865 camera
summonses. Pilecki said no Highway Bureau personnel had been added but described more radar patrols, visible
marked cars, pace-setting patrols and local-road deployments informed by camera data. These are agency accounts
of enforcement, not measured effects of those tactics.[^article]

The article reports citywide collisions down 77% and injuries down 75% between late March and late April,
attributing these figures to NYPD, alongside StreetLight's estimated 80–90% reduction in vehicle miles traveled.
Kuntzman interprets reduced traffic as allowing higher speeds while reducing opportunities for collisions, and
raises concern about drivers retaining faster-trip expectations after the pandemic. The mechanism and future
behavior are interpretations, not tested results.[^article]

## Limits and reconciliation

Camera summonses are counts of detected violations, not the fraction of drivers speeding or a distribution of
speeds. The article supplies no camera-hour or vehicle-passage denominator, coverage-change assessment or exact
calendar dates for the pre-shutdown 22-weekday comparison. It describes cameras as operating in residential
areas, so their counts do not measure highway speeding. Police and camera ticket totals also cover different
enforcement settings and cannot establish relative effectiveness.

The crash and travel estimates lack matched baseline definitions and mode-specific exposure; combining their
percentages would not establish cyclist risk per trip or a precise change in collision risk per mile. This NYC
shutdown comparison is distinct from the [Chicago camera evaluation coverage](2022-streetsblog-speed-camera-equity.md):
it does not estimate the safety effect of installing cameras, removing congestion or redesigning streets.
