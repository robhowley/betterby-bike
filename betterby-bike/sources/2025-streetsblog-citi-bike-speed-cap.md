# Citi Bike riders and the 15 mph speed cap

## Publication and reporting basis

Jonah Schwarz's July 11, 2025 Streetsblog NYC article reports rider reactions to Lyft reducing electric
Citi Bikes' assisted top speed from 18 to 15 mph the previous month at city officials' direction.
The [captured article](../../raw-sources/news/citi-bike-riders-are-pissed-about-eric-adams-s-15-mph-speed-limit-streetsblog-new-york-cit.pdf)
contains reporting on PDF pp. 1–6, followed by site navigation. All extracted pages were read, including
photo captions and the social-media quotation; there are no research tables or methodological appendices.
This is interview-based journalism and an illustrative fare calculation, not a policy evaluation.[^schwarz2025]

## Reported experiences and costs

Queens cycling advocate Jim Burke, 59, says electric assistance enabled him to keep riding after a heart
attack and stroke. He describes feeling less safe at the reduced speed in motor traffic and considering
buying his own e-bike. Other interviewed riders describe reduced confidence in accelerating away from
hazards or no perceived improvement for pedestrians. These are selected personal accounts, not measured
crash risk or a representative satisfaction survey.[^schwarz2025]

The article reports motor assistance cutting off at 15 mph on staff rides, while noting Lyft had not
explained the mechanism. A customer's description of braking around 12 mph and a Reddit user's report
of harder low-assist pedaling are unverified experiences, not established specifications. The reported
sequence is 20 to 18 mph in 2023, then 18 to 15 mph in 2025: the cumulative reduction from 20 is 25%,
not a 25% reduction from 18.[^schwarz2025]

For a hypothetical six-mile journey continuously averaging the cap, the article calculates 20 minutes
at 18 mph versus 24 at 15 mph, a 20% increase in time and time-priced charges. It quotes a member rate
of $0.25/minute, membership costing up to $219.99, and trip prices of $5.44 versus $6.53 (an additional
$1.09). The stated per-minute rate alone gives $5 versus $6; the article does not explain the extra
amounts. Stops, actual speeds and unchanged journey segments are not modeled, so this is not an
observed 20% increase in average fares or door-to-door travel time.[^schwarz2025]

## Limits and contribution

Streetsblog says the administration supplied no data supporting the lower cap's safety benefit and that
its reporters could not identify supporting research. That is a reporting limitation, not proof of no
benefit or of increased danger. NYU transportation researcher Marcel Moran predicts reduced attractiveness;
the article reports no cancellation, ridership-loss, revenue or injury evaluation, and Lyft did not respond
to repeated requests for comment. Its 70% e-bike share of Citi Bike rides has no specified observation period
or underlying dataset in the capture.[^schwarz2025]

This adds original accounts of how assisted speed and minute-based pricing may affect usable bikeshare
access. Existing Citi Bike commercial-activity research addresses a different outcome. No new Finding was
created from the anecdotes or hypothetical calculation. The account informs
[access quality synthesis](/topics/active-commuting-and-access.md#assistance-and-pricing-affect-usable-bikeshare-access),
without establishing a safest speed cap, population access loss or mode shift.

[^schwarz2025]: Jonah Schwarz, Streetsblog NYC, July 11, 2025, PDF pp. 1–3, speed changes and rider interviews; pp. 3–4, “Slower rides, higher costs,” fare illustration and customer accounts; pp. 5–6, usage claims and Moran's prediction.
