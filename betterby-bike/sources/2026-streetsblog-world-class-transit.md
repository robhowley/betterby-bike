# Analysis: What It Would Take To Put America First in Transit Again

## Publication and coverage

Kea Wilson, Streetsblog USA, January 14, 2026, 7:00 AM EST.
[Original article](https://usa.streetsblog.org/2026/01/14/analysis-what-it-would-take-for-america-to-be-first-on-transit-again).
All substantive article text and the text-bearing comparison chart were read (PDF pp. 1–7).
Wilson reports Transportation for America's [World-Class Transit analysis](https://t4america.org/resource/world-class-transit/)
and interviews lead author Corrigan Salerno. The underlying analysis and interactive city data were not reviewed.

## Reporting and evidence

The article describes a roughly $4.6-trillion, 20-year transit expansion scenario, compared with projected
$6.3-trillion highway spending over the same horizon. Its chart reports 27 transit vehicles per 100,000
residents for the average US urban resident, 36 for the top ten US cities and 133 for its selected
world-class cities. The chart combines NTD vehicles operating in maximum service with UITP city fleet
data; these are not necessarily identical measures. Salerno calls vehicles per capita a rough proxy,
not a direct measure of useful service, and notes that walkable and bikeable neighborhoods may need
fewer vehicles. The article describes density-adjusted local expansion estimates but does not give
reproducible adjustment methods.[^wilson]

Against projected US car-ownership spending above $77 trillion during 2026–2045, the analysis reportedly
estimates over $5.4 trillion in avoided ownership costs if the expansion reduces the ownership rate by
one-quarter of a percent each year. This is an assumed response, not observed ownership change or a
causal estimate. Wilson presents it as an investment that could pay for itself; the article does not
supply a full, auditable net-benefit calculation.[^wilson]

Salerno argues that large expansions are feasible and urges ambition comparable to the interstate
highway program. The reporting mentions service and maintenance spending as well as fleet purchases,
but says land-use reform and detailed financing mechanisms were beyond the report's scope. These
implementation gaps matter for whether more vehicles would provide usable alternatives to driving.
Claims about public-health and wider economic gains are arguments, not quantified evaluated effects here.[^wilson]

## Limits and reconciliation

The article uses $4.6 trillion repeatedly but $4.7 trillion once; the discrepancy remains unresolved.
It supplies neither uncertainty intervals nor detailed price-year, discounting, baseline or ownership-rate
calculations. Its chart compares selected cities, not a representative global sample. Vehicle counts
alone do not establish frequency, reliability, accessibility, destination coverage or bicycle access.

The existing [Freedom to Move household-savings Finding](../findings/access/us-freedom-to-move-household-savings.md)
concerns a different reduced-driving scenario through 2050. These totals should not be added or treated
as independent confirmation of one intervention. Existing AAA ownership-cost findings provide annual
new-car cost context, not validation of the projected ownership response.
