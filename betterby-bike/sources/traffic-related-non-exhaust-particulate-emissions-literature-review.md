# Non-exhaust traffic related emissions. Brake and tyre wear PM

Theodoros Grigoratos and Giorgio Martini, European Commission Joint Research Centre, 2014.
EUR 26648 EN, JRC89231, [doi:10.2790/21481](https://doi.org/10.2790/21481).
The [full report](../../raw-sources/research/jrc89231-online-final-version-2-pdf.pdf) supplies the publication
identity and supporting discussion missing from the earlier cropped executive summary. Its summary matches
that capture's previously recorded wording and numerical sequence; this is one publication, not new independent evidence.
The existing Source path is retained.

## Review basis

This narrative literature review draws on peer-reviewed papers, project reports, technical publications and
theses. It compares laboratory dynamometers and road simulators, on-road sampling, emissions inventories and
receptor models that infer sources from chemical signatures. It is not a pooled meta-analysis or a new fleet
experiment. The reviewed evidence covers varied vehicles and locations, including European and US studies;
no reproducible search protocol, study-selection count or formal risk-of-bias assessment is supplied.
Direct brake/tyre wear is the focus; road-dust resuspension and clutch wear are excluded from detailed analysis.[^jrc2014]

## Results and interpretation

The review reports nearly equal exhaust/non-exhaust contributions to traffic-related PM10, with brake wear
accounting for 16%–55% of non-exhaust PM10 in urban settings versus about 3% on freeways. These historical
source shares are neither all-ambient-PM shares nor measured effects of reducing driving. It anticipates a
rising relative non-exhaust contribution as exhaust controls tighten, not necessarily rising absolute emissions.
Wear contributes fine as well as coarse particles; generated wear mass is not the same as airborne PM10.[^jrc2014]

Tables 5 and 10 report average light-duty/passenger-vehicle PM10 factors of 6.7 mg per vehicle-km for brakes
and 6.3 for tyres. These summarize heterogeneous inventories and studies, not precision-weighted estimates
or current universal factors. Section 2.5 reports much lower brake factors at a highway site than at an urban
street canyon; tyre detection sometimes fails, and the review explains possible sampling/tracer limitations
rather than proving zero emissions. Its text and tables also disagree on some values.[^jrc2014]

Health sections describe plausible hazards from particle size and metal/organic composition, but no
comprehensive direct quantification of human health effects attributable specifically to brake or tyre wear.
Some cell studies show inflammatory responses; some tyre-road rat studies instead report little adverse
cardiopulmonary effect. These different exposures and experimental endpoints cannot establish equal toxicity
to exhaust, absence of harm, or a number of illnesses avoided by a street redesign.[^jrc2014]

## Access and limitations

Read extracted front matter, executive summary, introduction, brake generation/size discussion, sections
2.5–2.6 and 3.5–3.6, Tables 5 and 10, and conclusions including Table 11. This was a focused substantive
reading, not review of every chemistry/size-distribution section, reference or figure image. Underlying studies
were not independently accessed. The earlier crop was not reopened in this task; its existing Source account
was compared with the full report's executive summary.

Nonstandard sampling, different driving cycles, brake/tyre composition, vehicle load, road surfaces and
imperfect chemical tracers limit comparability. Section 3.5 gives Panko et al. (2013) as 7.0 mg per vehicle-km,
but Table 10 gives 2.4; executive-summary ranges also differ from the conclusions. No single range should be
silently treated as exhaustive. The review predates modern fleets and cannot compare present electric and
combustion vehicles or estimate cyclist exposure or a cycling-project benefit.
