# Injuries to pedal cyclists on New Zealand roads, 1988-2007

Sandar Tin Tin, Alistair Woodward and Shanthi Ameratunga. *BMC Public Health* 10:655, published 30 October 2010. [Publication](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-10-655) · [Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2010-10-nz-bike-injuries.pdf).

## Study and scope

This descriptive national study compared road injuries resulting in death or hospital inpatient treatment across 1988–91, 1996–99 and 2003–07. Mortality Collection and National Minimum Dataset records supplied cases; Household Travel Surveys supplied annualised hours travelled. Participants were aged five or older. Hospital cases were first admissions to public hospitals, with a principal injury diagnosis and a stay of at least one day. Injury profiles used up to ten diagnoses per case; a separate analysis estimated serious injuries consistent with Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) scores of at least 3.[^paper]

## Results

- Cycling injuries per million hours fell from 25.61 in 1988–91 to 21.38 in 1996–99, then rose to 30.74 in 2003–07. Only motorcycling had a higher rate among the five travel categories compared. Serious cycling injury rates also rose after 1996–99, but remained below 1988–91 levels.
- In 2003–07, non-collision crashes accounted for 40.35% of cases; collisions with cars, pick-up trucks or vans accounted for 25.51% of all cases and 38.49% of serious cases. Motor-vehicle collision cases and other crashes had different injury profiles.
- Ages 5–14 had the highest overall cycling injury rate, especially for crashes without motor vehicles, but the same age pattern did not hold for serious injuries. Males had higher rates than females in both motor-vehicle and other crash categories.
- Traumatic brain injury rates fell between the first two periods, while injuries to other body regions generally increased. The authors explicitly could not attribute the brain-injury decline to the 1994 helmet law.[^paper]

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors recommended injury prevention alongside cycling promotion, addressing both motor-vehicle collisions and cyclist-only crashes. Their suggestions about traffic calming, cycleways, road surfaces and skills training were not intervention effects measured in this study. Likewise, their safety-in-numbers discussion offered a possible explanation, not a causal test.[^paper]

The analysis excluded emergency-department-only, primary-care and self-treated injuries. Coding errors, the ICD transition in 1999–2000, changing admission practices and estimated injury severity may affect comparisons. Cycling exposure came from relatively few surveyed trips; survey non-response and population coverage add uncertainty. The reported confidence intervals assumed Poisson injury counts. Comparisons between modes did not control for differences in roads used. These are historical injury rates, not current risk estimates or a net health-benefit analysis.[^paper]
