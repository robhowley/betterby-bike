# Remove It and They Will Disappear

Jill Kruse, Surface Transportation Policy Project, proceedings paper labeled “Session #1.” The expanded title is
*Remove It and They Will Disappear: New Evidence Why Building New Roads Isn't Always the Answer*.
[Supplied publication](../../raw-sources/research/1998-remove-it-and-they-will-disappear.pdf).
The filename assigns 1998, consistent with the policy discussion, but the paper has no explicit publication date
or named conference. PDF creation metadata is February 2000, not proof of publication year.

## Reporting basis

Kruse presents a policy argument using secondary research and selected city accounts, not an original traffic
study. The central source is the March 1998 *Traffic Impact of Highway Capacity Reductions: Assessment of the
Evidence and Report on Modeling*, attributed to a team led by Phil Goodwin at University College London.
Kruse describes 60 worldwide cases involving maintenance/repair, natural disasters or deliberate policy changes.
The underlying report is identified through ordering information and historical web pointers on p. 9;
no verified direct report URL is supplied here. It was not independently reviewed.[^kruse]

## Main results and interpretations

- Kruse reports an average 25% of traffic disappearing following closures, with 60% in some cases, without defining
  the pooled denominator, weighting or case selection. Local and wider-area percentages differ substantially:
  Nürnberg's “nearly 80%” displaced-traffic account accompanies a 7% overall reduction in 1988–1992;
  the West Side Highway account combines 53% lost trips, 93% of those not reappearing elsewhere, and an 8%
  overall reduction attributed to the British review. These are not interchangeable effect sizes.
- A Hammersmith Bridge before/after user survey reportedly found 21% of former bridge car commuters no longer
  drove to work after closure to vehicles other than buses and bicycles. Transit, walking and cycling are mentioned,
  but their shares, respondent count and attrition are not given. Surrounding congestion reportedly did not markedly
  increase, without a numerical delay measure.
- Other favorable accounts include a 27% car-travel decline after the Osaka–Kobe network was restored following
  the 1995 earthquake, and Hamm's reported 16% traffic reduction on most of a road narrowed from four lanes to two,
  alongside 54% fewer traffic accidents. Exposure, controls and uncertainty are not supplied.
- Kruse acknowledges that some capacity reductions may produce no measurable traffic reduction. Network conditions,
  policy incentives and household activities can reinforce or undermine changes. The quoted researchers' conclusion
  is that well-designed, policy-justified reductions need not automatically be rejected for inevitable unacceptable
  congestion, not that every closure eliminates traffic or delay.[^kruse]

## Policy and economic claims

The paper discusses induced travel, bypasses shifting economic activity, and San Francisco/Portland waterfront
revival. These are secondary research claims and descriptive examples, not controlled estimates of closure-induced
jobs, sales or cycling gains. The broad claim that closures can boost economies is Kruse's policy interpretation.
The TEA-21 funding discussion and proposed British traffic-reduction legislation describe contemporary options and
proposals, not current rules or subsequently verified outcomes.[^kruse]

## Access and limitations

All nine pages were read as extracted text, including reporting methods, qualifications and resources; the central
case-results page (p. 3) was also checked visually. The supplied paper contains no supporting tables or figures.
It does not provide review selection criteria, full case results, uncertainty estimates or a reproducible definition
of “disappeared” traffic. Route changes, mode changes and avoided journeys cannot be quantified separately from this
account. Earthquake disruption and combined urban improvements complicate attribution. None of the cited primary
reports, surveys or datasets was independently read. Bundle searches found no duplicate publication or existing
Finding for these bounded results.
