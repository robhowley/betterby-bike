# Understanding Barriers to Bicycle Access & Use in Black and Hispanic Communities in New Jersey

Charles Brown, Elizabeth Harvey and James Sinclair, Rutgers Alan M. Voorhees Transportation Center.
[Supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/research/minority-report-draft-final-pdf.pdf) ·
[Original download URL](https://njbikeped.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Minority-Report-Draft_FINAL.pdf).
No publication date identified in the reviewed pages. PDF creation metadata says November 2017;
the URL's 2022 directory is not evidence of publication date. The filename retains “Draft_FINAL”.

## Study and main results

Paper pedestrian-intercept surveys were administered in English and Spanish in June–August 2015 at
selected high-footfall locations in a reported 34 New Jersey municipalities. Selection targeted large
Black and Hispanic populations and geographic/development diversity; surveyors did not screen by race.
Most surveying occurred on weekday afternoons, with some weekend sites. “Bicyclists” had ridden in the
past twelve months, not necessarily regularly; others received a different questionnaire. Two December
2015 New Brunswick focus groups explored experiences and preferences.[^report]

- Traffic-collision fear leads the reported barrier rankings. Crime, police profiling, maintenance,
  pavement and family responsibilities also appear, but the narrative's top-three list conflicts with
  Table 5. Multiple-response questions and unclear denominators prevent interpreting its percentages
  as population prevalence or shares choosing a single most important barrier.
- Figure 40 reports perceived unfair police stops among 23% of Black past-year riders, 14% of Hispanic
  riders and 28% of Mixed-Race riders. These are retrospective self-reports without exposure adjustment,
  not independently adjudicated stops. Black focus-group accounts describe avoiding particular towns.
- Figure 43 reports 77% wanting a bicycle lane on their street (83% of past-year riders; 73% of others).
  The question does not specify protection. Focus groups welcomed physical separation but also described
  impractical commutes, family obligations, crime concerns and mixed opinions about whether lanes alone
  would change riding.
- Bicycle availability and security matter alongside routes: Figure 44's overall values report 33%
  ever experiencing bicycle theft (42% of riders, 24% of non-riders); Figure 45 reports no safe home
  storage for 8% of riders versus 22% of non-riders. Theft subgroup chart alignment is defective, so
  no race-specific theft estimate is extracted. Costs were considered high by 28% after excluding
  “not sure” responses. These do not quantify the effect of subsidies, storage or theft on cycling.
- Authors recommend protected lanes, secure parking, affordable bicycle access and education/outreach.
  They also favor access to leisure destinations and stronger police-community relationships. These
  are proposals, not evaluated interventions or demonstrated safety/equity benefits.[^report]

## Material limitations

Reported survey totals conflict: 2,061 (p. 6), 2,062 (p. 21), and 2,661 (p. 29). Table 2 has varying
item totals and includes under-18 respondents despite an adult target and prose claiming their exclusion.
The Black focus group has 16 participants; Hispanic attendance is 11 on p. 19 but 10 on pp. 6 and 26.
Purposeful site/time selection is not a probability sample of statewide residents. No weighting or
response-rate account establishes representativeness; precise item bases and uncertainty are often absent.

Other reporting conflicts include 15% overall unfair stops in prose versus 14% in Figure 40, and 85%
prospective bikeshare use in prose versus 82% in Figure 38. Bikeshare questionnaires also route unaware
respondents past the intention question and offer “maybe”, absent from the plotted yes/no split.
Do not treat that figure as demand among all residents. Appendices show several multiple-response
questions whose reported distributions total about 100%; person-level prevalence cannot be assumed.
No underlying data were reviewed or used to repair these discrepancies.[^report]

Focused reading of the 64-page PDF: opening material, pp. 6–7 and 18–52, references and both appended
survey instruments. Literature review pp. 8–17 was not fully read; its cited studies were not independently
reviewed. Results were read as extracted text, with visual inspection of Table 5 and Figures 43–44.
