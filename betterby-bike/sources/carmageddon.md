## Road capacity and induced demand

The cropped chapter opening dates the New York collapse to **December 15, 1973**, describes the elevated
highway's construction between **1929 and 1951** and its route from southern Manhattan to Seventy-Second Street,
and quotes *New York Times* editorialist Simeon Strunsky's praise of it. These are secondary historical accounts;
no original records or dated editorial citation are supplied. The author's opening assertion that the collapse
proved city congestion was only tangentially linked to road space is an interpretation, not an estimated
relationship: this page contains no before/after traffic counts or delay measurements. The worker-access
narrative ends mid-sentence.[^westside]

Pages 114–115 combine historical narrative, attributed research and an explanation of travel choices, not original
analysis. The West Side Highway account describes a severe initial jam after its December 1973 collapse, but quotes
Sam Schwartz saying the predicted subsequent traffic disaster never appeared and the cars' destinations were unknown.
The book reports around 70,000 daily vehicles before closure and later increased travel into southern Manhattan's
business district with fewer people arriving by car, without counts, methods or precise follow-up dates.[^demand]

For expansion, it attributes a tentative **2% increase in overall driving per 10% increase in road capacity** to an
unnamed British Department for Transport-commissioned study based on U.S. and Dutch highway-network research.
It contrasts this with unnamed urban estimates of **up to 40% of added capacity used after six months and 100%
after four years**. Those latter percentages describe added capacity used, not growth relative to all prior driving.
The text acknowledges disputed scale/context and quotes higher expected induced demand in urban or highly congested
settings. No study titles, years, references, methods, tables or uncertainty intervals are supplied.[^demand]

The author explains how alternatives can change driving choices and argues that widening's initial speed benefit
can disappear. This is an interpretation, not a measured universal congestion trajectory. The passage also says
induced demand is not inherently bad; its rural Tanzania access example continues beyond the supplied spread.
No evaluated cycling, access or welfare effect is given.[^demand]

## Toll-road cases and forecasts (pages 116–117)

The opening continuation argues that additional road access can expand employment and farm markets, but that
congestion on unpriced roads is a poor measure of capacity need. The author's claim that rich-country cities
already have too much road space is an interpretation, not a measured cross-city result.[^tollcases]

Citing City Observatory, the book describes Louisville's six-lane John F. Kennedy Memorial Bridge carrying
around **120,000 vehicles daily in 2010**, a **$1 billion** second bridge named for Abraham Lincoln adding six
lanes, and **$2 per crossing** tolls on both bridges with commuter discounts. It dates opening/toll introduction
to **2017**, without primary records to verify that chronology. It reports subsequent combined use of around
**60,000 cars per day**, without an observation date. The author suggests diversion to the nearby untolled
Second Street Bridge but admits uncertainty about where traffic went. These are crossing counts, not a measured
halving of regional driving or an isolated toll effect.[^tollcases]

For the M6 Toll bypassing Birmingham, opened in **2003**, the book contrasts an expected **75,000 vehicles/day**
with traffic described as never much above **50,000/day**, and **30,000 cars/day** during the 2008 financial
crisis. It attributes average traffic overestimation of around **20%** to Standard & Poor's, quoting its 2002
warning about forecast optimism, and quotes an unidentified 2014 *Transportation* article on widespread forecast
shortfalls. No study titles, samples, forecast horizons, denominators or uncertainty are supplied. These secondary
accounts do not establish a universal forecasting error or show how much travel switched modes.[^tollcases]

The text connects forecasting to private toll-road financing and reports **$21 billion** of U.S. public-private
partnership spending during **1994–2006**, without a cited accounting basis. Claims that the added Louisville
lanes were pointless or that drivers did not value faster trips are the author's judgments, not evaluated
welfare, time-saving or equity results.[^tollcases]

## Congestion costs and tolling arguments (pages 118–119)

Page 118 critiques journey speed as the principal transport measure and reports INRIX estimates of congestion
costing the U.S. **$88 billion per year**, or around **$1,500 per driver**, and the UK **£21 billion**. The UK
figure appears in the same annual-cost discussion but has no separately stated period. No estimate year, valuation
method, driver denominator or original report is given. TomTom's congestion index is described as covering
**416 cities**, with selected rankings but no index year or calculation method. These are secondary reports of
estimates, not measured productivity losses established by this excerpt.[^tolls]

The author argues that willingness to pay to avoid delay is the actual congestion cost, interpreting reluctance
to pay tolls at an unnamed Kentucky bridge and the M6 Toll as evidence against large productivity-loss estimates.
This is a revealed-preference argument, not a supplied valuation study: the pages contain no toll amounts,
traffic counts, comparison periods, income information or quantified time savings. They cannot establish that
quoted congestion costs are wrong or that toll avoidance means trips lack value.[^tolls]

Page 119 recommends charging a reasonable road-use price while keeping necessary trips without realistic
alternatives affordable, and investing revenue in public transport. It reasons that congestion persisting after
such pricing could indicate insufficient road space, whereas a substantial decline could indicate existing
capacity was enough. Its Kentucky reference supplies no effect size or evaluation. The author prioritizes moving
people over cars, describes congestion as partly a sign of a successful city, and quotes planner Jeff Speck on
failure to act on induced demand. Claims that a small toll is more likely than widening to improve movement are
policy interpretations, not a measured toll-versus-widening comparison or demonstrated equity outcome.[^tolls]

## Road-space policy arguments (page 120)

The author repeats the West Side Elevated Highway example, saying more people could enter Manhattan than before
and arguing that removing road space need not reduce mobility. This adds no counts, dates, methods or new study
to the case on p. 114. It is secondary historical reporting, not another closure evaluation.[^roadspace]

The page advocates reserving roads for trips difficult to make otherwise (heavy goods, machinery and disabled
travelers unable to use subway platforms), or for users paying the space's market value. The predicted reduction
in congestion is the author's policy argument, not an evaluated pricing or access intervention.[^roadspace]

The author says London Underground ridership changed little with fare increases and interprets expensive housing
near good public transport as willingness to pay for access. No periods, fare changes, ridership counts or housing
comparisons are supplied; these do not establish fare elasticity or a causal property premium. The page also
argues that roads are overprovided relative to their value and cost, and says most vehicles are stationary
**95% of the time**, without a population, geography, observation period or source. That time-use assertion is
not a measured parking-space requirement. These unsupported or unquantified claims remain in this Source rather
than becoming standalone Findings.[^roadspace]

## Previously reviewed SUV-sales reporting

The visible passage says SUVs made up **47% of sales**, rising to **72% when “light trucks” are added**. It then
attributes a projection of **78% by 2025** to market research firm **IHS Markit**. The following sentence describes
this as roughly four in five cars sold “in America” being SUVs or pickup trucks. These are reported sales shares
and a forecast, not fleet shares, traffic shares or verified 2025 outcomes.[^carmageddon]

The excerpt supplies no baseline sales year, vehicle-category definitions, sales counts, data source for the
47%/72% figures, forecast report title or estimation method. Only the projection is explicitly attributed to
IHS Markit in the visible text. The book's description of SUVs as “rugged four-wheel drive” does not establish
that every vehicle in the reported sales category has that drivetrain.[^carmageddon]
