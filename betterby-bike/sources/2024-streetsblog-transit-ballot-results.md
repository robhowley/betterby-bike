# At Least Local Transit Initiatives Won Big in Tuesday’s Election

## Publication and access

Kea Wilson, Streetsblog USA, November 11, 2024. Read the supplied PDF's full substantive article
(pp. 1–4), including its correction note about San Francisco. This is election reporting drawing on
APTA's Center for Transportation Excellence, an interview with its executive director Jessica Grennan,
and [PeopleForBikes' ballot coverage](https://www.peopleforbikes.org/2024-voteforbikes), not a research study.
Underlying election returns and advocacy organizations' measure inventories were not independently reviewed.

## Reporting

Wilson reports $24.9 billion approved for transit improvements across 18 measures in the November election,
and 46 of 53 initiatives passing over the year, nearly 87%. These have different time scopes. Grennan
credits bipartisan campaigning and access to schools, doctors and jobs; her claim that support crosses
party lines is an interpretation, not a voter-level analysis.[^wilson2024]

Reported wins include Wheat Ridge's $75 million for sidewalks, crossings, bike lanes and drainage;
Albuquerque's $62.3 million for street safety, bike lanes and trails; Nashville's estimated $2.2 billion
for buses, sidewalks, bike lanes and signals; and Seattle's $1.55 billion multimodal levy. These are
mixed-purpose packages, not bicycle-only appropriations. Columbus's sales-tax initiative is expected
to bring in $6.2 billion by 2050, illustrating that amounts also span different funding periods.[^wilson2024]

The article also reports defeats in San Diego, Cobb and Gwinnett counties, Charleston County and
Kootenai County. Charleston's rejected package included both transit/green space and an expressway
extension, so its defeat cannot simply be read as opposition to sustainable travel. The updated article
says San Francisco approved a $750 million Muni funding measure but another measure nullified it;
that approval is not treated here as realized funding.[^wilson2024]

## Coverage and limitations

No existing bundle account of these ballot results was found. This adds funding-authorization evidence,
not another measure of transit ridership or an evaluation of infrastructure benefits. The article does
not provide a complete measure inventory, selection rules, vote shares or a reconciliation of the annual
total with its corrected San Francisco account. Listed dollar amounts mix projected revenue, bonds and
levies across different durations and modes. The Maricopa bullet's $3.1 million and the article's broader
funding classifications have not been independently checked; they are not used as standalone estimates.
Nothing here demonstrates spending, project completion, safer streets or improved equitable access.
