# Evaluation of New Zealand’s bicycle helmet law

Colin F. Clarke, *New Zealand Medical Journal* 125(1349), 10 February 2012, pp. 1–10.
[Original publication](http://journal.nzma.org.nz/journal/125-1349/5046/) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2012-02-evaluation-nz-bike-helmet.pdf).

## Review basis

Clarke reviews publicly available surveys, official road-fatality data and prior analyses to assess New Zealand’s
all-age bicycle-helmet law, which took effect on 1 January 1994. This is a secondary evaluation, not a new data
collection or controlled comparison of helmet use or legislation. It compares cycling with walking and other road users,
and also discusses health, enforcement, accident compensation, environmental effects and civil liberties.[^clarke2012]

## Reported measurements and calculations

- Survey data compiled in Table 1 show average cycling time falling from 11.4 to 5.6 hours per person per year between
  1989–90 and 2006–09, a 51% decline. The source also quotes larger declines for children and attributes the overall
  change to the law; the activity trend and that causal interpretation are kept separate in the
  [activity Finding](/findings/networks/nz-cycling-activity-decline-1989-2009.md).
- Clarke’s Table 3 calculation combines annual cyclist and pedestrian death counts with average activity hours. Its
  cyclist-to-pedestrian fatality ratio rises from 1.24 in 1989–90 to 2.44 in 2006–09, but the displayed 2006–09 cyclist
  counts sum to 39 while the prose and Table 3 use 41. The [fatality Finding](/findings/safety/nz-cyclist-pedestrian-fatality-ratio-1989-2009.md)
  preserves this discrepancy.
- Table 4 reproduces Tin Tin et al.’s death/inpatient injury rates, including the 30.74 cycling cases per million
  cycling hours in 2003–07 and the 20% increase from 1988–91. Serious cycling injury rates (AIS ≥3) instead fell 39%,
  from 10.27 to 6.24 per million hours, and annual overall cases fell from 941 to 682. Table 5 derives
  cyclist-to-pedestrian rate ratios from these same data: 5.97 to 12.91 overall and 4.9 to 6.57 for serious injuries.
  These are repeated or derived results from the existing national study, not independent confirmation.
- Clarke separately estimates a 32% increase in injuries per million cycling hours using 2009 road-crash data
  (approximately 27 to 35.7). Its case coverage is not established here as equivalent to death/inpatient records, and
  the printed baseline is “1998/90.” The underlying crash report was not independently reviewed; this is not a second
  estimate of the same outcome with a verified period.[^clarke2012]

## Interpretation and limits

Clarke concludes that the law reduced cycling and worsened cyclist safety relative to pedestrians. He also estimates
about 53 premature deaths per year by treating the lost cycling time as a law effect and applying a health-benefit
calculation. The [53-death Finding](/findings/safety/nz-helmet-law-premature-death-estimate.md) records this as an
assumption-based counterfactual, not an observed or causally identified mortality effect.[^clarke2012]

The review reports 9,618 helmet tickets in 2010 versus 5,550 in 2000 and a $55 fine, citing news coverage. These are
historical enforcement figures, not a measured cycling-deterrence effect. Clarke argues that compulsory helmets can
unfairly affect compensation, increase pollution by discouraging cycling and restrict individual choice; he recommends
road-safety policies without compulsory helmets. No compensation-case analysis, law-attributable emissions estimate
or controlled health analysis tests those claims.[^clarke2012]

The review supplies no control jurisdiction, matched pre/post design, helmet-use comparison, uncertainty estimates for
its new calculations or adjustment for confounding travel and road-safety changes. Population is used to calculate
per-person activity, but this does not isolate the law’s effect. Risk compensation and riding instability are proposed
mechanisms, not tested explanations. The head-injury discussion extrapolates from assumed age shares and reduced
cycling, and cites Robinson’s assessment rather than presenting a new controlled head-injury analysis. The underlying
Ministry, road-crash, Collins, Robinson and other cited materials were not separately reviewed for this ingestion.
All ten PDF pages, including Tables 1–5 and references, were read; Tables 1–5 were also checked visually.[^clarke2012]
