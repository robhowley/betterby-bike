# All The Ways That Car Domination Harms Communities

## Reporting basis

Kea Wilson's March 5, 2024 Streetsblog article reports on a UK/Germany team's review of nearly
500 studies worldwide and includes comments from study author Patrick Miner. The PDF links the
[underlying review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0966692324000267), which was
not separately read. All substantive article text (PDF pp. 1–8) was read, along with the mortality
infographic and harm-category diagram on pp. 2–3. This is secondary research reporting plus an
author interview, not a new empirical evaluation.[^article]

## What it reports

The review is reported to attribute at least 1.67 million deaths annually worldwide to crashes,
car-related air pollution and lead exposure, or one in 34 deaths, and 60–80 million deaths since
the automobile's advent. The article describes those totals as incomplete. Noise, sedentary travel,
heat, disrupted sleep and mental-health harms are discussed but not comprehensively included in
the global mortality headline.[^article]

Four categories organize the account: violence (including crashes, intentional attacks and policing),
ill health, social injustice, and environmental damage. It describes disproportionate burdens on
marginalized people and barriers to essential destinations for nondrivers. It also acknowledges that
some disabled people rely on cars, while arguing that car dependency poorly serves those unable
to drive or afford accessible vehicles. Environmental pathways include extraction, disposal,
sprawl, tire particles, runoff and habitat loss, rather than only tailpipe emissions.[^article]

Miner questions monetary cost-benefit accounting when harms cannot be priced, acknowledges omitted
harms such as surveillance, and recommends more space and safety for walking, cycling, wheeling and
public transport. These are interpretations and proposals, not quantified benefits of replacing
car-dependent transport.[^article]

## Limits and connection to existing coverage

The article does not provide the review's search methods, source-specific reference years,
uncertainty calculations or reconciliation of overlapping mortality pathways. Its infographic
rounds pollution deaths to 370,000 and total deaths to 1.7 million, while prose gives 246,000
pollution deaths plus 120,000 lead-related deaths and a 1.67-million total. These are reported
rounded estimates, not independently checked totals. Neither cyclist-specific risk nor the
preventable share under a particular street redesign is estimated.[^article]

The existing [JRC review](traffic-related-non-exhaust-particulate-emissions-literature-review.md)
provides separate non-exhaust emissions-method context, not independent confirmation of this death total.
