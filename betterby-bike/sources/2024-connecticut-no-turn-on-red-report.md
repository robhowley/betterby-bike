# Connecticut No Turn on Red (NTOR) Report

CTDOT issued this report in **January 2024**, pursuant to Public Act 23-116, to assess a blanket prohibition
of right turns on red at Connecticut signalized intersections. The [original publication](https://portal.ct.gov/dot/-/media/dot/programs/vision-zero/assets/ntor-report.pdf)
is preserved in the [supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/research/ntor-report-pdf.pdf).[^ctdot2024]

## Evidence and results

The report combines Connecticut Crash Data Repository records, UConn crash-report interpretation,
a narrative literature/policy review, municipal consultations, Synchro modeling and two implementation
case accounts. It is not a controlled before/after evaluation of a statewide ban.

- For September 2020–August 2023, 72 pedestrian crashes involved right turns at signals, of which five
  were serious or fatal. These include all signal phases. Only one serious-injury/fatal case was confirmed
  on red, a serious injury where an existing restriction was violated.
- For January 2015–August 2023, Table 2 lists 14 serious/fatal pedestrian right-turn crashes at signals:
  five on green, four on red and five on an unknown phase. Two of the four red-phase cases had existing
  prohibitions. These are crash counts without turn or pedestrian exposure, not policy-effect estimates.
- Synchro scenarios at 18 intersections in five locations predict heterogeneous delay, fuel use and queue
  increases under NTOR. Sites were selected for available recent Major Traffic Generator analyses, not as
  a representative statewide sample. At Cheshire's I-691 westbound ramps, modeled delay rises from
  21.1 to 51.6 seconds; at Clinton's I-95 northbound on-ramp/N High Street, from 18 to 18.2 seconds.
- The literature review describes potential pedestrian/cyclist benefits, especially at high-volume sites,
  while noting scarce severe crashes and uncertain right-turn-on-green displacement. Its claim of crash
  reductions of 50% or more is secondary synthesis, not a result of the Connecticut analysis.
- Municipalities report positive experiences with dynamic signs, but provide no controlled effect estimates.
  Rocky Hill's 2009 restriction was removed in 2011 after queues prompted opposition; Glastonbury installed
  a pedestrian-phase dynamic sign in 2023 after modeling full-time restrictions. Neither account estimates
  a comparative injury effect.[^ctdot2024]

## Interpretation and limitations

CTDOT recommends against a blanket ban, favoring site-specific restrictions, stronger vulnerable-road-user
criteria, dynamic signs, education and consideration of camera enforcement. That is its policy judgment,
not proof that blanket bans cannot improve safety. Predicted operational costs do not measure secondary
crashes, pedestrian comfort, cycling uptake or net social benefits. A small recorded share of severe crashes
is not low risk per crossing, and restrictions already concentrated at higher-risk sites confound comparisons.

About one third of the longer-period right-turn pedestrian crashes have unknown signal phase. The report's
p. 8 statement that 5% of 227 such crashes were serious/fatal does not agree with Table 2's 14 cases
(about 6.2%); retain the table count without resolving the discrepancy. Simulations lack uncertainty ranges
and detailed reproducible calibration/timing assumptions. Cited studies and primary crash records were not
independently reviewed. Full extracted text, including tables and appendix, and the cover image were read;
figures 1–2 were not visually analyzed. Historical policies are not verified current law.[^ctdot2024]
