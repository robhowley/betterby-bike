# ANALYSIS: When Speed Cameras Are Racist

## Publication and coverage

Kea Wilson, Streetsblog USA, January 18, 2022.
[Original article](https://usa.streetsblog.org/2022/01/18/analysis-when-speed-cameras-are-racist).
All substantive PDF text was read (pp. 1–7). This is analysis and interview reporting, not a primary evaluation.
It discusses a [ProPublica investigation](https://www.propublica.org/article/chicagos-race-neutral-traffic-cameras-ticket-black-and-latino-drivers-the-most)
and a University of Illinois Chicago study, linked through [Streetsblog Chicago's study coverage](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2022/01/11/uic-study-speed-cams-save-lives-but-drivers-in-poc-communities-get-more-tickets/).
Those linked materials and other underlying studies were not reviewed.

## Reporting and evidence

Wilson reports that 38% of Chicago speed-camera tickets went to residents of majority-Black ZIP codes,
which accounted for 27% of the city's population, despite cameras not being disproportionately concentrated
in Black neighborhoods. She distinguishes this residence measure from the UIC study's finding that cameras
located in Black neighborhoods issued a disproportionate share of tickets. Critics note that ZIP-code
composition does not establish individual drivers' race.[^wilson]

The article also reports the UIC estimate of a 15% reduction in expected fatal and serious-injury crashes
citywide during 2015–2017, with benefits not found at every camera site. It cites an international 2010
study's 11–44% crash reductions and selected NYC school zones' speeding-violation declines of up to 89%;
these are different outcomes and settings, not additional estimates of Chicago's effect.[^wilson]

Interviews with market urbanist Scott Beyer and NACTO's Jenny O'Connell emphasize redesigning roads to
reduce speeding. Wilson connects unequal ticket burdens to wide, fast roads and structural inequities,
not simply camera placement. She reports that all highest-revenue cameras in the Chicago study were on
four-lane roads. O'Connell recommends administrative rather than police management, safety-directed
revenue, contractor pay independent of ticket receipts and using persistent violations to prompt design
changes. Ability-to-pay fines and restorative approaches are proposed remedies, not evaluated outcomes.[^wilson]

## Limits and reconciliation

The article supplies no full UIC methods, comparison construction, uncertainty intervals or mode-specific
injury estimates. The ZIP-code ticket comparison lacks a driving-exposure denominator and does not
measure individual racial ticketing rates. Revenue incentives are discussed as a governance risk, not
proof that Chicago deliberately maintained dangerous roads for income. Existing bundle enforcement and
curb-management coverage does not represent this Chicago result; no independent confirmation is implied.
The reporting supports considering safety and financial burden together, not assuming camera removal
would improve either or that road redesign's comparative effect was tested.
