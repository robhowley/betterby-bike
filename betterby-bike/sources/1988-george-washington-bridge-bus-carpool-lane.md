# George Washington Bridge bus-carpool lane

John C. Powers, New Jersey Department of Transportation, June 1988. Report FHWA/NJ-89-001,
performing-organization report 89-001-0206. The cover and documentation page date the report to 1988,
not the 1989 implied by the supplied filename. [Supplied report](../../raw-sources/research/fhwa-nj-1989-001-0206-pdf.pdf).

## Study and methods

A roughly mile-long reserved approach to the bridge opened in October 1986, operating weekdays 7–9 a.m.
for buses and cars carrying at least three people, with motorcycles also eligible. It extended existing bus
priority; at the narrowest express-road section it used the shoulder, rather than removing a general lane.
Route geometry prevented some carpools from accessing it. Observers sampled vehicle occupancy, lane use and
auto/bus counts at three toll plazas during 6:30–10 a.m.; first-year reporting covers November 1986–October
1987, with February and August missed because of weather. Earlier observations support seasonal comparisons;
Palisades Interstate Parkway (PIP) traffic serves as an untreated comparison for carpool trends.[^powers1988]

## Results and interpretation

- Average 7–9 a.m. reserved-lane use included 430 carpools and 70 buses. Table 1 estimates about 5,000 people
  in those vehicles, roughly 21.5% of main/lower-plaza people. Bus occupancy is represented as up to 50 people
  per bus, not a documented passenger census. Another 50 buses used only pre-existing booth priority, not the
  reserved approach. The [utilization Finding](/findings/operations/gwb-bus-carpool-person-utilization-limits.md)
  separates those groups and preserves the bus-load assumption.[^powers1988]
- The report describes uncongested reserved-lane operation and savings up to 7 minutes in its summary, but up
  to 8 minutes in the body. Figure 11's delay envelope comes from four years before opening, not a controlled
  post-opening travel-time comparison. The earlier feasibility prediction was 1–10 minutes. Its statement that
  other traffic was not harmed is an author conclusion, not a quantified before/after effect.[^powers1988]
- Violators averaged about 150 in the 7:15–8:45 core period, about 30% of lane use, versus 330 and over 40%
  for the full two hours. The author says they did not congest the lane and recommends continued police
  presence; stable violation rates alongside declining summonses do not isolate enforcement effectiveness.
- Main/lower-plaza carpool shares rose by about half a percentage point while counts stayed roughly constant;
  PIP shares declined. Powers interprets this as a favorable lane effect rather than an April 1987 toll increase.
  Differing prior trends, changing traffic denominators and concurrent toll changes prevent a clean causal
  estimate. The summary says total commuter volume increased, whereas p. 17 describes declining auto volume.
  No claim of measured new carpool formation is extracted here.[^powers1988]

## Access and limitations

Read extracted text throughout the 34-page PDF (front matter and printed pp. 1–30), including methods,
results and conclusions. Visually read Table 1 (p. 10) and Figures 11–12 (p. 29). Other figures and Table 2
were available mainly as incomplete OCR and were not digitized or used for new numerical estimates.
The text references Table 3, absent from the supplied table list and extracted report. No underlying records,
external studies or detailed observation-day sample sizes were obtained. Bus occupancy assumptions and rounded
shares limit person-utilization precision. This is historical highway-approach
monitoring, not a bicycle-lane evaluation, network-wide congestion study or measured safety benefit.
