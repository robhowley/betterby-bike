# The Late Night Threat

Carson Benowitz-Fredericks and Bruce Lee Livingston, Alcohol Justice and California Alcohol Policy Alliance,
June 2018. [Supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/research/late-night-threat-final-pdf.pdf);
[publisher-hosted PDF](https://alcoholjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/LATE-NIGHT-THREAT-final.pdf).
The upload directory is not the publication year.

## Scope and reporting basis

This advocacy report opposes later California alcohol-service hours during the 2018 SB 905 debate. It combines
international research reviews and individual studies, agency statistics, journalism, a physician interview and
illustrative California scenarios. It reports no reproducible literature-search, inclusion or quality-assessment
procedure and is not a new crash evaluation or meta-analysis. The underlying studies were not independently read.
Its relevance here is impaired-driving context, not evaluated cycling infrastructure.[^latenight2018]

## Main evidence

- Australian studies are reported as finding increased crash injuries after extended hours, including 51.3% more
  alcohol-related road crashes in a Perth hotel comparison after midnight closing was extended to 1 a.m.
  U.S. studies concern DUI arrests or stops, not necessarily crash outcomes. A contrary England/Wales study
  reports fewer crashes relative to Scotland; the report says the reduction depended on weighting and was
  concentrated among younger drivers. These heterogeneous results are not a common effect per added hour.
- Appendix I describes mixed ride-hailing findings: a California fatality reduction, a null metropolitan-county
  comparison, and varying nonfatal alcohol-related crash results across four cities. The authors' categorical
  rejection of ride-hailing as a solution is stronger than these context-dependent comparisons establish.
- The violence and medical-service discussion reports higher assaults after some extensions and lower assaults
  after some restrictions, plus increased emergency demand. A San Francisco emergency physician describes
  intoxication, handoff and diversion challenges. These are not measured cycling effects or California ambulance
  response-time estimates from a last-call intervention.
- The authors' cross-boundary harm argument draws on Windsor/Detroit crash comparisons and Ventura County
  place-of-last-drink surveys. The California maps estimate possible affected areas, not measured policy effects.
  Figure 1 shifts the timing of 2013 California alcohol-involved fatalities under an assumed 4 a.m. last call;
  it is not observed post-policy commuting or school-travel harm.[^latenight2018]

## Recommendations and limitations

The authors recommend retaining last calls at 2 a.m. or earlier, avoiding differing local closing times, collecting
better U.S. evidence and expanding transit and enforcement. Those are policy positions, not tested packages.
The report acknowledges international cultural differences, varying state prevention/enforcement/transit provision
and difficult BAC and place-of-last-drink data collection. Effect sizes generally lack confidence intervals,
sample details and comparable exposure denominators. Historical transit and legal descriptions are not current facts.
The text contains an apparent direction error describing Greenville's earlier closing as moving from 2 a.m. to
4 a.m.; no Greenville numerical policy effect is extracted here.[^latenight2018]
