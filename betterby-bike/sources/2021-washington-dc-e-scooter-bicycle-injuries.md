# Injuries related to electric scooter and bicycle use in a Washington, DC, emergency department

Jessica B. Cicchino, Paige E. Kulie and Melissa L. McCarthy. *Traffic Injury Prevention* 22(5), 401–406 (2021),
published online May 7. [Publication](https://doi.org/10.1080/15389588.2021.1913280) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2021-washington-dc-e-scooter-bicycle-injuries.pdf).

## Study and publication overlap

Prospective interviews and medical-record review at George Washington University Hospital compared 99 injured
adult e-scooter riders (March 23–November 30, 2019) with 337 cyclists (March–November portions of April 2015–
September 2017). This is a distinct publication, but its scooter recruitment overlaps the
[existing severity paper](2021-e-scooter-rider-injury-severity-trip-characteristics.md), not independent replication.
Its cyclist data came from the earlier multisite *Not all protected bike lanes are the same* study (2020,
DOI 10.1016/j.aap.2020.105490), not a new bicycle cohort.[^paper]

English-speaking adults injured within the hospital catchment area in the preceding week were interviewed during
staffed hours (8 a.m.–10 p.m. for cyclists, 9 a.m.–10 p.m. for scooters). Exclusions included inability to consent or
communicate, multiple riders on one vehicle and, for cyclists only, racing/trick riding. Only scooter participants
received $10. Charts were coded using AIS; injury/treatment comparisons adjusted for age and gender, with logistic
odds ratios converted to reported relative risks. Rates used broader screened counts, 121 scooter riders and 483
cyclists, rather than only interviewees. Exposure combined operator data, commuting estimates and participants'
pre-injury trip lengths.[^paper]

## Results and interpretation

- Estimated presentations to this ED per million citywide miles were 20.7 for scooters versus 5.5 for bicycles,
  rate ratio 3.76 (95% CI 3.08–4.59). The ordering persisted in the reported sensitivity analyses, but neither
  absolute rate measures all DC injuries or all EDs.[^paper]
- Scooter incidents less often involved moving vehicles (13.1% versus 37.7%); surface/infrastructure incidents
  were more common. Circumstances include avoidance falls, not just collisions. These are case shares, not
  facility-specific injury rates.[^paper]
- Scooter riders more often injured the knee/lower leg/ankle/foot and less often the proximal upper extremity
  or chest/abdomen/spine. Head/neck injury and admission shares were similar, but concussion with loss of
  consciousness was more common in the scooter group (four versus two cases). Mean injury severity did not
  differ significantly. Helmet use differed sharply, but this comparison does not estimate a helmet effect.[^paper]

The authors propose surface maintenance and helmet promotion, and suggest cycling is an imperfect safety benchmark
for scooters. Neither infrastructure benefits nor declining scooter risk as riders gain experience were tested.

## Limitations and access

Different years, unequal recruitment/incentives, small scooter and subgroup samples, recalled circumstances and
exclusion of some severe injuries limit comparisons. Rates include some non-interviewed severe cases but still omit
after-hours and missed patients. Citywide mileage is mismatched to one ED's catchment; geographic differences in
mode use could bias the ratio either way. Bicycle exposure was extrapolated from commuting, while both modes used
injured participants' trip lengths. DC's 10 mph shared-scooter cap limits generalization.[^paper]
