# Study on non-exhaust emissions in road transport

EIT Urban Mobility's May 2025 report was conducted by e:misia in collaboration with EIT Urban Mobility,
Transport for London and the Greater London Authority. The [original publication](https://www.eiturbanmobility.eu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/41-EIT-Emissions-Report-5a-Digital-1.pdf)
and [local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/41-eit-emissions-report-5a-digital-1-pdf.pdf) are available.

## Scope and methods

The report combines a literature review of brake, tyre and road wear, expert consultations and a November 2024
stakeholder workshop with London scenario modelling. Chapter 6 extends London Atmospheric Emissions Inventory
2019 fleet/activity data to 2050 using UK SIBYL trends and literature-derived emission factors. Its geographical
zones include a non-GLA zone, so the model is not simply central London. The baseline already assumes substantial
electrification: 86% for light-duty and 53% for heavy-duty vehicles by 2050.[^report]

Three technology scenarios receive cost-benefit analysis: low-emitting brakes, brake filtration, and low-emitting
brakes plus tyres. Accelerated electrification, mode shift and their combination receive benefit-only analysis
because implementation costs were too uncertain. Monetization uses UK damage costs, with 3% annual social discounting
and 1% annual inflation beyond 2025.[^report]

## Results and interpretation

The car-to-public/active-transport scenario reports cumulative non-exhaust savings through 2050 of 5,548 tonnes
PM10, including 2,610 tonnes PM2.5. It assumes reduced car kilometres and increased bus kilometres rather than
estimating how a particular intervention causes mode shift. Accelerated electrification also reduces modeled
non-exhaust emissions, despite higher tyre/road wear, through regenerative braking. Exhaust and greenhouse-gas
benefits are outside this comparison.[^report]

Among the three technology scenarios, combined low-wear brakes and tyres have the highest modeled cumulative
net benefit (€235 million over 2026–2050) and benefit/cost ratio (1.71). Brake filtration saves more PM mass but
has a lower net benefit (€100 million); low-emitting brakes alone have the lowest cost per kilogram of airborne
PM saved. Thus “best” depends on the metric. Mode-shift benefit-only results cannot establish a superior net
return to these costed technologies.[^report]

The authors recommend promoting public and active transport alongside electrification, lighter vehicles and
wear-resistant components. These are recommendations supported by conditional modelling and review, not observed
cycling-infrastructure effects. They also warn that lower particle mass need not mean lower toxicity.[^report]

## Access and limitations

Focused reading covered the executive summary (pp. 5–6), emission-factor methods and Table 2 (pp. 25–26), all of
Chapter 6 (pp. 57–71, including Tables 3–5 and figure captions), and conclusions/acknowledgements (pp. 72–78).
Other review chapters, annex material, underlying studies and the linked Power BI model were not reviewed.
Quantities extracted here are explicit in the text/tables; charts were not visually digitized.

Emission factors vary with measurement methods; heavy-duty estimates require extrapolation. No uncertainty interval
for the extracted scenario total is supplied in the reviewed results. Page 62 describes the car-kilometre reduction
against 2015, whereas Table 4 says against baseline. The headline “up to five times” comparison also changes framing
between sections; the Finding retains these ambiguities rather than treating the slogan as a transferable effect.

Filename, title, e:misia, SIBYL and non-exhaust searches found no existing representation of this publication.
The existing [2014 JRC review](traffic-related-non-exhaust-particulate-emissions-literature-review.md), previously
represented by an unidentified excerpt, concerns source shares and wear measurements, not these scenarios;
overlap in underlying review studies is unresolved.
