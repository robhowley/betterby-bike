# NJ faces ‘a real problem’ of pedestrian safety. Here’s what towns are doing to improve it

Kyle Morel, NorthJersey.com, August 11, 2021, updated August 14.
[Original article](https://www.northjersey.com/in-depth/news/watchdog/2021/08/11/pedestrian-safety-nj-towns-combine-education-enforcement/5383314001/).
Appears on pp. 12–22 of the supplied PDF, separately from the repeated Gomez article.

## Issue and reporting basis

Morel interviews police in Fair Lawn, Morristown and Newton and TransOptions project manager Laura Cerutti about
pedestrian safety. The three towns were among 13 North Jersey communities participating in TransOptions' Street
Smart campaign. Its education, engineering and enforcement approach included temporary signs, social-media
outreach, pedestrian-decoy operations and educational handouts. Newton also described school crossing guards and
campaigns in 2015 and 2018.[^morel2021]

The article cites state police data compiled by Street Smart NJ: pedestrians accounted for **176 of 558 New Jersey
traffic deaths in 2019 (31.5%)**, versus a **17.3% national share**. These are shares of traffic deaths, not pedestrian
risk per trip. Local police supply other counts and estimates, rather than a common evaluation dataset.[^morel2021]

## Reported outcomes and proposals

Newton's Lt. Michael Monaco reported greater law adherence; Morristown's Sgt. Brian LaBarre described a slight
crash decline and fewer severe darting-out incidents. Neither supplied numerical before/after comparisons.
Fair Lawn Capt. Joseph Dawicki explicitly attributed its post-January-2020 crash reduction likely to less driving
and walking during COVID-19, rather than demonstrating campaign effectiveness. TransOptions' optimism is a program
provider's assessment. The article also retains criticism of police details as entrapment or revenue generation,
as well as officers' accounts of supportive residents.[^morel2021]

The report describes newly signed New Jersey passing legislation and Morristown's hoped-for flashing crosswalk
beacons. These are historical policy and installation plans, not evaluated safety benefits or verified current law.
Officers' claims about speed, responsibility and injury severity are interview opinions, not measured risk functions
or a quantitative allocation of fault.[^morel2021]

## Limits and access

All substantive text on PDF pp. 12–22 was read as part of the 34-page capture. The article's own URL was recovered
from embedded links; the repeated page footer instead names Gomez's article. The subscriber label did not block
this saved text, but embedded video was not viewed. Underlying state records, campaign evaluations and legislation
were not independently reviewed. The account lacks exposure measures, controls, comparable pre/post periods and
numerical program effects. It cannot establish that education or enforcement reduced crashes, nor that these
approaches outperform street redesign.[^morel2021]
