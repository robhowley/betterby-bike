# Protected Bicycle Lanes in NYC

New York City Department of Transportation (NYCDOT), September 2014.
[Original report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2014-09-nyc-pbl-analysis.pdf).

## Scope and methods

This 31-page agency presentation evaluates seven miles of typical one-way Manhattan avenue protected lanes: 12 projects
on six avenues installed in 2007–2011, selected for availability of at least three years of post-project safety data.
Only treated portions enter the corridor analysis. Designs differ in lane removal/narrowing, prior bicycle facilities,
split-phase signals versus mixing zones, bus lanes and broader network changes.[^report]

NYPD AIS/TAMS records supply before/after injuries and injury crashes. Eight corridors have cyclist-risk changes based
on three-year injury periods normalized by representative April–October 12-hour bicycle counts. Separate mobility
analyses use taxi records and short travel-time/vehicle-count surveys. Retail results reproduce NYCDOT's December 2013
*The Economic Benefits of Sustainable Streets*, not an independent economic evaluation.[^report]

## Main results

- Injury crashes fell 17% and total injuries 20%; cyclist injuries changed little (100 to 98), while pedestrian and
  motor-vehicle occupant injuries fell more. The chart does not clearly identify whether displayed counts are annual
  averages or period totals.
- The cyclist-risk proxy fell on seven of eight reported corridors, but rose 11.2% on Broadway (18th–23rd). A separate
  preliminary citywide indicator fell 75% in 2001–2013; it is not a protected-lane treatment effect.
- NYCDOT reports steady central-business-district and First Avenue taxi speeds, and shorter Columbus and Eighth Avenue
  travel times. These measure different routes, hours and periods, not one network-wide delay effect.
- Two-year retail-sales growth on Ninth and Columbus Avenues exceeded named comparison corridors. These comparisons
  differ from the earlier 2012 report's Ninth Avenue headline and should not be pooled.
- The report records 110 added trees and crossing-distance reductions of 17–30 feet. For Second Avenue (14th–23rd),
  it describes a 2010 buffered-lane/bus-lane layout followed by a 2013 protected-lane upgrade, with AM/PM vehicle volumes
  down 11.8%/15.3%, bus ridership up 9% and bicycle volumes up 60%. Its suggestion that users shifted modes is an
  interpretation, not tracked individual travel behavior; the slide omits measurement dates.[^report]

## Limits and relationship to earlier evidence

The report provides more site and measurement detail than the 2011 mayoral memo or 2012 case-study brochure, but no
controlled safety analysis or uncertainty estimates. Counts approximate exposure rather than measuring all trips or
kilometres; concurrent street changes and citywide trends prevent attributing results to protected lanes alone.
Short mobility samples, different seasons and limited economic methods constrain generalization. Overlapping NYC
corridors are not independent replications of earlier reports.

All 31 pages were read as extracted text; safety and retail panels (pp. 7 and 14) were checked visually. Underlying
crash/count datasets and the separate 2013 economic report were not independently reviewed.
