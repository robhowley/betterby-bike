# Smart Congestion Relief

Todd Litman, Victoria Transport Policy Institute, **6 October 2023**, 80 pages.
[Supplied report](../../raw-sources/research/2023-vtpi-smart-congestion-relief.pdf) ·
[Publisher URL](https://www.vtpi.org/cong_relief.pdf). The supplied dated version, not a potentially revised online
file, was reviewed. The cover identifies related summaries presented in 2012 and 2014 and published in 2013;
these are versions of the same research, not independent confirmations.

## Question and evidence basis

The report asks how congestion measurement changes the apparent merits of road expansion, walking/cycling/transit
improvements, pricing, compact mixed-use development and transportation demand management (TDM). It combines
transport-economic reasoning, narrative literature synthesis, historical city comparisons, selected program accounts
and hypothetical calculations. Evidence spans U.S. cities and international examples; there is no single study
population, original controlled intervention, systematic search protocol, pooled effect or formal quality appraisal.
Its evaluation-method discussion covers analysis scope, baseline speeds, time values, sampling, induced travel,
safety/emissions, accessibility, economic efficiency and equity.[^litman]

## Main synthesis

- Congestion intensity and burden are different: Table 1 ranks New York slightly worse than Houston by Travel Time
  Index, but better by annual delay per commuter after weighting auto-commuter delay by auto commute share.
  These are historical, mixed-source comparisons, not a measured cycling effect or current city rankings.
- Litman recommends measuring access to destinations and delays to people across modes, including walking/cycling
  barriers, rather than only vehicle speeds. He favors economically efficient speed baselines, sensitivity analysis
  of time values, and accounting for generated traffic and net additional travel after capacity expansion.
- His overall recommendation combines better alternatives, priority for space-efficient modes, pricing and TDM
  before considering expansion financed by peak-period users. The claimed co-benefits and ranking of strategies
  are a policy synthesis, not experimentally established returns for a typical project.[^litman]

## Cycling evidence, mixed results and trade-offs

Page 51 reports Hamilton and Wichman's 2016 Capital Bikeshare study as finding **4% or more lower congestion**
than would otherwise occur, with larger reductions in congested areas. The underlying study is identified in the
references but not reproduced: dates, sample, identification details and uncertainty are absent from this account.
The school and shopping-district trip reductions on the same page are hypothetical examples, not observed cases.

Litman's four-condition cycling discussion retains potential delay on narrow, congested roads where faster vehicles
cannot pass, and possible turning delays even where cyclists have space. His claim that most mode shifts reduce
congestion is conditional reasoning, not a measured universal bike-lane effect. Improvements may reduce congestion
without eliminating it; their benefits also include access for non-drivers, not just benefits to remaining motorists.

The broader review retains contested Denver light-rail findings (p. 53), reported approximately neutral congestion
cost effects from density alone (p. 60), and a Puget Sound model in which neither highway expansion nor transit
improvements recouped direct costs through congestion savings alone (Table 23). Litman argues for additional
benefits, but the report does not supply a complete project-specific benefit-cost calculation proving that argument.
Pricing adds user costs, privacy and implementation concerns; its distributional effects depend on alternatives,
price design and revenue use. Faster traffic and induced travel can offset safety and emissions benefits.[^litman]

## Tables and numerical limitations

Tables 10 and 14 are assumed cost scenarios, not measured outcomes; Table 13's scores are author ratings, not
statistical effect sizes. Table 14 prints a positive $3,000 TDM barrier-effect cost despite describing a benefit,
and its listed highway cost changes do not sum to the printed total. Table 23's attribution says WSDOT 2004 while
nearby text cites WSDOT 2006. These unresolved inconsistencies preclude treating the illustrative totals as verified
project returns. Tables 1 and 2 use different historical inputs; their city values should not be combined into one
series. Cited studies and models have different populations and measures, with insufficient detail here to reassess
causality or precision. The report's broad policy conclusions exceed what any one secondary example establishes.[^litman]
