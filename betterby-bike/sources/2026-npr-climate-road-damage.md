# As climate change damages streets and highways, the road ahead may be expensive

## Publication and reporting basis

Scott Neuman's NPR article is dated **July 7, 2026** in the supplied capture. All substantive
reporting on PDF pages 1–5 was read; page 6 contains footer links. The [original article capture](../../raw-sources/news/a-hotter-climate-could-make-roads-more-expensive-npr.pdf)
provides incident reporting and interviews, not a pavement experiment or cost analysis. Its own web URL
is not identified in the capture's text; no underlying research paper is named.[^neuman2026]

## What it reports

- During the preceding Fourth of July heat wave, concrete pavement on I-97 south of Baltimore warped,
  closing a lane. The article also describes pavement failure on a Chicago street and state transport
  departments' warnings, without a systematic incident count or attribution analysis.
- Strong Towns founder Charles Marohn describes moisture-weakened road foundations and prolonged heat
  as contributors to failure. Maryland DOT spokesperson Charlie Gischlar relates prolonged heat and
  traffic to the I-97 incident. University of Texas engineer Amit Bhasin distinguishes concrete
  expansion and buckling from asphalt rutting, especially under slow-moving traffic.
- Interviewees describe concrete as generally more expensive initially but longer-lived and less
  maintenance-intensive, and asphalt as cheaper initially and easier to repair but requiring more
  frequent work. These are qualitative expert comparisons, not priced lifecycle estimates.
- Proposed adaptations include more durable asphalt mixes, changes to concrete reinforcement,
  joint spacing and panel size, and incorporating changing extremes into design assumptions.
  Bhasin stresses the cost/reliability tradeoff and need for climate information; Arizona State
  engineer Mikhail Chester argues for changing historical infrastructure-design approaches.[^neuman2026]

## Limits and relationship to existing coverage

No repair bill, national cost forecast, evaluated adaptation, crash outcome or cycling-specific benefit is
reported. Bhasin's example of a design working 99% of the time with disruption 1% of the time illustrates
a decision tradeoff, not measured reliability. The article does not establish climate attribution for
individual failures or a universal material ranking across climates and traffic loads.[^neuman2026]

Existing [NJDOT guidance coverage](/sources/2015-njdot-roadway-design-manual.md) concerns traffic calming
and bicycle accommodation, while [urban-heat coverage](/sources/gothamist-nyc-urban-heat-island-9-7-degrees.md)
reports a separate city-temperature comparison. Neither duplicates this pavement-maintenance account.
This source adds engineering context to [street-redesign evaluation](/topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md#design-guidance-is-not-an-outcome-evaluation),
not independent support for existing safety or emissions Findings. No new Finding was extracted because
the article supplies qualitative guidance rather than a distinct evaluated result.
