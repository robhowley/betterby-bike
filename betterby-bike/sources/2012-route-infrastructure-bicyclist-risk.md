# Route Infrastructure and the Risk of Injuries to Bicyclists: A Case-Crossover Study

Kay Teschke and colleagues. _American Journal of Public Health_ 2012;102(12):2336–2343, December 2012.
[Publication](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2012.300762) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2012-12-route-infrastructure-bicyclist-risk.pdf).

## Study and scope

The study recruited 690 adults aged 19 or older treated within 24 hours of a cycling injury at five Toronto and
Vancouver emergency departments, May 18, 2008–November 30, 2009 (276 Toronto, 414 Vancouver). Each injury site was
compared with one randomly selected point along the same trip. Control selection was proportional to distance traveled,
matching personal characteristics and conditions stable within a trip, rather than using a separate uninjured group.
Blinded observers classified 14 route types and measured site features. Conditional logistic regression estimated odds
ratios; the final model included route type, grade, streetcar/train tracks and construction.[^paper]

Hospital recruitment captured collisions and falls, not just motor-vehicle crashes: 33.5% involved a direct motor-vehicle
collision. Estimated participation was 66.5% after allowing for unknown eligibility, versus 93.1% among those known to
be eligible. Most participants were regular cyclists (88.1%).[^paper]

## Results

Relative to major streets with parked cars and no bicycle infrastructure, cycle tracks had the lowest adjusted injury
odds (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.02–0.54), based on only two injury and ten control sites. Local streets without infrastructure
and designated local bike routes also had significantly lower odds. Major streets without parking or bike infrastructure
had OR 0.63 (0.41–0.96). Painted lanes, shared lanes, traffic-calmed local bike routes, sidewalks and other off-street
paths had lower point estimates, but their adjusted confidence intervals included one. Rail tracks, downhill grades and
construction were independently associated with higher odds in the final model.[^paper]

Figure 1 compares these injury estimates with preferences from a separate 2006 Metro Vancouver survey. The authors
argue that several lower-risk routes are also attractive to cyclists and recommend safer infrastructure. Neither
ridership growth nor the effect of installing facilities was measured here. The discussion cites the
[Montreal study](/sources/2011-montreal-cycle-track-injury-risk.md); that citation is not new Montreal evidence.[^paper]

## Interpretation and limitations

Within-trip matching controls stable rider and trip factors, not every difference between sites. These are observational
route associations, not before-and-after installation effects or absolute injury rates. Sparse observations limit some
route estimates; the study did not distinguish one-way from two-way cycle tracks. Downtown hospital locations likely
weighted the sample toward urban cores. Children, fatal cases, people unable to recall their trip or communicate, and
mountain biking, racing and trick riding were excluded. Findings do not establish effects on fatal or severe head
injuries.[^paper]

Infrastructure was observed after the injury, creating possible misclassification, especially for temporary conditions
or sites where layouts changed within a block. The authors expected blinded measurement errors to tend toward the null.
Full text, Tables 1–4 and Figure 1 were reviewed as extracted text; linked supplementary materials and the underlying
preference survey were not independently reviewed.[^paper]
