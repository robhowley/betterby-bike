# U.S. micromobility injury burden, 2019–2022

Kathryn G. Burford, Nicole G. Itzkowitz, Andrew G. Rundle, Charles DiMaggio and Stephen J. Mooney.
*American Journal of Public Health* 114(12), December 2024, pp. 1365–1374.
[Original publication](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2024.307820).

## Study and methods

Cross-sectional analysis of 2019–2022 National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) ED records.
The authors describe a stratified probability sample of 96 hospitals in the United States and territories;
weights produce national estimates. Product codes and narratives identify devices; 160 unweighted records
involving multiple micromobility modes were excluded. Census populations supply rates per 100,000 residents,
not per rider, trip or mile. Survey-weighted, nested, year-adjusted estimates and log-binomial models compare
injury dispositions and diagnoses. Motor-vehicle/pedestrian involvement and helmet use are extracted from
clinical narratives; alcohol and drug fields record positive indications only.[^burford2024]

## Results and interpretation

- Estimated four-year ED burden was 1,933,296 injuries (95% CI 1,717,064–2,149,529), mostly bicycles
  (1,623,143), followed by powered scooters (164,712), hoverboards (88,478 in prose) and e-bikes (56,963).
- Main-text trends report population-based injury-rate increases of 293.0% for e-bikes and 88.0% for
  powered scooters from 2019 to 2022, versus a 26.2% hoverboard decline. Bicycle rates declined after 2020;
  powered scooter growth began to plateau by 2022. These do not establish changes in risk per ride.
- E-bike ED cases had more internal injuries than bicycle or hoverboard cases. Hospitalization was
  significantly more prevalent for e-bikes only relative to hoverboards, not all other devices. Hoverboards
  had more fractures and concussions, despite lower admission prevalence. Pediatric patients represented
  76.2% of hoverboard cases, versus 14.5% of e-bike and 16.1% of powered scooter cases.
- Recorded motor-vehicle involvement was highest for e-bikes (35.4%); positive alcohol indications were
  highest for powered scooters (9.0%). Helmet use was identifiable in only 20.3% of narratives, with large
  mode-specific missingness. These are injured-patient descriptions, not causal risk factors or population
  helmet-use estimates.[^burford2024]

The authors recommend improved surveillance, protective-equipment strategies and safer active-transportation
infrastructure. No infrastructure, helmet-law or alcohol-prevention intervention was evaluated. Missing exposure,
clinical-note measurement error, incomplete race/ethnicity and sparse death/burn cells limit inference;
the paper warns that small estimates may be unstable or unreliable.[^burford2024]

## Access and overlap

Read all ten supplied pages as extracted text, including methods, limitations, references, Tables 1–2 and
figure labels; visually checked Table 2 and Figure 2 on p. 1371. The separate online Appendix Tables A–C,
Appendix Figure A and Zenodo data/code were not reviewed. A request to a candidate publisher supplement URL
returned HTTP 403; its exact file location was not verified. Trend percentages above are attributed to the
main-text results, not an independent review of the appendix's annual estimates or coding list.

Title, DOI, author and underlying-study searches found no duplicate publication in the bundle. This analysis
shares 2019–2022 NEISS surveillance with [CPSC's 2023 report](/sources/2023-cpsc-micromobility-deaths-injuries-hazard-patterns.md),
which it cites; it adds bicycle and diagnostic comparisons, not an independent injury sample. Device coding,
periods and CPSC follow-up procedures differ, so totals should not be added or silently reconciled. The cited
DiMaggio et al. 2020 analysis is an earlier NEISS study, not separately reviewed here. Table headers give
88,477 hoverboard injuries versus 88,478 in prose, a one-case discrepancy retained rather than repaired.
