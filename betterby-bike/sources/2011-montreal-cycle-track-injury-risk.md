# Risk of injury for bicycling on cycle tracks versus in the street

Anne C Lusk, Peter G Furth, Patrick Morency, Luis F Miranda-Moreno, Walter C Willett and Jack T Dennerlein. *Injury Prevention* 2011;17:131–135. Accepted 1 December 2010; published online 9 February 2011. The local filename reflects 2010, not the publication year. [Publication](https://doi.org/10.1136/ip.2010.028696) · [Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2010-12-risk-of-injury-on-cycle-tracks-vs-street.pdf).

## Study and scope

The study compared six Montreal cycle tracks, all two-way on one side of a road, with nearby alternative routes without bicycle facilities. Tracks were separated by medians, parking lanes or delineator posts. Emergency medical response (EMR) records supplied injuries during the April–November cycling season from 1999 to July 2008; police records supplied bicycle–motor vehicle crashes for 2002–06. Simultaneous two-hour commuting-period bicycle counts in 2009 estimated relative track/street exposure. Historical track counts and assumptions about season length and distance ridden supplied absolute bicycle-kilometre estimates. Whole route segments, including intersections, were studied.[^paper]

## Results

- Pooled injury relative risk was 0.72 (95% CI 0.60–0.85) on tracks versus reference streets. Three routes had significantly lower risk; three did not differ significantly, including Rachel with a point estimate above one. No route showed a statistically significant increase.
- Simultaneous counts recorded about 2.5 times as many cyclists on tracks as on reference streets. This comparison does not measure ridership growth caused by construction.
- Track rates were 8.5 EMR-recorded injuries and 10.5 police-recorded bicycle–motor vehicle crashes per million estimated bicycle-kilometres. These are different event measures, not additive counts.
- A separate reanalysis of published Palo Alto sidewalk-bikeway data showed how adding assumed non-intersection roadway crashes changed the apparent risk comparison. It was not another Montreal observation or a new independent study.[^paper]

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors argued that cycle-track construction should not be discouraged. Their evidence supports lower pooled injury risk in these observed routes, not a universal causal reduction from installing protected lanes. Traffic danger, assessed indirectly with motor-vehicle-occupant injuries, differed considerably between pairs; the two tracks with the lowest absolute injury rates had much lower traffic-danger proxies than their reference streets. Six two-way tracks in one city cannot establish which design features explain safety differences or estimate effects for one-way tracks.[^paper]

There were no injury-severity data. Historical injuries were compared with short 2009 exposure counts, so changes in relative route use remain uncertain. Cross-street injuries were removed using proportions from police crash records because EMR records did not identify the cyclist's approach street. Absolute rates additionally depend on interpolated counts, 200 effective cycling days per year and expert estimates of the fraction of each segment ridden. Published crash rates from other settings are not matched controls.[^paper]
