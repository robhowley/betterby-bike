# Severity of e-scooter rider injuries associated with trip characteristics

Jessica B. Cicchino, Paige E. Kulie and Melissa L. McCarthy. *Journal of Safety Research* 76 (2021), 256–261.
[Publication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsr.2020.12.016) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2021-e-scooter-rider-injury-severity-trip-characteristics.pdf).

## Study and methods

Researchers prospectively recruited 105 injured adult riders and six non-riders injured by e-scooters at George
Washington University Hospital, March–November 2019. Screening ran 9 a.m.–10 p.m.; patients presented within a
week of injury. Interviews captured trip circumstances and mapped locations, checked against Street View where
available. Intersection incidents were assigned to the facility used before entering the intersection. Charts supplied
injuries and treatment; two riders leaving before physician assessment were excluded from those analyses.[^paper]

The outcome was at least one Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score ≥2 injury (moderate or worse), not any injury
or injury per trip. Stepwise logistic regression retained variables at p <0.15; odds ratios were converted to relative
risks because the outcome was common. The final model included age, sex, road versus other location, motor-vehicle
collision and riding frequency. Missing values were excluded. Most shared scooters were subject to a 10 mph cap;
actual incident speeds were not measured.[^paper]

## Results

Among 103 clinically assessed riders, 39 (38%) had AIS ≥2 injuries, two had AIS ≥3 injuries and nine were admitted
or transferred. Road cases more often had AIS ≥2 injuries than other-location cases (14/24, 58%, versus 25/78, 32%):
adjusted RR 1.96 (95% CI 1.23–2.36). At least weekly use versus less frequent use had adjusted RR 1.86
(1.11–2.32), although its unadjusted comparison was inconclusive (45% versus 33%, p =0.22).[^paper]

Falls attributed to surface features, infrastructure or other reasons accounted for 71/105 rider incidents; ten
involved a moving motor vehicle. Only one of those ten had AIS ≥2 injury, versus 38/93 in other circumstances;
the adjusted association was inconclusive (RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.02–1.10, p =0.07), not evidence that vehicle collisions
are protective. Age and female sex also had inconclusive adjusted associations (both p =0.06). Speed-cap status did
not significantly distinguish severity in the unadjusted comparison (29/84 versus 8/16, p =0.24).[^paper]

Six non-rider cases included four encounters with parked scooters and two with moving scooters. Three had AIS =2
extremity injuries; two were admitted and underwent orthopedic procedures. These cases document harm beyond riders,
not a population rate or evidence that sidewalk riding is harmless.[^paper]

## Interpretation and limitations

There were no facility-specific riding denominators: the 60 sidewalk, 24 road and eight bike-lane cases cannot rank
facilities by injury probability. The authors propose higher speeds or behavioral differences as explanations for road
and frequent-rider severity; neither mechanism was established. Their call for separated riding space is a policy
interpretation, not an evaluated intervention, and they explicitly say crash/fall risk must accompany severity when
choosing facilities.[^paper]

One hospital, a small sample, exclusion of three people too injured to communicate and incomplete overnight
recruitment limit generalization, especially to fatal or severe trauma. Lacerations, contusions and abrasions were
classified as external injuries regardless of body location, complicating comparisons of the reported 7% head-injury
share with other studies. The 37% first-ride share does not establish a higher first-trip injury rate without exposure.[^paper]

All six PDF pages, including methods, discussion, limitations and Tables 1–5, were read as extracted text; Tables 4–5
were also checked visually. Minor reporting inconsistencies remain: the recruitment paragraph calls 105 the total
enrolled despite separately describing 105 riders plus six non-riders, and Table 2 lists 17 uncapped scooters while
the methods enumerate 16. These do not supply alternative denominators for the quoted Table 3–5 results. Cited studies
and underlying patient data were not independently reviewed. Funding was from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety.
