# Why US Cities Are Investing in Safer, More-Connected Cycling Infrastructure

Olivia Fiol, Yonah Freemark and Yipeng Su, Urban Institute, Urban Wire, February 2, 2022.
[Original article](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/why-us-cities-are-investing-safer-more-connected-cycling-infrastructure)
·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/news/2022-urban-inst-cities-invest-in-bikes.pdf).

## Issue and reporting basis

The authors summarize their examination of cycling infrastructure choices in **13 U.S. cities**, using municipal
investment data over the preceding five years and interviews with advocates and city staff. The work was part of an
examination of Final Mile, a philanthropically funded program supporting complete, safe bicycle networks. The article
links [Making the Case for Improved Bicycling Infrastructure](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/making-case-improved-bicycling-infrastructure);
that underlying report was not separately reviewed. This is a research-team explainer with secondary examples and
policy recommendations, not a full methods report or a controlled evaluation of Final Mile.[^urban2022]

## Main reporting

- The average share of new cycling infrastructure classified as protected rose from **57% in 2016 to 78% in 2020**.
  Cities differed: Austin, New Orleans, Portland and Seattle devoted about 90% or more of new investment, excluding
  bicycle boulevards, to secure projects; most new lanes in Atlanta, Kansas City, Los Angeles and Nashville were
  unprotected. Denver's example compares 2016 with 2021, a different endpoint from the aggregate.[^urban2022]
- Pittsburgh staff reported the share of lanes linked to one another rising from **40% to 80%** after recent
  investments. A Denver advocate likewise described a shift from disconnected segments toward a network.[^urban2022]
- **Fewer than one-fifth of Los Angeles libraries** were within a quarter mile of protected cycling infrastructure.
  This describes proximity, not residents' usable routes, library visits or equitable access.[^urban2022]

## Repeated evidence and interpretations

The **8.8% versus 7% Salt Lake City sales comparison** links the
[Streetsblog account already represented here](2015-streetsblog-slc.md), not independent evidence. The article dates
parking conversion to 2013 and abbreviates the project as nine blocks of parking converted to a lane; the existing
[Broadway Finding](/findings/business/salt-lake-broadway-retail-sales.md) retains partial parking removal and the
unresolved sales-period difference across accounts. This article does not resolve that chronology.[^urban2022]

Its **28% lower Montreal injury-rate** example repeats the
[existing six-track study](/findings/safety/montreal-cycle-track-relative-injury-risk.md). Its NYC **40–50% “injury
rates”** wording links [Wolfson's 2011 memo](/findings/safety/nyc-wolfson-protected-lane-crash-claims.md), which actually
claims injury-crash reductions without exposure denominators. Neither is new confirmation. The article also cites
NYC/DC commuting growth in 2009–2014 and Bay Area comfort research; these contextual accounts do not measure effects
of the 13 cities' recent investments.[^urban2022]

The authors expect better networks to increase biking and recommend prioritizing communities of color and low-income
families, putting existing residents at the center of decisions amid gentrification concerns, and offering classes
and bike-purchasing assistance. These are recommendations, not measured equity, displacement or program effects.
Their broad claims that parking replacement benefits health, safety and businesses exceed what the construction
and proximity measures alone establish.[^urban2022]

## Limits and access

All nine PDF pages were read as text, including substantive pp. 2–7; embedded links were recovered. Graphics were not
interpreted, so no chart-only values are extracted. The article does not detail city selection, aggregate weighting,
a consistent protected-facility classification, connectivity calculation, library denominator or uncertainty.
It includes buffered lanes in its introductory protected-infrastructure discussion, so its category should not be
assumed to mean only physically separated lanes. The linked report and contextual research were not newly reviewed.
