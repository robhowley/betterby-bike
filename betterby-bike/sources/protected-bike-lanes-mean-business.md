# Protected Bike Lanes Mean Business

Michael Andersen (PeopleForBikes Green Lane Project) and Mary Lauran Hall (Alliance for Biking & Walking).
[Original report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2023-protected-bike-lanes-mean-business.pdf).
No publication date is printed. The supplied filename says 2023, but PDF creation/modification metadata is January 14,
2014 and references extend through 2013; the filename is not evidence of a 2023 study.

## Scope and reporting basis

The report pairs interviews with 15 businesspeople in Austin, San Francisco, Portland, Chicago and Washington, DC
with selected external research. It argues that protected lanes support redevelopment, recruitment, employee health
and retail activity. This is an advocacy narrative, not a systematic review or a controlled five-city evaluation.
Interview selection, response rates and a common outcome-measurement method are not supplied.[^report]

## Reported results

- **Retail:** a Portland comparison reports lower spending per bicycle trip but higher monthly spending than for
  car arrivals. A Melbourne study is cited for higher retail revenue per unit of bicycle-parking space and time.
  Valencia Street merchant responses are favorable overall, but 4% said changes hurt sales. These are different
  measures, not three estimates of a protected-lane sales effect.
- **Original business accounts:** Chicago's 694 Wine & Spirits owner describes greater visibility and a sidewalk
  café permit following parking/sign removal with a lane project. Green Zebra's founder describes a business model
  needing about 30% of customers to arrive by foot, bicycle or transit, not an observed customer share. Employers
  describe bicycle access as a recruitment and location consideration, without measured hiring or retention effects.
- **Real estate:** interviewees anticipate higher values or more attractive streets, rather than report measured
  protected-lane premiums. Separate callouts cite Krizek (2006) for $510 higher median home value per quarter-mile
  nearer an off-street Minneapolis–St. Paul trail, and Lindsey et al. (2004) for homes within half a mile of Indiana's
  Monon Trail selling for 11% more than similar homes farther away. The report does not reproduce controls,
  uncertainty or valuation periods; trail associations cannot establish on-street protected-lane effects.
- **Health:** workers describe feeling healthier and more alert. A QBP workplace-incentive case reports a 4.4%
  healthcare-cost decrease versus a 24.6% national increase, without the comparison period in this report. A Portland
  trail-network estimate attributes $155 million in annual avoided obesity-related costs to physical activity.
  Neither is a measured healthcare saving caused by installing protected lanes.
- **Use and perceptions:** selected corridor bicycle counts reportedly rose 54–266%, mixing buffered and protected
  designs; surveys report up to 99% feeling safer and up to 30% reporting increased riding. These maxima do not
  establish average effects, net new citywide cycling or measured injury reductions.[^report]

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors propose plausible mechanisms: slower passers-by notice businesses, frequent nearby shoppers return,
compact parking serves more customers, and comfortable commutes appeal to employees. Interviewees' explanations
are not causal estimates. The report itself says lanes cannot solve every problem and require careful planning.
It does not establish typical business effects, profits, displacement, net employment or distributional outcomes.

Health/productivity callouts generalize physical-activity evidence to cycling and protected lanes without supplying
study populations or comparable effect definitions. The 32%/55%/52% headline figures should not be read as measured
lane-induced reductions in absence/costs or productivity gains. Similarly, anticipated real-estate benefits and modeled
health savings are not realized financial returns.[^report]

The [Ninth Avenue sales claim](/findings/business/nyc-ninth-avenue-retail-sales.md), up to 49% versus 3% elsewhere in
Manhattan, explicitly repeats NYCDOT's 2012 report. It adds no independent confirmation. Sources cited for the other
quantitative claims were not independently reviewed during this ingestion.
