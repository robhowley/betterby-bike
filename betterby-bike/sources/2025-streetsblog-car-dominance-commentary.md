# Car dominance and the visibility of street space

## Publication and basis

Nicole Gelinas, Streetsblog New York City, June 16, 2025, in the outlet's “Car Harms” series.
The [captured essay](../../raw-sources/news/car-harms-monday-so-dominant-that-you-don-t-even-notice-you-re-being-inconvenienced-for-th.pdf)
is seven pages; substantive text and author biography occupy pp. 1–5. It is commentary based on
urban observations, historical interpretation and linked reporting, not a new transport evaluation
or attitude survey. The author is identified as a contributing editor to the Manhattan Institute's
City Journal and author of *Movement: New York’s Long War to Take Back its Streets from the Car*.

## Argument and supporting examples

Gelinas argues that apparently empty bus and bicycle lanes can reflect movement rather than wasted
space: users have already passed while adjacent cars queue. In discussing Fifth and Madison avenues,
she cites up to 130 buses an hour and bus passengers outnumbering passenger-car and for-hire occupants
two to one. The capture links this passage to [earlier Fifth Avenue reporting](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2024/10/22/adamss-fifth-avenue-plan-will-make-bus-riders-suffer-experts),
which was not reviewed. These figures are not a new count or a universal lane-capacity comparison.[^essay]

The essay contrasts objections to outdoor dining as private, sometimes unused street space with the
normalization of parked private cars. It says peak outdoor dining occupied far less than 1% of the
city's three million parking spaces, while motor-vehicle lanes occupy 60% of the roadbed on each of
Fifth and Madison avenues. These are the author's reported scale comparisons, without measurement
dates or a reproducible counting method in the essay.[^essay]

Gelinas interprets the displacement of streetcars, introduction of traffic signals, legalization of
overnight residential street parking and postwar sidewalk narrowing as adaptations to cars' spatial
demands, rather than proof of their urban efficiency. She advocates slow, narrow motor-vehicle lanes,
yielding to pedestrians and cyclists, and charging for vehicle storage. She acknowledges that some
people need cars but argues that those needs should fit a street system prioritizing walking, transit,
bicycles and micromobility. This is a policy position, not an evaluated intervention.[^essay]

## Limits and relationship to existing evidence

The essay supplies no systematic lane observations, survey sample, before-and-after comparison or
uncertainty estimates. A lane's appearance alone cannot establish passenger throughput, spare capacity,
congestion effects or public support. The historical account and linked statistics were not independently
verified; no new Finding is extracted from these illustrations and recommendations.

The [windshield-bias coverage](2024-streetsblog-windshield-bias.md) reports survey contrasts in acceptance
of driving harms. This essay offers a related interpretation of street-space disputes, not independent
confirmation of those survey results. The [street-redesign synthesis](../topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md#reserved-lane-passenger-estimates-are-not-added-capacity)
separately explains why passenger estimates do not establish capacity added by a redesign; the essay's
visual argument should not replace that distinction.
