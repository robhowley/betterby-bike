# Massive Hazards

Road to Zero Coalition, an initiative of the National Safety Council, October 2024.
[Supplied report](../../raw-sources/research/2024-road-to-zero-massive-hazards-light-trucks.pdf);
[publisher's coalition site](https://www.nsc.org/roadtozero).

## Evidence and scope

A task force of researchers, advocates, industry representatives and safety professionals assembled academic studies,
agency statistics, technical reports and journalism about U.S. light trucks, with some international evidence.
This is a narrative research and policy synthesis, not a new crash dataset or a systematic review with documented
search, inclusion or quality-assessment procedures. One cited study is itself a meta-analysis. The report generally
includes SUVs, pickups and vans as light trucks, but excludes vehicles over 10,000 lb; classifications differ across
regulatory and crash-data sources. Recommendations do not represent every contributor or coalition member.[^rtz2024]

## Main results and qualifications

- Reported pedestrian and cyclist studies generally associate light trucks with worse injury outcomes after a
  collision than cars. Desapriya et al.'s 2010 meta-analysis of 12 studies is reported as finding 50% higher pedestrian
  fatality risk. These are secondary estimates across differing populations, not one pooled result from this report.
- Monfort and Mueller's bicyclist study is described as finding more severe injuries, head injuries and ground
  impacts with SUVs than sedans, including cyclists pushed beneath rather than onto vehicles. The prose labels it
  2019, but reference 65 identifies the [2023 paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/15389588.2023.2219795).
  The report provides no cyclist-specific effect size, sample details or uncertainty for this comparison.
- Higher and blunter front ends are associated with pedestrian fatality in reported crash studies. The IIHS figure
  also shows similar risk for low/blunt and medium/sloped profiles relative to low/sloped vehicles, not uniform
  excess risk for every change in shape. Tyndall's proposed height-cap benefits are modeled, not observed policy effects.
- Turning versus straight-ahead comparisons among fatal pedestrian crashes support concern about visibility, but
  do not measure deaths per turn or prove blind zones caused those crashes. Direct-vision reaction-time evidence
  comes from heavy trucks, and the report explicitly calls for corresponding light-truck research.
- Car-driver fatality disadvantages narrowed markedly for SUVs but persisted for pickups in the reported
  1989–2016 compatibility research. The report also acknowledges an IIHS fixed-barrier analysis finding no evidence
  of greater battery-electric vehicle aggressivity; real vehicle-pair compatibility remains uncertain.
- Weight, speed and acceleration are discussed as interacting hazards. Claims that older crash-study results would
  be worse today are interpretations, not updated measurements. The report repeats
  [The Economist's 2024 analysis](/sources/2024-economist-big-cars-killing.md) on p. 12, not independent confirmation.
  White, Li, and Anderson/Auffhammer also overlap the earlier article's historical references.[^rtz2024]

## Recommendations, not evaluated packages

The coalition advocates safer front-end geometry and direct vision, broader crash testing, speed assistance,
revised tax/fuel-economy incentives, weight-based fees and smaller public fleets. It cites earlier evaluations of
turn calming and other street treatments, and Hoboken's attributed safety account, to motivate interim street
changes. It does not evaluate its combined recommendations or establish that fees, height caps or these treatments
eliminate light-truck risk. Legislative and regulatory descriptions reflect October 2024, not verified current law.[^rtz2024]

## Access and limitations

Read extracted text across all 54 PDF pages (cover plus printed pp. 1–53), including research summaries, footnotes,
outstanding research questions and recommendations. Visually checked the supporting IIHS profile graphic on
printed p. 22 and compatibility trend chart on p. 31. Other illustrations were not visually reviewed or digitized.
There are no separate methods/limitations sections or tabulated study estimates; methodological detail is uneven.
Cited primary papers, datasets, external technical reports and the publisher's live site were not independently read.

The report generally omits confidence intervals, model specifications and detailed sample selection. Its pedestrian,
cyclist, vehicle-occupant, simulator and barrier-test evidence cannot be treated as interchangeable. Fatality severity
conditional on a crash is not injury incidence per trip. Several references have inconsistent dates, so use the
identified publication and report locator rather than silently resolving those discrepancies.[^rtz2024]
