# Evaluation of Solid Green Bicycle Lanes to Increase Compliance and Bicycle Safety

City of New York Department of Transportation (NYCDOT), submitted to the Federal Highway Administration, Office of Traffic Operations, 8 February 2011. [Original report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2011-nyc-green-paint.pdf).

## Study and scope

NYCDOT compared motorist behavior on solid-green curbside bicycle lanes and standard white-striped, unpainted lanes in Manhattan and Brooklyn. Observations ran from 4–6 pm in September 2010. Neither treatment had physical protection. Two of the three unpainted controls were not curbside, introducing a layout difference alongside paint color. Methods describe four green sites, but the location and results tables list five green sites and three controls.[^report]

Surveyors counted motorists and cyclists, recorded standing in the lane, driving in the lane and driving on its boundary line, and assessed whether the lane was clear when cyclists were present. This was a cross-sectional comparison, not a controlled before-and-after test. Land use was described as similar, but traffic patterns and volumes could not be fully accounted for.[^report]

## Results

- Reported aggregate motorist shares were 7% versus 16% driving on the boundary line and 4% versus 7% driving inside green versus unpainted lanes. Standing or parking was comparable (Chart 2 reports 1% standing in each group).
- Findings and Chart 2 report lanes clear when cyclists were present in 96% versus 93% of cases. Site results varied: Brooklyn's Clinton Street comparison favored green paint, while Bleecker Street had less line encroachment but slightly more in-lane driving and a lower clear-lane percentage at the green site.
- The report could not separate any ridership effect from citywide cycling growth and network expansion. It did not measure a treatment-specific collision or injury effect.[^report]

## Interpretation and limitations

NYCDOT interpreted the observations as suggesting improved visibility and less encroachment. Cyclists' anecdotal belief that paint deters parking was not supported by a measured difference in standing/parking. The conclusion that green lanes contributed to cycling growth and safety goes beyond the comparison: accompanying citywide trends do not isolate paint's contribution.[^report]

The short observation window, unmatched lane layouts and absence of reported confidence intervals or significance tests limit inference. Internal discrepancies also limit precise aggregation: the “All Green” counts omit the fifth site's counts, although it appears in Chart 2; clear/obstructed percentages do not consistently sum to 100%; and the conclusion's 93–95% clear range differs from the Findings' 93% and 96%. These are reported values, not corrected estimates. The [measurement-limit Finding](/findings/design/nyc-green-paint-measurement-limits.md) documents the discrepancies.[^report]
