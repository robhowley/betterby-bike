# Streetsblog: post-pandemic driving and congestion (2024)

## Reporting and evidence

George Kevin Jordan reports StreetLight Data's vehicle-miles-traveled (VMT) and congestion trends,
interviewing its content director Emily Adler and Open Plans co-executive director Sara Lind.
The article discloses Open Plans as a Streetsblog sister organization. Substantive reporting occupies
PDF pp. 1–5; the remaining pages contain newsletter, related-story and site material. The p. 3
metro scatterplot was also read visually. A second, downtown chart is captioned but absent in the capture.
The linked [2024 VMT report](https://www.scribd.com/document/770099809/2024-VMT-Report-r6-1)
was not independently reviewed.[^article]

## Main reporting

StreetLight reportedly estimated a **12% increase in nationwide per-capita VMT between May 2019
and May 2024**, with total VMT increasing in **88 of the 100 most populous metro areas**.
Reported metro increases include El Paso (42%), Boise City (58%), McAllen-Edinburg-Mission (68%),
Chicago (5%) and Philadelphia (6%). These metro figures are not explicitly labeled per capita
in that passage. A separate 20% per-capita increase since January 2023 is reported without a
clear endpoint or seasonal adjustment, so it should not be combined with the May-to-May comparison.[^article]

The article says only San Francisco and Albuquerque among the 100 metros had lower congestion
in spring 2024 than spring 2019. The p. 3 top-25-metro plot associates larger VMT increases with
larger congestion increases, showing San Francisco with roughly 14% lower VMT and New York
with roughly 14% higher VMT. Its congestion axis is labeled percent change, whereas the prose
mixes percentages and percentage points. Los Angeles appears at approximately zero congestion
change despite the caption saying traffic worsened in 24 of 25 metros. Precise congestion changes
are therefore not extracted as comparable effect sizes.[^article]

Adler describes persistent congestion and the value of trend benchmarks. Lind attributes gridlock
to increased driving rather than bike lanes or outdoor dining and criticizes New York's paused
congestion-pricing plan. These are interpretations and advocacy, not evaluated policy effects.

## Limits and connections

The headline says remote work "led to" increased driving, but the article supplies no comparison
of remote and office workers, remote-work exposure measure or causal design. It also lacks
sampling, population-denominator, uncertainty and congestion-metric details. The figures describe
coincident trends, not evidence that remote work caused extra mileage or that any street treatment
caused or prevented congestion. Climate and fatality references are context, not evaluated outcomes
of the reported mileage changes.
