# Commuting habits, Slate coverage

David Zipper, Slate, December 3, 2024; correction December 16, 2024. The PDF metadata title is
“Driving to work: Why it’s psychologically difficult to switch to public transit or biking.”
The captured publication is linked above; its article URL is not supplied in the PDF text or link annotations.

## Reporting basis

Zipper combines secondary research coverage with interviews with transport researcher Ben Clark and
behavioral scientist Todd Rogers. The article links a
[2016 UK commuting study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0965856416303871),
a [2021 behavioral-science paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/237946152100700204),
and [Kristal and Whillans's 2019 workplace-experiment account](https://hbr.org/2019/12/why-its-so-hard-to-change-peoples-commuting-behavior).
These underlying publications were not independently reviewed.[^zipper2024]

## Main reporting

The UK study reportedly found only one in ten car commuters changing mode annually. Status quo,
present and sunk-cost biases are offered as explanations for persistence, not measured causal shares.
Workplace experiments offering tools including carpool matching and personalized transit plans reportedly
failed to change commuting behavior. This null evidence qualifies the article's encouragement of
behavioral support.[^zipper2024]

A [Danish study on intervention timing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289986565_The_importance_of_timing_for_breaking_commuters'_car_driving_habits)
reportedly found that a free month of transit increased use only among people who had recently changed
home or job. A [2016 UCLA student report](https://www.lewis.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/09/2015-2016_Brown_RightPlaceRightTime.pdf)
reportedly found less driving and more transit use when car-free information arrived before students
reached campus. Employer relocations and divorce are also discussed as opportunities to reconsider travel.
The article gives no effect sizes, uncertainty intervals or follow-up durations for these examples.
Welcome packages of transit and bikeshare passes are Zipper's proposal, not an evaluated combined program.

The corrected London Underground example concerns a **two-day** strike in 2014, not a month:
some passengers reportedly retained newly discovered routes afterward. This is route choice within
transit, not demonstrated car-to-bike switching. Brussels reportedly installed 40 kilometres of bike lanes
during lockdown before a cycling surge; the article supplies no causal analysis or quantified cycling change.
Zipper and Clark argue that attractive alternatives and behavioral support are complementary, not that
nudges replace safe infrastructure or frequent transit.[^zipper2024]

## Limits and reconciliation

Read all five PDF pages, including the correction; substantive reporting occupies pp. 1–4 and p. 5 is
site material. This is a journalistic selection of studies, not a systematic review. Samples, assignment
methods, comparisons and persistence are insufficiently described to rank interventions or estimate local
mode shift. No independent primary-study review is implied. The bundle's
[ACS commute-share evidence](/findings/networks/us-2019-2022-active-commute-share.md) describes population
trends, not these intervention mechanisms; the sources should not be counted as confirming one effect.
