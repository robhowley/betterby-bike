# Cyclists and Pedestrians Can End Up Spending More Each Month Than Drivers

Emily Badger, CityLab, December 5, 2012.
[Original article][original] ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/news/2012-citylab-pedestrians-bikes-spending.pdf).

[original]: https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2012-12-05/cyclists-and-pedestrians-can-end-up-spending-more-each-month-than-drivers

## Reporting and evidence

Badger examines merchants' concerns about losing parking as Portland extended bicycle infrastructure beyond its urban
core. She interviews Portland State University's Kelly Clifton and reports her team's Oregon Transportation Research
and Education Consortium project, linking a
[November 2012 draft report](http://kellyjclifton.com/Research/EconImpactsofBicycling/OTRECReport-ConsBehavTravelChoices_Nov2012.pdf).
That underlying report was not reviewed for this ingestion.[^citylab2012]

The article reports **89 businesses, including 11 grocery stores**, with **1,884 customers** surveyed at convenience
stores, bars and restaurants and **19,653 supermarket customers**. Non-grocery exit surveys ran June to early October
2011, using 5–10-minute tablet questionnaires about demographics, travel, that visit's spending and estimated monthly
visit frequency. Restaurants were high-turnover sit-down pizza and Mexican establishments. A local grocery chain's
customers were surveyed during one week in April 2012.[^citylab2012]

Bicycle customers reportedly spent more per month than drivers at bars, restaurants and convenience stores, despite
often spending less per visit. Drivers remained the largest customer group in each category and spent more at
supermarkets. The charts show that walking customers did not exceed drivers' monthly spending in every category:
walkers spent more at bars, but less at restaurants, convenience stores and supermarkets.[^citylab2012]

## Interviews, interpretation and limits

Clifton describes frequent visits as relevant to customer loyalty and neighborhood businesses. Badger offers walkable
shopping and impulse stops as explanations; these are interpretations, not tested mechanisms. Clifton's suggestion
that lower car costs leave a Portland “green dividend” for local purchases is explicitly unverified by this study.
Her call to accommodate “interested but concerned” riders and criticism of unequal scrutiny of transport investments
are policy opinions, not measured infrastructure outcomes.[^citylab2012]

Monthly spending is estimated using reported visits rather than tracked monthly purchases. The article acknowledges
that customer characteristics could explain differences but provides no adjusted effects, confidence intervals or
response rates. It is not a before/after test of parking removal or bicycle lanes. Grocery and non-grocery samples
also differ in survey period and collection setting. The headline should not be generalized to all non-driving
customers or all retail categories.

All six PDF pages were read as text, and all three charts on pp. 4–5 were inspected visually. Chart bars support the
directional comparisons above; exact values were not inferred from their unlabeled bar lengths. The 2024 print date
and related-story inserts are not the article's publication date.
