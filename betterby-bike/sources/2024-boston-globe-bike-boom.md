# More Bostonians are biking as bike lanes boom, but barriers remain

Taylor Dolven, The Boston Globe, August 5, 2024, reproduced on Boston.com. Read the supplied 12-page PDF as text;
substantive reporting is on pp. 2–10. [Published article](https://www.boston.com/news/the-boston-globe/2024/08/05/more-bostonians-are-biking-as-bike-lanes-boom-but-barriers-remain/).

## Reporting and results

The Globe analyzed Boston's fall bicycle counts at three locations with newly added lanes. The city counts bicycles
and cars at dozens of locations over a few days four times a year; the article uses the fall observations, not annual
average daily traffic. Results differ: the Mass Ave. bridge count rose each year after 2021 to 6,160 bikes per day in
fall 2023 (51% above 2021); South Huntington rose from 228 in 2022 to 301 in 2023 but remained below 2017's 369;
Cambridge Street rose 12% to 414 in fall 2022, then dipped slightly in 2023.[^dolven2024]

Interviews add experiences, not a representative adoption survey. Srikanth Sundaresan describes lanes making child
transport and commuting more comfortable, but says he would not take his child through a construction section shared
with large trucks. Rich Esposto feels safer on Centre Street and finds cycling convenient for day care, while still
making those trips predominantly by car. Holly Eisenberg attributes her shift toward cycling to slow, infrequent
transit and describes dooring and traffic concerns. All three were already experienced cyclists. Advocate Vivian Ortiz
and streets chief Jascha Franklin-Hodge emphasize protection and network gaps; their expectations are not measured
safety or mode-shift effects.[^dolven2024]

The article separately reports nearly 3.8 million Bluebikes trips in 2022, largely attributing the spike to the Orange
Line shutdown, and cites a 2023 StreetLight Data report estimating Boston-metro daily bicycle trips per 1,000 people
at 26 in 2019 and 35 in 2022. These are different geographies and measures from the corridor counts, not additional
estimates of lane effects. Boston's goal that half of residents live within a three-minute walk of a safe, connected
route by the end of 2025 was a target, not an achieved outcome.[^dolven2024]

## Limits

This is original reporting using municipal counts, selected interviews and secondary trend estimates, not a controlled
lane evaluation. Exact count days, weather comparability, uncertainty and untreated-route comparisons are not supplied.
Local counts cannot distinguish new trips from route diversion or establish citywide growth caused by lanes. The
municipal data and StreetLight report were not independently reviewed; the latter's methods and direct report URL
were not recovered from the text. Photos were not used as evidence. Safety, emissions and congestion benefits appear
as motivations or interpretations, not measured outcomes here.
