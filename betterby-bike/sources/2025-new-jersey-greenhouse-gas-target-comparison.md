# New Jersey greenhouse-gas target comparison

James Shope, Robert Kopp, Marjorie Kaplan, Jeanne Herb and Ashlyn Spector prepared this April 2025 report
for Rutgers University's New Jersey Climate Change Resource Center.
[Original publication](https://doi.org/10.7282/00000491) and
[local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/comparison-of-new-jersey-s-greenhouse-gas-reduction-targets-with-united-states-nationally-.pdf).

## Scope and methods

The report compares New Jersey's 2030 interim target and 2050 statutory limit with the U.S. goals cited
at publication and four AR6 scenario groups limiting global warming to 1.5°C or 2°C. It converts targets
to a 2015 baseline and harmonizes modeled emissions to historical 2015 values using an offset that
linearly disappears by 2050. Medians and 10th–90th percentile model ranges are reported. There is no
New Jersey-specific model: the state is assumed to make the same percentage reductions as the U.S.
The report treats New Jersey targets as net emissions although the legal texts do not expressly do so.[^report]

## Results and interpretation

New Jersey's 50% and 80% reductions below 2006 become approximately **40% by 2030 and 76% by 2050**
below 2015. The report's U.S. comparison is 44–47% and 100%, respectively. Its modeled U.S. 2050 median
for 1.5°C with no/limited overshoot is 90% (82–101% model range). The authors interpret New Jersey's
2050 target as closer to a 2°C pathway and recommend a statewide 2050 net-zero target. This is an
approximate target comparison and attributed recommendation, not a state warming forecast or proof
that adopting a target delivers reductions.[^report]

For progress context, the report estimates that reaching 60.9 million metric tons CO2-equivalent in
2030 from 97.6 in 2021 requires annual absolute cuts of about 4.1 million metric tons. It also estimates
that the earlier 80x50 pathway would leave approximately 72 million metric tons net emissions in 2030,
above the later interim target. These are inventory-based arithmetic and a secondary pathway estimate,
not a feasibility assessment or a measured effect of transportation policy.[^report]

## Access and limitations

All 43 pages were read as extracted text, including technical methods, tables, caveats and figure captions.
Figures were not visually reviewed or digitized; underlying models, inventories and legal documents were
not independently accessed. No cycling, mode-shift or street-redesign benefit is estimated.

Comparability of land-use/forestry sequestration accounting is assumed, not established. Harmonization
is simpler than AR6's; international climate finance and fair-share contributions are outside scope.
All policy descriptions are historical to April 2025, not verified current rules or commitments.

Internal inconsistencies remain: the 2014–2019 annual reduction is 0.9 million metric tons in the
executive summary but 1.0 in the main text/appendix; U.S. 2015 net emissions are 5,916.0 in the methods
but 5,988.9 in the goals section, whose summary also changes 44–47% to 45–47%. Introductory references
to the 2035 NDC coexist with repeated citations to the 2021 NDC. Extracted comparisons retain the
report's main tables, not a silently corrected recalculation.[^report]

Title, author, DOI and target searches found no existing account of this publication in the bundle.
Existing emissions evidence concerns transport accounting or particulate scenarios, not this target comparison.
