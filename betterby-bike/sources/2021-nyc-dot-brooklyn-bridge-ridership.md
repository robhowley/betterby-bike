# Brooklyn Bridge Bike Lane Ridership Skyrockets

NYC DOT, November 8, 2021. Read the complete two-page press-release capture. It links
[East River bridge counts](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/east-river-bridge-counts-24hrs-1980-2021.pdf)
and [Cycling in the City 2021](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/cycling-in-the-city-2021.pdf);
those underlying documents were not independently reviewed.

## Reporting and evidence

DOT reports that a protected two-way bike lane opened September 14, 2021, replacing a car lane
on the Brooklyn Bridge and leaving the formerly shared promenade to pedestrians. Work included
barriers, protective fencing, a connecting Manhattan path, signals and traffic changes. Automated
24-hour counters recorded average daily bicycle volumes of 4,206 in October 2021 versus 2,239
in October 2020, an 88% increase. September rose 56%, but its 2021 average spans both sides of
the opening date. Both months also exceeded their 2019 counts.[^dot2021bridge]

The release says counts on the other East River bridges remained largely consistent with historical
trends, without showing their figures. It places the project within citywide cycling growth, citing
over 530,000 daily cycling trips in 2019 and 61% Midtown-core cycling growth from 2015 to 2020.
Those figures cover different places, periods and measures, not outcomes of the bridge conversion.
DOT says it is monitoring bridge traffic and describes cleaning and winter-maintenance preparations.[^dot2021bridge]

Officials and advocacy organizations interpret the increase as evidence of latent demand and safer,
more convenient cycling. Citi Bike's Laura Fox also reports doubled bridge rides since before the
pandemic, attributing momentum to the cycling boom and e-bikes. These statements are attributed
interpretations and operator context, not independent evaluations of this lane.[^dot2021bridge]

## Limits and reconciliation

This is an implementing agency's descriptive announcement, not a controlled evaluation. It gives
no counter validation, missing-day treatment, uncertainty, weather adjustment or numerical comparison
series. Counts are crossings, not unique cyclists or proven new trips; route redistribution and
pandemic-era travel changes are not separated. It supplies no measured crash, pedestrian-safety,
congestion, emissions or car-trip outcome despite supportive quotations.

The bundle's earlier NYC street-lane and Cambridge neighborhood results concern different projects.
This adds a distinct bridge count series, not a replication of their estimated effects.
