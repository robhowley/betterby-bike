# Creating Accessible, Equitable, Safe, and Complete Networks for Young Pedestrians

## Publication and access

D. Lambert, N. Pullen-Seufert and O. Peters (2024), Pedestrian and Bicycle Information Center (PBIC), an information
brief supported by FHWA. The [supplied publication](../../raw-sources/research/2024-creating-accessible-equitable-safe-complete-networks-young-pedestrians.pdf)
was reviewed through all extracted text of its 16 pages, including substantive pp. 3–13, examples and references
(pp. 14–15). Visually checked p. 8's crash account and the curb-extension, crossing-island and daylighting diagrams
on pp. 10 and 12. There are no quantitative supporting tables or separate methods/limitations sections. Other
illustrations were not visually reviewed. Cited studies, municipal reports and guidance were not independently read;
all their results below are secondary reporting. No external URL for this brief was verified.

## Purpose and evidence basis

The brief explains how U.S. transportation planners can account for children's and youths' physical and cognitive
abilities and different destinations and travel times. It is a narrative research-and-practice synthesis, not a
systematic review, original cohort or controlled project evaluation. It gives no search strategy, inclusion criteria,
quality grading or pooled effect. It recommends meaningful youth participation, youth-specific travel/injury data,
and countermeasures chosen for developmental needs rather than treating children as smaller adults.[^brief]

Children's shorter stature limits sight lines and driver visibility. The authors describe developing attention,
gap judgment, reaction timing and bicycle motor/cognitive coordination, while noting that development varies
between children and that older youths may still lack experience with unfamiliar, complex roads. They argue that
education alone is insufficient and that street design must accommodate mistakes by both drivers and children.
Travel extends beyond school commutes to parks, friends, activities and older youths' jobs, often outside adult
peak hours. Age restrictions can also limit driving and shared-mobility options.[^brief]

## Reported evidence and planning examples

- **Injury vulnerability:** a cited North Carolina study using five years of pedestrian crash data and ED records
  reported traumatic brain injuries in 11.4% of children aged 0–14 versus 8% of adults aged 25–64. A separate study
  of 5,000 hospitalized Los Angeles County pedestrians reported head/neck injuries most common in children beyond
  superficial injuries, versus musculoskeletal injuries in adults. These describe injury cases, not per-trip risk;
  the brief does not supply sample counts by age, adjustment or uncertainty for these comparisons (p. 4, refs. 12–13).
- **Youth-specific measurement:** worker commuting data for ages 16 and older omit much youth travel. PBIC proposes
  arrival/dismissal counts, school surveys and participatory mapping. It describes Michigan's grades 3–12 survey and
  Boston surveys/focus groups prioritizing low-income youth, youth of color and underserved neighborhoods. These
  identify experiences and barriers, not quantified equitable access improvements (pp. 7–8).
- **Crash-network coverage:** the Philadelphia demonstration examined five years of pedestrian crashes involving
  people under 18. PBIC reports 61% of youth pedestrian injury crashes outside the city's high-injury network,
  with youth injury crashes less represented on it than adults'. Three unspecified crash types comprised 89% of
  youth pedestrian crashes. No exact observation years, case counts or model estimates are supplied (p. 8, ref. 30).
- **Participation:** Austin held over 50 engagement events; Honolulu involved high-school students in a 2019
  quick-build's locations and mural design; Milwaukee's 2020 workshops addressed eight schools. These are
  implementation accounts, not measured injury, mode-share or equity effects (pp. 6–7).
- **Speed enforcement:** PBIC reports a Montgomery County residential/school-zone evaluation's 10% reduction in
  mean vehicle speeds and 62% reduction in the likelihood of exceeding the limit by more than 10 mph at camera
  sites. For NYC it reports an average 73% speeding reduction at camera locations as of December 2021, and greater
  decreases in deaths/serious injuries on camera corridors than similar roads outside school speed zones. The
  brief supplies no study-level methods, baseline periods, uncertainty or numerical NYC casualty effect. These
  are different measures, not youth-specific injury reductions or evidence for bicycle lanes (p. 11, refs. 38–39).

## Recommendations and limits

The authors organize design around spatial separation, time separation, physical speed reduction, speed enforcement
and driver awareness. Examples include accessible sidewalks wide enough for accompanied children, shorter/staged
crossings, paths, turn restrictions, road diets, raised crossings, daylighting and lighting. Path separation still
requires attention to at-grade road crossings. The brief explicitly retains responsible-adult accompaniment for
young children despite engineering improvements. It recommends accessibility compliance but does not audit designs
or establish measured benefits for disabled children.[^brief]

Police crash underreporting, particularly for children and less severe injuries, and missing locations constrain
network targeting; PBIC recommends separate youth analysis and supplementary ED/trauma records. Its brief summaries
cannot establish exposure-adjusted risk, causal benefits or national prevalence. Calling speed enforcement equitable
is a recommendation, not an evaluated distributional outcome. The document is nonbinding except for cited statutes
and regulations, and is not verified current legal guidance. The School Streets sentence cites reference 42, while
the reference list ends at 41 (a School Streets brief); the numbering mismatch remains unresolved.
