# Impact of 4-to-3 lane conversions on emergency response

Nicole Corcoran, Cara J. Hamann, Michelle L. Reyes, Stephanie Jansson and Joseph E. Cavanaugh.
*Transportation Research Interdisciplinary Perspectives* 26 (2024), 101158, available online July 4, 2024.
[Original publication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trip.2024.101158) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2024-07-road-diets-ems.pdf).

## Question and methods

The study examines whether converting four through lanes to one lane each way plus a center turn lane affects
emergency response in Iowa. It combines two distinct samples:[^corcoran2024]

- **Responder survey:** 167 eligible responses from 239 submissions, covering 14 conversions in ten cities.
  Eligible sites were at least 0.5 miles long and converted between 2015 and spring 2020. Responders recalled
  pre-conversion expectations and described post-conversion experiences; two reviewers independently coded open-text
  themes, with a third resolving disagreements. Cedar Rapids and Des Moines supplied about 58% of respondents.
- **Incident analysis:** Cedar Rapids Fire Department records from 2014–2020, covering four conversions after other
  cities lacked adequate data. Table 6 reports 1,202 pre-conversion and 2,665 post-conversion trips (3,867 total).
  Origins and routes were inferred as shortest paths from assigned stations. Conversion-year trips and trips not
  traveling along converted segments were excluded. A Poisson model of alarm-to-arrival minutes, offset by log route
  distance, estimated response rates in minutes/km, adjusting for site, season, lanes, speed limit, signals, peak
  traffic and share of the trip on converted segments. Lower rates mean faster responses, not more calls served.

## Main results

The adjusted **before/after rate ratio was 1.024 (Table 7 Wald 95% CI 0.992–1.056)**, with after as reference.
The authors found insufficient evidence of a conversion-related change, not proof of equivalence or universal absence
of delay. Three site estimates changed little; 42nd Street's apparent improvement relied on only 14 pre-conversion
trips. Survey responses were mixed: among 163 answering the response-time question, 51% perceived unchanged times,
33% slower and 16% faster. Negative accounts described driver confusion or blocked center lanes; positive accounts
highlighted passing space. Education on yielding is an author recommendation, not an evaluated intervention.[^corcoran2024]

## Limitations and reporting discrepancies

One city's before/after analysis without non-converted controls cannot isolate causal effects or generalize to all
road diets or protected bike lanes. Actual routes and dispatch-to-arrival driving times were unavailable;
alarm-to-arrival includes pre-departure time. Converted segments averaged about one-fifth of inferred trip distance.
The survey was voluntary and retrospective, with uneven community coverage and few volunteer responders.[^corcoran2024]

The publication contains unresolved inconsistencies: the abstract says 170 survey responses versus 167 in the results;
section 3.2.1 says 3,872 trips versus Table 6's 3,867; and p. 8 gives a confidence-limit lower bound of 0.922 versus
Table 7's 0.992. Survey city-table headings and percentages do not clearly identify question-specific denominators,
and the Des Moines narrative omits the additional slightly-negative category when quoting 41% negative. The Findings
retain explicit table attribution rather than silently reconciling these differences.

All 12 PDF pages, including appendices and references, were read as extracted text; pp. 7, 8 and 11 were also checked
visually. Confidential incident data were unavailable for independent reanalysis. Cited studies were not independently
reviewed for this ingestion. The FHWA New Jersey cases cited in the introduction are already represented in the bundle,
not new independent observations.
