# The 2025 State Energy Efficiency Scorecard

## Publication and access

Mark Kresowik, Sagarika Subramanian, Mike Specian, Forest Bradley-Wright, Daivie Ghosh, Paul Mooney,
Stephanie Sosa-Kalter, Archibald Fraser, Brian Fadie and Joanna Mauer. ACEEE, March 2025, report U2502.
[Original publication](https://www.aceee.org/research-report/u2502) ·
[Supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/research/aceee-report.pdf).

Focused extracted-text reading covered the front matter/executive summary, Chapter 1's data collection,
limitations and scoring changes (pp. 5–7), Chapter 3's introduction and scores (pp. 48–52), vehicle-incentive
Table 20 through its e-bike entries (pp. 54–57), and VMT/land-use/transit discussion and tables (pp. 64–75,
plus part of p. 76). Other sector chapters, the rest of the transportation chapter, external program rules
and underlying datasets were not reviewed. This is a partial transport-focused ingestion, not a review of
all energy-efficiency findings. No chart digitization or visual PDF review was performed.

## What the report measures

The 16th Scorecard benchmarks the 50 states and District of Columbia across six energy-policy areas.
Information primarily reflects the preceding two years, but individual indicators use older data.
ACEEE solicited state agency review: 37 utility commissions and 37 state energy offices responded.
For nonrespondents it used public information and prior responses. Most nonutility scores credit policy
adoption rather than rigorously evaluated savings; the authors explicitly acknowledge simplification,
inconsistent reporting and missing outcome data.[^aceee2025]

Transportation combines clean-vehicle policies and deployment, equitable-transport policies, VMT changes
and targets, land-use planning, transit funding and freight planning. Table 19 gives California 25 points,
New York 23.5 and New Jersey 19. These are rubric points, not cycling, safety or energy-saving rates.
ACEEE recommends compact development, connected walkable neighborhoods and alternatives to driving;
this chapter does not test those recommendations as interventions.[^aceee2025]

## Useful transport evidence

- Table 20 reports a Colorado $450 point-of-sale e-bike tax credit introduced in April 2024 and Rhode
  Island rebates with a higher income-qualified cap. These are descriptions of purchase assistance, not
  evaluations of take-up, additional purchases, car replacement or access gains.
- Table 23 averages three overlapping long-period changes in motor-vehicle miles per capita. New Jersey's
  reported average is −2.7%, even though its latest comparison, 2014–2023, is +1.5%. Colorado and
  Massachusetts receive target-policy credit despite positive average VMT changes. Policy adoption and
  travel outcomes therefore remain distinct even within this benchmark.[^aceee2025]

## Qualifications

These are historical policy descriptions, not verified current eligibility or legal advice. No causal
cycling-project, emissions or distributional effects are established. VMT per capita is not total driving,
cycling mode share or household access; the overlapping comparisons have pandemic-era endpoints and are
not three independent studies.

Internal scoring descriptions also conflict: the executive summary says 17 jurisdictions have VMT/GHG
goals, whereas Chapter 3 says 19 states earned target points. The outline allocates one freight point,
while later prose and Table 19 allow two. Page 65 calls the −5% threshold two points but later says nine
states earned the “full point.” These discrepancies are unresolved; no reconciled national policy count
or normalized transportation score is extracted.
