# No, Bike Lanes Don't Hurt Retail Business

Eric Jaffe, CityLab, September 10, 2013.
[Original article](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2013-09-10/no-bike-lanes-don-t-hurt-retail-business) ·
[Local PDF](../../raw-sources/news/2013-citylab-bikes-biz.pdf).

## Reporting and evidence

Jaffe challenges fears that replacing parking or traffic lanes with bicycle lanes harms retailers, using Kyle Rowe's
Seattle case studies. He quotes an opponent's comment to the city about 65th Street parking and congestion, then
reports Rowe's comparisons of city taxable retail sales before and after installation against a similar unchanged
retail corridor and the surrounding neighborhood. This is secondary reporting, not a new sales analysis.[^citylab2013]

The article links [Rowe's underlying case study](https://docs.google.com/file/d/0B0xHj6OM3QVWMUxScjZuMndxVkk/edit)
and quotes his [Seattle Transit Blog account](http://seattletransitblog.com/2013/08/06/seattle-case-study-economic-impacts-of-bike-facilities/).
Neither linked document was reviewed; all results here come from the supplied article and its charts.

## Main results and interpretation

- **NE 65th Street:** after removal of 12 parking spaces and striping a bicycle lane, the sales index rose far above
  comparison trends. Jaffe calls this a 400% increase, but the chart peaks near an index of 400 from a baseline of 100,
  making the percentage wording unsafe to reuse as a precise growth estimate.
- **Greenwood:** after removal of a traffic lane and some parking for bicycle lanes, sales generally tracked the
  surrounding area rather than showing a comparable surge. The chart also shows a dip around installation.
- Rowe's quoted conclusion is that neither district suffered a negative impact. Jaffe acknowledges the lack of
  experimental controls for attributing the 65th Street increase to the lane, but closes with a broader benefit-or-no-harm
  claim. That interpretation is stronger than these two observational comparisons establish.[^citylab2013]

The introduction's customer-spending argument links the already-ingested
[Portland coverage](/sources/2012-citylab-pedestrians-bikes-spending.md), not independent evidence. A closing reference
to NYC analysis supplies no additional numerical results; it is not extracted as another confirmation.

## Limits and extracted findings

The article gives no business counts, dollar totals, detailed comparison selection, uncertainty intervals or adjusted
effects. Sales indexes do not measure profits, customer numbers or individual-business survival. Other local changes
could explain trends, and corridor-level results cannot rule out losses at particular stores.[^citylab2013]
