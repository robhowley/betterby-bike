## Reporting and attribution

The roundup says crashes on New Jersey's main highways increased **27%** as enforcement declined, then kept rising
for six of the next seven months. It supplies no baseline, precise comparison period or crash counts for this
percentage. Its framing links the changes, but is not a causal analysis.[^nj2024]

The embedded post contains an image of Times prose: state troopers began writing far fewer tickets in **July 2023**;
the following month, citations for speeding, drunken driving, cellphone use and other violations fell **81% statewide
compared with the year before**. The quoted passage says the slowdown lasted more than eight months and coincided
with an almost immediate increase in motor-vehicle crashes, citing records obtained by the Times. It describes
troopers patrolling busy highways and remote rural roads, not a measured reduction in every police agency's
enforcement.[^nj2024]

Komanoff speculates that dozens of motorists, including farmstand owner Giuseppe Zuccarello, might still be alive
had officials addressed the slowdown instead of opposing New York congestion pricing. This is his political and
counterfactual interpretation, not a quantified mortality effect established by the visible reporting. The excerpt
also quotes academics describing the slowdown as unrivaled in modern policing, without their names or supporting
comparisons.[^nj2024]
