# Gothamist: NYC urban heat island, 9.7 degrees

Undated Gothamist Instagram post, with reporting credited to Caroline Lewis and photography to Angela Weiss / AFP
via Getty Images. The [supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/news/gothamist-nyc-urban-heat-island-9-7-degrees.pdf)
preserves one carousel slide and its visible caption, not a full article. No original post, article or study URL
is visible.[^gothamist]

## Reporting and evidence basis

The graphic reports “9.7 degrees” as how much hotter NYC is for the average resident due to tall buildings, little
tree cover and many cars and buses, calling it the largest per-capita temperature boost in the United States.
The caption describes urban areas with these characteristics as tending to be hotter than rural environments,
identifies this as the urban heat island effect, and attributes the national comparison to a “new study.” It
directs readers to an interactive neighborhood map via a link in the account's bio, but supplies neither the map
nor a usable URL.[^gothamist]

These are secondary claims; no temperature measurements or underlying study results were independently reviewed. The screenshot
does not name the study, its authors or its publication date. It does not state Fahrenheit or Celsius, the
comparison baseline used for the 9.7-degree figure, the observation/modeling period, the cities covered, how
residents were weighted, or uncertainty. The caption's general urban/rural explanation does not establish the
study's precise comparator, and “feels this effect” does not establish a heat-index measure.[^gothamist]

## Access and interpretation limits

Text extraction yielded no substantive text; the entire single-page screenshot was read visually. The visible
graphic and caption are legible, but other carousel slides, full-caption completeness, the associated article,
interactive map and underlying study were not reviewed. The publication date and national ranking remain
unverified beyond the supplied reporting.[^gothamist]

The screenshot does not separate contributions from buildings, tree cover and motor traffic, or evaluate tree
planting, traffic reduction, cycling or a street redesign. Its 9.7-degree claim must not be presented as a measured
cooling benefit from any such intervention. No standalone Finding is extracted because the quantitative claim
lacks units and study details; existing Topic conclusions are unchanged.
