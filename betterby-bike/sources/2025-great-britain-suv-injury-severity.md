# SUV injury severity in Great Britain, 2004–2023

Anna Goodman, Phil Edwards and Anthony A. Laverty, *Findings*, July 7, 2025.
[Original publication](https://doi.org/10.32866/001c.141647).

## Study and methods

The authors linked Stats19 police-reported road casualties in Great Britain, 2004–2023, to manufacturer-reported
body style and dimensions through Vehicle Data Global. They retained 359,003 pedestrian/cyclist casualties in
crashes involving one passenger car or SUV and no other motor vehicles, with valid vehicle identification and
complete covariates. There were 323,529 passenger-car and 35,474 SUV casualties; 275,978 slight injuries,
78,433 serious injuries and 4,592 fatalities. Table 1 lists 148,256 pedestrians and 210,747 cyclists.[^goodman2025]

Binary comparisons were fatal versus nonfatal injury and killed/seriously injured (KSI) versus slight injury.
Adjusted models included casualty mode, age and sex, driver age and sex, vehicle age, region, urban/rural status,
road class and speed limit, light, surface and year. SUV status, weight, height and length were modeled separately,
not mutually adjusted. Vehicle dimensions varying within make/model/year used averages among UK registrations
in February 2025; width was excluded because measurement definitions differed.[^goodman2025]

## Results

- Adjusted SUV fatality odds were 1.14 times passenger-car odds for adults aged 19+, 1.77 for children aged
  0–18 and 3.09 for ages 0–9. These are overlapping child strata, not independent groups.
- Adult cyclist KSI odds were higher (1.11), but adult pedestrian KSI odds were not clearly different (0.97).
  Child KSI odds were higher, particularly at ages 0–9. Age interactions and the adult mode interaction matter
  more than a single all-user SUV estimate.
- Weight and height generally had stronger severity associations than length, but not every subgroup showed
  a monotonic or statistically clear increase. Adult pedestrian KSI estimates were largely null.
- SUVs rose from 4% to 26% of striking cars in the analyzed casualties from 2004 to 2023. This is not a
  collision rate per vehicle. The authors' 3–35% attributable fractions assume replacing all SUVs with passenger
  cars and use adjusted odds ratios plus the 2023 SUV crash share; they are not observed preventable casualties.
- The paper compares its results with a mainly US systematic review. That review was not independently read
  and is not additional primary evidence from this ingestion.[^goodman2025]

## Access and limitations

Read all extracted text of the supplied ten-page article, including methods, Tables 1–3 and discussion.
Figure graphics were not visually reviewed; no exclusion counts were taken from Figure 1, and the SUV time-series
endpoints above are explicitly reported in prose. The linked external supplement, raw records, lookup data and
cited papers were not accessed. The article has no separate limitations section.

These observational, selected police-recorded casualties measure severity conditional on recorded injury, not
crash occurrence or risk per trip. Complete-case selection, reporting and classification differences, residual
confounding (including actual impact speed, which is not the speed-limit covariate), and retrospective dimension
averages limit causal interpretation. Dimensional models cannot isolate independent height or weight effects.
The attributable fractions additionally require causal and counterfactual assumptions; odds ratios are not
risk ratios, especially for common KSI outcomes. No fee, fleet-replacement or street-design intervention was tested.
