# Can BART’s new speedy fare gates finally solve the ‘piggybacking’ problem?

Rachel Swan, San Francisco Chronicle, May 4, 2026. Read the full 12-page PDF capture, including photo captions;
substantive reporting ends on p. 11. The [captured publication](../../raw-sources/news/can-bart-s-speedy-new-fare-gates-solve-the-piggybacking-problem.pdf)
is the reviewed source; an original article URL was not established. This is laboratory and station reporting
with agency interviews and selected rider accounts, not an independent gate evaluation.

## Changes being tested

BART officials describe shortening gate closure from 800 to 500 milliseconds, reducing the sensor area that
keeps panels open, and disabling part of an AI camera's movement-tracking function. The aim is to stop people
following paying passengers through the gates. Testing began in April 2026 at Antioch and the middle row of
Concord gates; officials expected systemwide rollout on May 18. That was a future plan at publication,
not confirmation of completion. A laboratory demonstration blocked a trailing spokesperson.[^chronicle]

Engineers describe months of adjustment to coordinate cameras and software while avoiding striking passengers.
At Concord, one rider said he had not seen piggybacking in a long time. Another passed with an electric scooter
without being struck; a woman flinched as panels opened and closed. These are individual observations, not
representative tests of disability access, bicycle passage, injury risk or throughput.[^chronicle]

## Older gate results are not results of the new timing

The article attributes declines in graffiti, vandalism, garbage-can emptying and fire-extinguisher spraying to
February board reports about the taller gates installed in 2025. Budget analysts estimated $10 million in annual
revenue and nearly 1,000 maintenance hours already saved. The annual revenue figure is an estimate, while the
maintenance claim has no stated observation period. Neither supports an effect of the subsequent faster-closing
upgrade. The historical estimate of up to $25 million a year lost to evasion also lacks methods in this capture.[^chronicle]

Paying riders reportedly complained about close tailgaters, station agents usually did not respond to gate
alarms, and police could not continuously monitor every entrance. These accounts explain the agency's motivation,
not the frequency of evasion or the effectiveness of a particular enforcement strategy.[^chronicle]

## Evidence limits and bundle contribution

Computer systems engineering manager John Yen says BART had not collected or shared piggybacking data, despite
the sensors' theoretical ability to log incidents. No baseline, before/after rate, denominator, independent
comparison or accessibility assessment is supplied. Reduced evasion, net revenue gains and safe passage cannot
be inferred from the demonstration or interviews. Linked reporting and underlying board documents were not reviewed.

This adds a station-entry constraint to the [transit-access synthesis](../topics/active-commuting-and-access.md#transit-coverage-and-usable-service-differ),
not evidence that cycling access improved. No separate Finding was created: implementation details and sparse
rider observations do not establish a reusable outcome estimate.
