# Road Diets and Emergency Response: Friends, Not Foes

Federal Highway Administration (FHWA), *Myth Busters*, report FHWA-SA-17-020.
[Original publication (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2016-fhwa-road-diet-response-times.pdf).
The pages do not state a publication date. The local filename says 2016; PDF metadata records creation in December
2016 and modification in March 2017, which does not establish a publication date.

## Scope and reporting basis

This two-page agency fact sheet describes converting four-lane undivided roads to one through lane each way plus a
two-way left-turn lane (TWLTL). It argues that emergency vehicles can use the center lane to bypass traffic more
predictably than on a four-lane road, where drivers in inside lanes may be unsure where to move. Before/after diagrams
illustrate the proposed mechanism, not measured response times.[^report]

The evidence consists of descriptive cases from West Avenue in Ocean City and South Broad Street in Woodbury, New
Jersey, including named police officials' accounts. It is not a timed before/after evaluation: no dispatch records,
response-time definition, sample sizes, observation periods, controls or uncertainty estimates are supplied.[^report]

## Reported outcomes

- **Ocean City:** West Avenue was a four-lane emergency thoroughfare with parallel parking and heavy bicycle and
  pedestrian use. After conversion to three lanes, FHWA reports easier passage using the TWLTL while drivers pull
  into the extra shoulder width. Police Traffic Sergeant Brian Hopely describes the configuration as safer; no
  numerical safety or response-time change is given.
- **Woodbury:** a mile-long South Broad Street road diet followed concerns about speeding, turns, parking and
  pedestrian safety. Retired Police Chief Reed Merinuk says response times were unaffected and responders found
  destinations easier and safer to reach. FHWA also reports reduced crashes and speeds and greater pedestrian
  confidence, without measurements or methods.[^report]

## Interpretation and limits

The fact sheet's broad assertions that road diets do not degrade response times and can significantly improve them
exceed the evidence shown here. In particular, Woodbury's quoted account describes **unchanged**, not faster, response
times. These selected favorable operational accounts support considering center-lane access in design discussions,
not a universal no-delay guarantee or an estimated time saving. They do not evaluate protected bike lanes independently
or establish performance where a usable center lane is absent.

The introductory 19–47% motor-vehicle crash reduction is attributed to a separate 2010 FHWA study,
[*Evaluation of Lane Reduction “Road Diet” Measures on Crashes*](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/10053/).
It is neither a response-time result nor a measured crash effect for these two cases. That study was not reviewed.
Both pages of this fact sheet were read as extracted text.[^report]
