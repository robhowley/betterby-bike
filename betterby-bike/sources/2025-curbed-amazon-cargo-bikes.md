# The Amazon Bike-Truck Has Been an Adjustment

Clio Chang, Curbed, September 17, 2025. The PDF metadata title is *Amazon Cargo E-Bikes Are Changing How We Use NYC
Streets*. Read the complete article on PDF pp. 1–3; pp. 4–8 contain site material.
[Original publication capture](../../raw-sources/news/amazon-cargo-e-bikes-are-changing-how-we-use-nyc-streets.pdf).

## Reporting and context

Chang observes Amazon pedal-assist delivery vehicles in Brooklyn and Manhattan and interviews drivers about sharing bike
lanes and general traffic. Drivers report cyclists telling them to leave bike lanes and motorists objecting to their
slower speeds. One explicitly feels safer in the bike lane. Public-rulemaking accounts include opposition from cyclists
and support from Transportation Alternatives for replacing larger delivery trucks.[^chang2025]

The article dates NYC's cargo-bike pilot to 2019 and the expanded rules to 2024. It reports a four-wheel commercial
pedal-assist category, maximum dimensions of 16 feet long, four feet wide and seven feet high, no sidewalk use even for
deliveries, and a 15-mph limit. Amazon vehicles reportedly top out at 12 mph. These are historical secondary
descriptions, not independently reviewed or verified current legal requirements. The article links
[DOT's 2024 announcement](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/html/pr2024/e-cargo-bike-on-city-streets.shtml) and
[pilot evaluation](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/commercial-cargo-bicycle-pilot-evaluation-report.pdf);
neither was read.[^chang2025]

## Claims, recommendations and limits

Chang explicitly says cargo-bike-specific traffic and safety data are unavailable. DOT's cited overall decline in
cyclist and other micromobility injuries does not isolate these vehicles. A reported European study's 60%-faster
city-center deliveries links to
[Rapid Transition's secondary cargo-bike account](https://rapidtransition.org/stories/large-tired-and-tested-how-europes-cargo-bike-roll-out-is-delivering/),
not a study reviewed here. The article supplies no study design, sample or uncertainty, and DOT's statement that two
large cargo-bike trips carry one delivery-truck load lacks load definitions. Neither figure is a measured NYC
Amazon-fleet effect.[^chang2025]

Chang argues wider or separate lanes would address conflicts. Transportation Alternatives spokesperson Alexa Sledge
advocates reallocating street space; one driver welcomes wider lanes. The article notes some wider Manhattan lanes and
Astoria's 16-foot-wide 31st Avenue boulevard, but does not evaluate them. Amazon declined comment on wider lanes. Claims
of safer, cleaner and more efficient deliveries are arguments supported by secondary reporting, not local measured
outcomes.[^chang2025]
