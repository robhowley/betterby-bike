# Transportation Energy Data Book: Edition 40

## Publication and coverage

Stacy C. Davis (Oak Ridge National Laboratory) and Robert G. Boundy (Roltek, Inc.), February 2022,
updated June 2022, ORNL/TM-2022/2376. Prepared for the US Department of Energy's Vehicle Technologies
Office. [Publisher site named in the report](https://tedb.ornl.gov/) ·
[Supplied publication](../../raw-sources/research/transportation-energy-data-book-edition-40.pdf).

This is a **partial, active-travel-focused reading** of a 444-page statistical compilation: title and
publication information, introduction (printed p. xxv), NHTS methods (p. 9–6), and pp. 9–22 through 9–24
(Tables 9.17–9.19 and Figure 9.9). Figure 9.9 was also read visually (PDF page 258). Contents and keyword
searches guided selection; the energy, vehicle, freight and emissions chapters and estimation appendices
were not substantively reviewed. No underlying survey files or external publications were reviewed.

## Reporting basis

The book compiles and sometimes reformats published transportation statistics, supplementing gaps with
ORNL estimates. Its introduction cautions that estimate accuracy cannot exceed primary-data accuracy.
The selected active-travel figures are generated from the **2017 National Household Travel Survey**
website, not a new independent travel study. NHTS samples US households and expands responses to
national estimates covering daily travel by all purposes and modes.[^tedb40]

Page 9–6 distinguishes person trips from vehicle trips: two household members in one car make two person
trips but one vehicle trip. It warns about changing survey procedures and populations across editions.
For 2017, online mapping produced derived distances described as about 10% shorter than self-reported
trips; that is a measurement warning, not evidence of a 10% behavioral decline.[^tedb40]

## Relevant results

- Figure 9.9 places **20% of bicycle person-trips and 6% of walking person-trips** in its Home To Work
  category, described in the accompanying text as to/from work. Other Social/Recreational accounts for
  31% and 30%, respectively; shopping accounts for 12% and 14%. These describe purposes within each
  mode, not the proportion of commuters cycling or walking.
- Table 9.17 reports to/from-work averages of **2.72 miles and 21.79 minutes by bicycle**, **1.19 miles
  and 15.26 minutes walking**, and **12.21 miles and 25.47 minutes by car**. They compare different
  observed journeys, not alternative modes on the same route.[^tedb40]

## Limitations and existing coverage

The selected results have no displayed sample sizes, confidence intervals or mode-specific distance-error
estimates. They do not identify access quality, infrastructure effects, journeys that could switch modes,
or energy/emissions savings. Historical trip profiles should not be presented as current conditions.

Table 9.19 labels its last column 2020 ACS but cites 2015–2019 five-year estimates, while adjacent
Table 9.18 cites 2015–2020. This dating inconsistency is unresolved; no commute-share trend is extracted.
The bundle's [2022 commuting brief](2024-commuting-in-the-united-states-2022.md) already addresses ACS
worker-share limits, while [Washington's estimation study](2017-estimating-walking-bicycling-state-level.md)
uses older 2009 NHTS inputs. Neither duplicates these 2017 within-mode purposes and work-trip averages.
