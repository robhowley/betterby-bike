# Improving Pedestrian and Bicyclist Safety at Intersections

Krista Nordback and colleagues, UNC Highway Safety Research Center, Toole Design, Kittelson and Associates,
William Hunter and Peter Koonce. April 2019, prepared for NCHRP. Although the cover says Final Report,
the disclaimer calls it an **uncorrected contractor draft**, not necessarily the sponsors' conclusions.
[Supplied report](../../raw-sources/research/nchrp15-63-finalreport-april2019v5coveredited-pdf.pdf).

## Scope and methods

The report combines a treatment-literature review, practitioner interviews, crash-type assessment and research-gap
prioritization with an original protected-intersection evaluation. This ingestion is a focused partial reading:
opening summary, Chapter 5's study overview, selected methods and results (especially pp. 52–58, 66–70 and 77–84),
and Chapter 6. The full literature review, crash-type analysis and appendices were not reviewed; their underlying
studies are not independently ingested here.[^report]

The original evaluation compared The Alameda/Hopkins with The Alameda/Marin in Berkeley, California, and
300 South/200 West with 300 South/200 East in Salt Lake City, Utah. Each pair contained one protected and one
nearby conventional intersection. Selection favored comparable four-leg, two-way, signalized sites, excluding
some one-way-street and two-way-cycle-track configurations. May 2018 daylight video covered three partial days
per site: 40 camera-hours per Berkeley site and 56 per Salt Lake City site, not that many distinct elapsed hours.
Radar measured selected turning movements; public records supplied crash histories.[^report]

## Results and interpretation

- Salt Lake City southbound right turns averaged 12 mph at the protected site versus 13 mph at the comparison
  (15 versus 42 vehicles). Berkeley lacked a usable right-turn comparison after radar-angle exclusions.
- Left-turn speeds were lower at the protected Salt Lake City site but higher at Berkeley's. Sparse bicycle
  speed measurements showed no significant difference and included instrument limitations.
- Behavior was mixed: the authors report more two-stage bicycle left turns but worse signal compliance at
  protected sites. Table 29 shows lower motorist-yield shares among bicycle/turning-vehicle yielding interactions
  at protected sites, but movement-specific and denominator-specific results differ.
- Crash records were too sparse for effectiveness estimates. The authors call the overall findings mostly
  equivocal; their view that protected intersections are promising is an interpretation, not a measured injury benefit.[^report]

## Limitations

Two nonrandomized site pairs cannot isolate design effects. Low interaction counts, differing traffic volumes,
limited daylight coverage, obscured video and restricted radar angles limit comparisons. Yielding required visible
speed or trajectory changes; yielding outside camera view could be missed. A short post-encroachment time alone
was not treated as proof of an unsafe encounter. Unequal before/after periods and underreported crashes further
limit crash interpretation.[^report]

The report contains unresolved inconsistencies: Table 30 lists one Berkeley comparison left-turn motorist yield,
where the text says both of two interactions yielded; Table 29's aggregate also implies two. Table 18's Berkeley
leg labels conflict with nearby prose. Neither discrepancy is silently repaired here.[^report]
