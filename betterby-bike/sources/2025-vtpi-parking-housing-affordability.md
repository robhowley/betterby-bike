# Parking Requirement Impacts on Housing Affordability

Todd Litman, Victoria Transport Policy Institute, **21 December 2025**. Subtitle: *The Costs of Residential
Parking Mandates and Benefits of Reforms*. [Supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/research/parking-requirement-impacts-on-housing-affordability.pdf)
· [Publisher URL](https://www.vtpi.org/park-hou.pdf). This account uses the supplied dated version.

## Question and evidence basis

The report asks how residential parking minimums affect housing costs, parking demand and access to compact,
multimodal neighborhoods. It combines narrative research synthesis, selected predominantly US case studies,
parking-cost estimates and hypothetical household budgets. It is not a systematic review, original controlled
reform evaluation or pooled estimate. Dollars are US dollars unless otherwise indicated.[^litman]

## Main results and recommendations

- Illustrative car-free, lower-income households pay $150 monthly for one mandated space or $300 for two:
  about 13% of assumed $1,200 rent or 21% of $1,400 rent. These are assumed costs and potential savings,
  not observed rent reductions after reform.
- Secondary occupancy accounts describe substantial unused parking in selected Boston, Denver and Seattle
  multifamily developments. The New Jersey rental summary also retains a small parking shortfall for
  two-bedroom high-rise units, so excess supply is not universal.
- The author estimates typical annualized parking costs of $1,500 to over $5,000 per space and concludes
  reform can typically lower basic housing costs 10–20%. That range is a synthesis claim, not an identified
  average treatment effect or guaranteed pass-through to tenants.
- Litman recommends removing minimums, unbundling parking charges from housing, sharing spaces, managing
  spillover and improving walking, cycling, transit and carshare access. Removing minimums permits rather
  than prohibits parking. Secure bicycle parking and e-bike charging are recommendations, not evaluated
  cycling interventions.[^litman]

## Trade-offs and limits

The report recognizes convenience, reduced spillover conflicts and lower fee-collection costs from abundant
bundled parking. Reform may mean longer walks or direct parking charges. It recommends managing nearby public
parking and fee discounts for low-income and disabled motorists. Households with vehicles, opportunities to rent
out spare spaces, older housing, subsidies or existing mandate reductions do not face identical burdens.
Vehicle ownership, land costs and travel alternatives matter; reform does not itself establish adequate non-car access.

Several internal discrepancies limit numerical reuse:

- Page 14 calls 0.80 × 0.85 × 0.90 a **61% reduction**. It is 61.2% remaining demand, or a 38.8% reduction;
  overlapping adjustment factors still require judgment, not mechanical transfer.
- Table 5's non-car-free excess-space entries do not consistently equal mandated spaces minus displayed
  ownership; Table 5 and Figures 13–14 also differ in household labels. Only transparent car-free examples
  are extracted as a Finding.
- The Gabbe/Pierce rent estimate is assigned to 2012 on p. 19 and 2010 on p. 22. Jia/Wachs is cited as 2019
  in the text but 1999 in the references. Neither discrepancy is resolved here.
- TDM summaries differ across pp. 13 and 21 in magnitudes and comparison descriptions. They are not
  combined into a reform effect. Secondary studies lack enough methods and uncertainty here to reassess
  causality; none was independently read.[^litman]
