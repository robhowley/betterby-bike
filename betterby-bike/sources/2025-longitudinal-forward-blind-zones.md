# Forward blind-zone changes in popular vehicle models

Alexander K. Epstein, Alyssa Brodeur, Juwon Drake, Eric Englin, Donald L. Fisher, Stephen Zoepf,
Becky C. Mueller and Haden Bragg. *SAE International Journal of Transportation Safety* 13(1):67–78,
2025; online March 26, 2025. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.4271/09-13-01-0005).

## Study and methods

Researchers measured 17 accessible vehicles from six top-selling U.S. nameplates across selected redesign
cycles, model years 1997–2023: Chevrolet Suburban, Ford F-150, Honda CR-V, Honda Accord, Jeep Grand Cherokee
and Toyota Camry. This compares different vehicles/generations, not repeated observations of the same car
or a representative fleet sample. The IIHS markerless camera method projected manually annotated window,
mirror and pillar boundaries onto the ground plane. All measurements used a standardized 50th-percentile
male eyepoint and mid-height, mid-track seat position, photographing the forward 180° in 30° increments.
Researchers removed nonphysical outliers and overlapping points in ArcGIS and constructed visible/occluded
areas within semicircles centered on the driver, not the bumper.[^epstein2025]

## Results and interpretation

All six nameplates had lower visible ground area within 10 m in their latest versus earliest measured models.
The CR-V fell from 106.3 to 44.2 m² (1997–2022), and Suburban from 88.5 to 43.3 m² (2006–2023), about 58%
and 51% reductions. Sedan changes were only 7–8%, which the authors say may fall within measurement error.
Changes were not uniformly downward at every redesign: Grand Cherokee visibility improved from 2020 to 2023
but remained below 2006. In the separate 10–20 m band, visibility increased for some models and decreased
for others. Table 1 supplies descriptive measurements, not confidence intervals or causal estimates.[^epstein2025]

The authors interpret near-field losses as motivation to study a possible visibility–fatality relationship,
not proof of one. They discuss mirrors, lenses, cameras and automatic braking as possible countermeasures,
with limitations, rather than evaluating their safety effects. Background crash statistics and heavy-truck
simulator studies are secondary context, not outcomes measured in these 17 vehicles.[^epstein2025]

## Access and limitations

Read all 12 supplied PDF pages visually (printed pp. 67–78), including Table 1, Figures 1–7, methods,
discussion/future work and references. The PDF yielded no substantive text extraction. External papers,
software, datasets and DOI landing page were not independently reviewed. The paper says numerical 15 m
results are available from the authors but does not report them; they were not obtained.

Coverage is limited to six nameplates, selected years and one eyepoint/seat configuration. Ground-plane
occlusion is not whole-person invisibility or visibility at shoulder height. Static geometry does not capture
moving drivers and road users, recognition, reaction time or indirect-vision aids. No crash linkage, exposure
denominator or causal model estimates how much these changes affect injuries or fatalities. The authors call
for broader sampling, multiple driver heights, confounder control and further measurement assessment.[^epstein2025]
