# Streets for Recovery: The Economic Benefits of the NYC Open Streets Program

NYC Department of Transportation, October 2022. [Original report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/streets-for-recovery-the-economic-benefits-of-the-nyc-open-streets-program.pdf).
Bloomberg Associates provided technical support, Bennett Midland conducted analysis and interviews, and NYC's
Department of Finance supplied anonymized sales-tax aggregates.

## Scope and methods

The report compares restaurants and bars on five selected full-closure Open Streets with nearby non-Open-Streets
corridors in Astoria, Chinatown, Koreatown, Park Slope and Prospect Heights. Selection required early participation,
a comparable nearby commercial corridor and at least ten tax filers per study area in each quarter. Matching
considered length, restaurant/bar counts, traffic lanes, transit proximity and pre-pandemic economic trends.
These five corridors span Queens, Manhattan and Brooklyn, not all five boroughs.[^report]

The study covers March 1, 2020–August 31, 2021, six tax-reporting quarters. Baseline data cover March 1,
2017–February 29, 2020; sales comparisons use average quarterly taxable sales from that baseline. Outcomes are
sales, tax-filer counts and continued filing by pre-pandemic firms. Main controls had low Open Restaurants
participation; an additional comparison used nearby corridors with high outdoor-dining participation but no
Open Streets. Open Streets allowed dining in roadway space otherwise used by moving vehicles, distinct from
Open Restaurants' sidewalk/roadway dining permissions.[^report]

## Results and interpretation

- By summer 2021, the report's mean corridor sales comparison was +19% from baseline on Open Streets versus
  −29% on main controls, a 48-percentage-point difference, not a causal 48% sales gain. Chinatown still had
  lower sales than baseline. Three high-Open-Restaurants comparisons favored Open Streets; two were similar.
- Pre-pandemic filer retention was higher on the selected Open Streets in each studied borough. Brooklyn's
  control retention is inconsistently reported as 80% in prose and 81% in the table.
- Net filer counts grew more, or contracted less, than on controls. Chinatown's count fell 8%; the headline
  about growth in “new businesses” refers to net counts after closures, not gross openings.[^report]

DOT interprets these comparisons as evidence that the program supported recovery and recommends its expansion.
Merchant testimonials describe perceived business survival; they are not independent estimates of prevented
closures. The contextual 100,000-jobs-saved claim is not estimated by the analysis presented here.

## Limitations and access

Read all extracted text from the 14-page PDF and visually read the charts/table on printed pp. 15, 17 and 19.
Underlying tax records and the cited 2013 economic report were not reviewed. This publication provides no raw
sales or filer denominators, confidence intervals, model specification or significance tests despite using
“significantly.” It does not establish representative program-wide effects, isolate cycling infrastructure,
measure profits/jobs or demonstrate net citywide spending gains. Nonrandom corridor selection, different outdoor
dining participation and uneven pandemic recovery limit attribution; additional high-dining controls are unnamed
and unquantified. Seasonal adjustment and inflation adjustment are not documented. The stated early-participation
criterion lacks corridor-specific implementation dates. Filer retention is an administrative survival proxy,
not a direct count of jobs saved or proof that a business would otherwise have closed.

The report builds on the 2013 methodology underlying the existing [NYC retail comparisons](/findings/business/nyc-pbl-retail-comparisons.md),
but these are distinct pandemic-era hospitality observations, not another estimate of earlier bike-lane effects.
