# Lessons from the Green Lanes

Christopher Monsere, Jennifer Dill, Nathan McNeil, Kelly J. Clifton, Nick Foster, Tara Goddard, Mathew Berkow, Joe Gilpin,
Kim Voros, Drusilla van Hengel and Jamie Parks. NITC-RR-583, June 2014.
[Original report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2014-06-pdx-lessons-green-lanes.pdf),
[publication record](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/cengin_fac/144/),
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.15760/trec.115).

## Question and methods

The study evaluated use, design comprehension, conflicts and perceptions of recently installed protected bicycle lanes
in Austin, Chicago, Portland, San Francisco and Washington, D.C. Routes included Barton Springs, Bluebonnet, Rio Grande,
Dearborn, Milwaukee, NE Multnomah, Oak, Fell and L Street. Candidate facilities came from the Green Lane Project cohort,
not a representative sample of U.S. streets. Many used relatively temporary paint/flexpost treatments.[^report]

The 2013 fieldwork combined city-provided before counts with city/video after counts, nearby-resident mail surveys
(2,283 responses, 23% of deliverable mailings), and bicyclist intercept invitations to an online survey (1,111 responses,
33% of distributed postcards). Video review covered 168 hours, 16,393 bicyclists and 19,724 turning/merging vehicles;
the intersection safety subset covered 144 hours and nearly 12,900 bicycles. Austin had no video analysis. The study
collected no original before observations or control-location data. Not every route had every survey component.[^report]

## Results and interpretation

- **Use:** the report's summary estimates show route-count increases of 21–171% within roughly a year, not equivalent
  growth in total cycling. Intercept responses suggest both route diversion and trips otherwise made by another mode.
- **Comfort and design:** riders rated vertical separation more favorably than paint-only buffers. Intersection
  positioning varied across designs; high stated understanding did not always match observed behavior.
- **Safety:** no collisions or near collisions were observed in the safety video sample, but low-level conflicts were.
  Riders also self-reported collisions outside that observation window. These are not long-term injury-risk estimates.
- **Public response:** nearby respondents generally supported more protected lanes, including most car commuters.
  Driving, parking and pedestrian experiences were mixed, with several more negative Chicago results.
- **Business:** most respondents reported no change in visits; favorable overall balances coexisted with negative
  balances among Chicago residents. Sales or profit changes were not analyzed in this report.[^report]

The authors recommend extending protected lanes and carefully designing intersections. Those recommendations draw on
perceptions, observed behavior and prior literature, not a demonstrated causal reduction in crashes in this study.

## Limitations

Short, inconsistently timed counts are sensitive to weather, season and route substitution. The ACS citywide commuting
comparison is not a matched control. Resident respondents were older and more often homeowners and college graduates
than nearby Census populations; intercept respondents already used the lanes. Nonresponse, recall and selection limit
generalization. Some tables count the same respondents' answers for multiple facilities, so table totals need not equal
unique people. Designs, traffic and network roles differed across locations.[^report]

Reporting inconsistencies also warrant care: Table 6-1 omits an Oak summary estimate although Table 6-3 gives Oak
comparisons; Table 6-6's 24% overall cycling-frequency increase does not reconcile with its displayed site percentages
and sample sizes; the conflict summary's six minor conflicts omit a pedestrian conflict included in Tables 8-8–8-9.
The Findings retain qualified measures rather than silently repairing these totals.
