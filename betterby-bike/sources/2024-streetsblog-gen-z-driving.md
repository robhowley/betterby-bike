# Gen Z licensing and driving, Streetsblog coverage

Kea Wilson, Streetsblog USA, December 24, 2024. [Original publication](https://usa.streetsblog.org/2024/12/24/best-of-2024-the-real-disappointing-reason-why-gen-z-is-getting-fewer-drivers-licenses).
The editor identifies this as an evergreen republication, not a new study or independent replication.

## Reporting basis

Wilson covers University of Houston researcher Kailai Wang's comparison of U.S. youth travel in 2009
and 2017 and interviews him about generational differences. The PDF links the
[underlying study, ScienceDirect identifier S0264275124001653](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0264275124001653).
That study was not independently reviewed. The article reports adjusted associations but gives no sample
size, model specification, uncertainty intervals or complete survey methods.[^wilson2024]

## Main evidence

The article reports that 65% of 16–20-year-olds had a license in 2017, described as “down 8 percent”
from 2009 without clarifying relative percent versus percentage points. It reports median weekly driving
trips down 16% but median weekly driving distance up nearly 12% between those years. Overall trips
for purposes other than school and work reportedly fell by double-digit percentages. Online ordering
in the preceding week more than doubled in this age group and nearly tripled among unlicensed youth.
These are reported historical comparisons, not measured effects of cycling infrastructure.[^wilson2024]

Wang describes a remaining generational difference after adjustment for socioeconomic and built-environment
variables, and a negative association between population density and willingness to acquire a license.
He interprets some residual difference as changing attitudes. Wilson also discusses unequal documentation,
licensing and ownership barriers, citing other researchers, and the possibility that young people simply
stay home more. Those mechanisms are not separately quantified here. COVID-era digital behavior is
context, not an explanation established by data ending in 2017.

Wang recommends nearby destinations and public transport to sustain lower car dependence. He cautions
that reduced ownership could lead to ride-hailing rather than walking, cycling or transit. These are
interpretations and recommendations, not evaluated mode-shift results.

## Limits and reconciliation

Read substantive PDF pp. 1–4; later pages contain the author biography and site material. Embedded
chart values were not independently transcribed; numerical claims above come from the prose. Exact
trip/distance denominators, survey design and the licensing-change unit remain unresolved.
[Newsweek's 2025 car-buying coverage](/sources/2025-newsweek-car-buying-interest.md) mentions teenage
licensing trends but does not represent this Wang comparison. Its purchase intentions are a different
measure. Neither account establishes cycling substitution or improved access.
