# Economic Impacts of Bicycle and Pedestrian Street Improvements

National Street Improvements Study, Portland State University, Bennett Midland and PeopleForBikes.
[Original summary report (local PDF)](../../raw-sources/research/2020-economic-impacts-of-street-Improvements-summary-report.pdf).
The document has no printed publication date; the filename and February 2020 PDF metadata supply the year used here.

## Scope and methods

This five-page summary examines before/after business activity on **14 improved corridors and 14 control corridors**
in Indianapolis, Memphis, Minneapolis, Portland (Oregon), San Francisco and Seattle. Listed improvements date from
2000 to 2014 and include bicycle and pedestrian infrastructure, sometimes with travel-lane or parking removal.
Retail and food-service businesses were studied; car-oriented businesses such as gas stations were excluded.[^report]

Data sources were Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) employment, Quarterly Census of Employment and
Wages (QCEW), National Establishment Time Series (NETS) employment/sales, and retail sales tax records. The study used
aggregated trend analysis, difference-in-differences (DID) and interrupted time series (ITS). City partners helped
select corridors except in San Francisco and Portland; controls were intended to resemble treated business corridors.
The summary does not reproduce matching diagnostics, observation windows or model specifications.[^report]

## Main results

- The authors describe little evidence of economic harm overall. Their retail tally is nine cases with positive
  sales and/or employment results, two with no impact and three with mixed results. This is a corridor classification,
  not an average effect size or a claim that every metric improved.
- Favorable retail examples include Minneapolis's Lyndale Avenue South and Portland's Stark/Oak corridor. Madison
  Avenue in Memphis combines significantly higher retail sales with potentially adverse retail employment;
  Broad Avenue and Minneapolis's Riverside Avenue have inconclusive retail evidence.
- Food service is described as the sector benefiting most, including some projects removing parking or travel lanes.
  Yet San Francisco's 17th Street has negative or null food-service results, Seattle's Second Avenue has no significant
  effect, and Riverside's food-service growth is not clearly attributable to the improvement.
- The report cautions that sales revenue can rise through higher prices rather than more purchases, and employment
  and wages are poor indicators of immediate consumer responses. It recommends using multiple sources and methods.
  The authors prefer ITS for robustness while noting its need for more post-intervention observations.[^report]

## Limits and relationship to earlier evidence

The overview's statement that impacts were positive or non-significant is broader than its corridor table supports:
negative and mixed results must remain visible. The summary supplies significance labels but no effect coefficients,
confidence intervals, business counts or detailed analysis periods. Null results do not prove zero effect. Its multiple
models and datasets are analyses of the same projects, not independent replications; the selected corridors do not
establish a national success rate or the separate effect of bike lanes, pedestrian changes or parking removal.

Earlier NYC and San Francisco evaluations are background, not additional cases to pool with these findings. The
2013 NYC report also underlies the bundle's [2014 NYC retail comparisons](/findings/business/nyc-pbl-retail-comparisons.md).
All five supplied PDF pages were read as extracted text, and the results tables on PDF pp. 3–4 were checked visually.
The separate final technical report, guidebook, underlying datasets and background studies were not reviewed. The
summary's guidebook reference still contains a placeholder rather than a usable link.
