# 2024 State Report Cards

Safe Routes Partnership, September 3, 2024. The supplied seven-page PDF captures the organization's
resource page, not the full *Making Strides 2024* report. All substantive captured text was read;
interactive maps are not available as readable state-level results in the extraction.
[Underlying report](https://saferoutespartnership.org/resource/making-strides-2024-state-report-cards-on-support-for-walking-bicycling-and-active-kids-and-communities/)
and linked grading guide, appendices and webinar were not reviewed.

## Reporting basis

The page summarizes the publisher's state policy inventory as of 2024 across four areas: Complete Streets
and active-transportation planning; federal/state active-transportation funding; Safe Routes to School
funding and supportive practices; and active neighborhoods and schools. It links detailed scoring methods
rather than reproducing them. This is a policy benchmark, not an evaluation of cycling outcomes.[^srp]

## Main reported results

- **37 states** had some form of Complete Streets policy, primarily through transportation-department policies.
- **38 states** defined and prioritized high-need communities within Transportation Alternatives Program (TAP)
  funding in 2024, compared with **24 in 2022**; **17** supplied matching funds for projects benefiting those communities.
- **33 states** dedicated state revenue to walking, biking and/or Safe Routes to School; the two-year average
  annual state spending exceeded **$1 billion**, and **23 of those 33** increased per-capita spending since 2022.
- **21 states** gave Safe Routes to School a TAP scoring preference or set-aside. **32** allowed TAP funding
  for its programming, while **19** did not, despite federal eligibility.
- Supportive school-design guidelines remained at **11 states**, unchanged from 2022. **18 states** had at least
  two full-time-equivalent Safe Routes to School staff, with another **8** having at least one.[^srp]

The publisher interprets increased equity prioritization as an attempt to reduce funding-access barriers.
Its staffing paragraph refers to a correlation with best/emerging practices, but supplies neither an effect
size nor enough research detail to extract a separate result.

## Limits and extracted finding

Policy adoption, eligibility and staffing do not establish funds reaching communities, completed facilities,
safer journeys or equitable outcomes. The captured page does not define high need, reproduce scoring rules,
identify the two spending-average years or explain whether its jurisdiction counts include DC. In particular,
its 32 eligible plus 19 ineligible jurisdictions total 51 despite calling them states; retain counts without
inventing a denominator or percentages. State-level rankings and grading reproducibility remain unreviewed.
These are historical 2024 claims, not current policy terms.
