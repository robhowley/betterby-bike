# Daylighting and Street Safety: An Analysis

New York City Department of Transportation, Office of Research, Implementation & Safety, January 2025.
[Original report](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/daylighting-and-street-safety.pdf).

## Study and methods

DOT used 2017–2023 crash data in two different analyses. A before/after comparison covered 756 intersections
with treatments installed in 2019–2021: 567 sign-only and 189 hardened, including Citi Bike stations, bike
corrals, turn calming, neckdowns and enhanced crosswalks. Two years before and after each installation were
compared with nearby untreated intersections using average differences in annual injury changes. Other
concurrent safety interventions were excluded; nearby controls were intended to account for localized
COVID-era trends.[^dot2025]

A separate cross-sectional analysis compared intersections with approaching-side hydrants or bus stops
that restrict parking against intersections without them. These longstanding features lacked installation
dates. The total-injury analysis included 20,543 intersections, 7,558 with these “hydrant zones”; the pedestrian
analysis included 7,113 intersections, 2,747 with zones. Injury counts were divided by nearby intersection
counts, not pedestrian or vehicle exposure.[^dot2025]

## Results and interpretation

Hardened daylighting was associated with **0.14 fewer pedestrian injuries per intersection-year** relative
to nearby changes (p=0.01). Sign-only daylighting had no statistically significant pedestrian or total-injury
association; hardened daylighting also had no significant total-injury association. The 14 neckdown sites
had a larger pedestrian estimate, but treatments change crossing geometry as well as visibility. These
are not isolated bicycle-injury or visibility-only effects.[^dot2025]

Hydrant-zone intersections had **1.3 times** the normalized pedestrian and total injury measures of
non-zone intersections. This cross-sectional association does not establish that removing parking caused
injuries or that universal daylighting would increase harm. DOT recommends site-specific hardening and
other robust treatments rather than universal sign-only daylighting. Its risk-compensation and wider/faster-turn
explanations are hypotheses, not mechanisms tested by these injury comparisons.[^dot2025]

## Access and limitations

Read the complete extracted text of the supplied 53-page PDF, including printed pp. 29–41 methods/results
and pp. 47–49 limitations, sample accounting and appendix table. Illustrations were not separately visually
reviewed; no underlying data, external case-study evaluations or current legal requirements were checked.

Crashes are assigned to intersections rather than treated approaches. Parking noncompliance is unknown;
pedestrian volumes and precise vehicle exposure are unavailable. Nearby counts imperfectly normalize
conditions. Hydrant-zone samples exclude many other treatments and intersections with zero nearby injuries;
some eligibility datasets are not current through 2023. Before/after subgroups are small and nonrandom,
and nearby controls do not prove equivalent underlying trends. Reported tests use a 0.05 threshold;
confidence intervals and a multiple-comparison adjustment are not provided.[^dot2025]

The p. 41 narrative describes a prior curb-extension result as 44% fewer pedestrian injuries, whereas p. 26
labels its similar 44.7% estimate as pedestrian killed/severely injured. That secondary figure is not extracted.
The report's comparative recommendations are not a randomized ranking of treatments.
