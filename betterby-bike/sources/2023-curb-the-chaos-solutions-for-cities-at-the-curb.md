# Curb the Chaos: Solutions for Cities at the Curb

Open Plans, June 2023. The publication team credits Michael Sutherland and Sara Lind as project lead and
contributors, with graphic design by Angel Mendoza.
[Original report, supplied PDF](../../raw-sources/research/2023-curb-the-chaos-solutions-for-cities-at-the-curb.pdf).
Open Plans identifies itself as a people-first street advocacy organization; this report presents a policy synthesis.

## Question and reporting basis

The report asks how cities should allocate and manage curb space as deliveries, passenger pickup, micromobility
and public-space uses compete with vehicle storage. It combines a literature review, an explicitly non-systematic
review of U.S. city strategies, practitioner interviews in 12 cities, and interviews with curb users and technology
companies. Practitioners were recruited starting with field leaders and through inquiry and professional networks.
The authors describe geographic and city-size diversity, but this is not a probability sample. The interviewee list
includes disability advocates, cycling/community groups, freight and rideshare businesses, and enforcement/data
vendors; appearance does not imply endorsement of the recommendations.[^openplans]

## Main synthesis and case studies

- Four interview-derived priorities are recognizing a multipurpose curb lane, coordinating curb policies,
  consolidating and standardizing digital curb data, and improving enforcement. These are the authors' synthesis,
  not four experimentally demonstrated interventions.
- Seattle's land-use hierarchy puts modal-plan priorities first, with long-term vehicle storage ranked differently
  across residential, commercial/mixed and industrial settings (Figure 1). San Francisco's 2020 strategy supplies
  a hierarchy and implementation priorities. Its reported inventory allocates 90% of curb to parking and 1% to
  active uses, but this report does not reproduce the inventory's measurement definitions. These describe policy
  and allocation, not measured subsequent safety gains.
- Omaha's digital-curb account describes field verification, manual data conversion and adoption of the Open
  Mobility Foundation's Curb Data Specification (CDS). As of March 2023, 670 curb zones were reportedly publicly
  accessible through APIs requiring technical knowledge. A user-friendly map was planned. Reduced cruising,
  dwell time and conflict are intended benefits, not quantified outcomes in this case.
- Figure 2 distinguishes Omaha camera-based billing, Philadelphia app reservations with officer enforcement,
  and Oakland permit/location-data billing. These are different smart-loading-zone implementations, not three
  measured confirmations of shorter loading times.[^openplans]

## Secondary results and their boundaries

The curb-use pages mix cited research with expected benefits, implementation conflicts and proposed solutions.
Selected numerical claims include SFpark's 43% shorter parking-search time, 8% less traffic and 22% less double
parking; NYC neighborhood loading zones' reported 73% double-parking reduction at **some sites**; and San Francisco
bus-lane enforcement's delay reductions of up to 20% on some corridors. No common comparison design or pooled
estimate is supplied. The NYC bus-enforcement paragraph attributes an improvement of up to 36% to reliability,
without defining the measure; it should not be silently relabeled a speed increase.[^openplans]

The report also cites Vade's camera-based bike-lane violation estimates. Observations from 13 Manhattan cameras
are extrapolated to 6.8 million annual citywide violations and $780 million in potential citations. Those are
vendor-study estimates reported secondhand, not a citywide count, collected revenue or an evaluated enforcement
benefit. The observation-window description and scaling cannot be reconstructed here.

Hoboken's 2018–2022 zero-death record is attributed largely to daylighting, but no design separating daylighting
from other changes is supplied. Bike-parking adoption, parklet revenue and carshare benefits are likewise
secondary claims, not effects measured by Open Plans. Their cited primary studies were not read.[^openplans]

## Recommendations, trade-offs and limitations

Open Plans recommends neighborhood-specific curb plans rather than blindly applying a hierarchy, explicitly noting
that replacing a loading zone with a bike lane without relocating loading can create conflicts. It advocates
micromobility storage, protected lanes, loading access and public/green space instead of default free parking.
The report retains maintenance/procurement costs, inaccessible or obstructed loading, dockless sidewalk clutter,
private use of public space for dining, and the risk that EV chargers lock curb space into a single use.

Digital inventories should develop alongside physical reforms, not delay them; recommended contracts preserve
city data ownership and standard formats. Enforcement should prioritize behavior and efficiency over revenue.
Privacy, equitable camera placement and state authorization remain constraints, not demonstrated solved problems.
The effort/impact/timeline ratings in pp. 60–61 and the proposed NYC hierarchy in Figure 3 are author judgments
and recommendations, not measured effect sizes. No systematic quality appraisal, interview coding protocol,
controlled original outcome evaluation or uncertainty estimates for the overall recommendations are provided.[^openplans]

## Access and evidence overlap

Read the complete extracted text of the 86-page supplied PDF, including substantive pp. 1–79, methods,
benefits/conflicts/solutions tables, Figures 1–3, recommendation tables, interviewees and references; visually
checked printed pp. 55–56 (PDF pp. 59–60). Other illustrations and photographs were not individually inspected.
No cited primary study, municipal report, vendor analysis or linked digital sample was independently reviewed.
Publication/title and underlying-result searches found no existing summary or matching Findings. Repeated SFpark
claims within this report are one evaluation, not independent confirmations; its general NYC transit/design
examples must not be counted as new replications of existing municipal evidence in the bundle.
