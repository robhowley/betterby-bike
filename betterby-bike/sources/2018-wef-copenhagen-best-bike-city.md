# What makes Copenhagen the world's most bike-friendly city?

Sean Fleming, World Economic Forum, October 5, 2018. [Original article](https://www.weforum.org/agenda/2018/10/what-makes-copenhagen-the-worlds-most-bike-friendly-city/).
Read all 13 PDF pages as text; substantive reporting is on pp. 3–6. This is a news explainer,
not an infrastructure evaluation or a comparative city-ranking study.

## Reporting and evidence

Fleming describes Copenhagen's cycling infrastructure as an explanation for its popularity and safety. He reports
29% of all journeys by bicycle, 41% of work/study commutes and 62% among people both living and working/studying in
the city. Daily cycling distance was 1.4 million km in 2016, up 22% since 2006. Reported perceived safety rose from
53% to 76% over 2006–2016; the council's 90% target for 2025 was a goal, not an achieved result.[^fleming2018]

The article repeats finance-ministry values of 4.80 kroner per bicycle kilometre or 10.09 when replacing a car
journey, already covered by [The Copenhagen Post](/sources/2018-cph-post-value-of-biking.md). Its potential daily
benefit of US$1.05m–2.17m scales those values by cycling distance, not measured cash savings. It attributes 32% of
high-street and supermarket spending to bicycle arrivals via the Cycling Embassy of Denmark, but supplies no
survey details. Claims about congestion, sick leave and stronger retail effects in cycle-friendly areas are
explanations in the article, not separately evaluated mechanisms.[^fleming2018]

Reported investment since 2005 was around one billion kroner. For 2017 it lists 3.9 km of new paths, five new super
cycle paths and 3,500 parking stands. Its printed “600 km of green cycling routes” also appears in the Copenhagen
Post coverage; the unit and intended measure remain unresolved. The authorities' expectation of 15%–20% more
cyclists per dedicated cycleway is not a measured effect in this article.[^fleming2018]

International examples include planned French investment, Xiamen's elevated cycleway and discarded shared bicycles
in Xiamen and Beijing, qualifying a uniformly positive expansion story. Fleming also attributes typical cycling-scheme
benefit-to-cost ratios of 5:1–19:1 and £377 million in NHS savings for a single “cycling city” to University of
Birmingham work for the UK Department for Transport. The article does not give the savings horizon or enough
methodological detail to transfer these estimates to Copenhagen.[^fleming2018]

## Limits and extracted Findings

Underlying municipal statistics, ministry calculations, retail evidence and the UK study were not separately
reviewed. No survey samples, uncertainty estimates or controlled infrastructure comparisons are supplied. The
headline does not substantiate a standardized “best city” ranking; perceived safety is not injury risk. Repeated
valuation and investment reporting is not independent confirmation of the Copenhagen Post account.
