# Can This Tool Predict Where Your City's Next Car Crash Will Happen?

## Publication and coverage

Kea Wilson, Streetsblog USA, April 13, 2026. The full nine-page PDF capture was read;
substantive reporting occupies pp. 1–7. The apparent illustration spaces on pp. 4–6 are blank in
this capture, not readable charts. The article is principally an interview with Ryan McMahon,
Cambridge Mobile Telematics' senior vice president, not an independent tool evaluation.
Linked company material, municipal analyses and underlying datasets were not reviewed.

## Reporting and evidence

StreetVision, announced the preceding September, combines volunteered cellphone telematics with
public crash records to identify potential hot spots and assess countermeasures. The article reports
adoption by transportation departments in New Mexico, Massachusetts, Ohio and Texas, among others.
McMahon describes anonymized, aggregated data volunteered for insurance discounts, covering 55 million
drivers, characterized as roughly one in five US license-holders. Hard braking, speeding and phone
manipulation provide faster behavior indicators than waiting for official crash records.[^article]

The article reports two local changes observed through the platform: a Flagstaff, Arizona, signal-to-roundabout
conversion followed by 24.5% less hard braking and 14% less phone distraction, detected within weeks;
and Kenmore, Washington, school-zone speed cameras followed by 30% less speeding within two weeks of
24/7 enforcement. It supplies no baseline event counts, traffic denominators, precise metric definitions,
comparison sites or uncertainty estimates. These are reported behavior changes, not measured crash or
injury reductions.[^article]

For Mississippi, the platform reportedly estimates active phone manipulation during 3.4% of driving time,
excluding travel below 10 mph and phone use classified as passenger or transit-rider activity. The claimed
filtering accuracy is not evaluated. McMahon also says phones were in drivers' hands in the minute before
about one-third of crashes. This is not comparable to the article's separate NHTSA figure that 8% of crash
deaths involve a driver using a cellphone: denominators and attribution differ, and pre-crash phone presence
does not itself establish causation.[^article]

An Ohio example describes weaker response to a hands-free law near the Kentucky border, proposed as a
reason to target awareness spending geographically. McMahon proposes ranking intersections to protect
pedestrians and cyclists before crashes occur. He emphasizes that the tool supplies information, not
automated infrastructure changes or decisions. These are targeting proposals and developer interpretations,
not measured effects of reallocating spending or evidence of lives saved.[^article]

## Limits and reconciliation

Voluntary insurance-linked participation does not establish representative coverage of all drivers, streets
or times. The article gives no held-out prediction accuracy, false-positive rates, calibration, causal
countermeasure analysis or cyclist/pedestrian exposure measures. Its developer-centered reporting cannot
establish that StreetVision predicts or prevents crashes as advertised.

Unlike the existing [Chicago camera crash-reduction coverage](2022-streetsblog-speed-camera-equity.md),
the Kenmore account reports speeding, not a crash outcome. The platform's proposed timeliness advantage
also differs from the documented [New Jersey crash-data access gaps](2024-new-jersey-crash-data-access.md);
this article does not evaluate whether it resolves those gaps.
