# The Origins of US Transportation Policy: Was There Ever Support for Gasoline Taxes?

Christopher R. Knittel, **7 January 2013**, MIT CEEPR Working Paper 2013-010.
[Supplied publication](../../raw-sources/research/2013-010-pdf.pdf), 37 numbered pages plus cover and title/abstract.

## Question and evidence basis

The paper asks whether gasoline taxes were seriously considered during the 1970s U.S. oil crises, and why
policymakers chose price controls, rationing, fuel-economy standards and vehicle taxes instead. It combines a
narrative review of contemporary newspapers, reported opinion polls, policy history and descriptive international
fuel-consumption comparisons. Polls come from different organizations, samples and questions; this is not a
systematic review, a new representative survey or a causal evaluation of tax policy.[^knittel2013]

## Main results

- Gasoline-tax increases were debated by the Nixon, Ford and Carter administrations, advisers and Congress.
  Knittel describes Nixon and Carter supporting proposals and Ford opposing gasoline taxes while favoring oil
  tariffs and price decontrol. Congressional and public opposition were important barriers, not an absence of ideas.
- A November 1974 Harris survey of 1,525 households reported 13% support and 74% opposition to an unqualified
  ten-cent-per-gallon increase. Support reached 51% only when the question combined an income-tax credit with
  ending dependence on Middle East oil. These are hypothetical responses, not demonstrated policy outcomes.
- Other polls generally favored rationing or taxes on inefficient cars over higher gasoline prices. Support for
  rationing was not fixed: Cambridge surveys reported 65% in 1977 and 49% in 1980 when contrasted with $2 gasoline,
  but gasoline prices and question wording also changed. These are not a panel of the same respondents.
- Unadjusted international comparisons show relatively slow U.S./Canadian per-capita road-fuel growth after 1972.
  After accounting for GDP, population density and year/country effects, their growth was nearer the middle of
  the comparison. Knittel cautions that initial vehicle efficiency and income growth complicate policy comparisons;
  this does not isolate the effect of a gasoline tax, CAFE or cycling investment.[^knittel2013]

## Interpretation and limitations

Regressivity and burdens on rural residents and long-distance commuters recur in the historical objections.
These are reported concerns, not measured household tax burdens or evidence that alternatives were equitable.
Knittel argues that support for visibly costly rationing and vehicle taxes challenges an explanation based solely
on hidden regulatory costs. His suggestions about time valuation and distributional concerns are hypotheses;
he explicitly does not establish why consumers preferred these policies and notes contrary time-valuation evidence.

The review does not give a reproducible comprehensive press/poll selection protocol. Poll uncertainty, weighting
and response rates are not consistently supplied. Conditional questions can promise outcomes a modest tax could
not realistically deliver. The paper does not measure mode shift, cycling benefits, current attitudes or present
legal requirements. Its descriptions of policies continuing “today” refer to January 2013.[^knittel2013]
