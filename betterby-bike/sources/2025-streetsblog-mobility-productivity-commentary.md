# Driving, household costs and economic productivity

## Publication and basis

Todd Litman, Streetsblog New York City, June 23, 2025, in the outlet's “Car Harms” series.
The author, founder and executive director of the Victoria Transport Policy Institute, presents his
[Mobility-Productivity Paradox report](https://www.vtpi.org/ITED_paradox.pdf) through charts,
secondary examples, personal experience and policy arguments. That linked report was not independently
reviewed. The 12-page capture contains substantive article material on pp. 1–10, followed by site content.
This is an author-written research commentary, not a new controlled street-redesign evaluation.[^essay]

## Argument and evidence

Litman argues that some driving is highly productive but that automobile dependence encourages trips
whose costs exceed their benefits. His state-level scatterplots show lower GDP per capita with higher
vehicle miles traveled per capita and more urban lane-miles per 1,000 residents, and higher GDP per capita
with greater urban population density. A separate major-US-urban-region plot shows higher GDP per capita
with a higher congestion travel-time index. Captions cite FHWA 2020, USDOT 2024 and TTI 2023; these are
source labels, not documentation of a common observation year or adjusted causal model.[^essay]

The parking plot compares GDP per capita with the percentage of downtown land devoted to parking,
showing a negative fitted relationship with R² = 0.137. An Eugene, Oregon, map illustrates taxable value
per acre, labeled with City of Eugene data for 2017 and credited in the caption to Steuteville 2022.
Neither graphic measures business sales changes after parking removal or a bicycle-lane installation.[^essay]

For households, the essay illustrates about $8,000 annually for an average automobile, comprising
$6,500 for vehicles and fuel and $1,500 for residential parking. Living car-free for under $1,000 a year
is the author's personal experience, not a matched household comparison; currency basis, price year and
transferability are not specified in the article. An external-cost chart compares costs per passenger-mile
across six modes and places gasoline and electric cars above walking, cycling, e-bikes and public transit.
Its categories include subsidies, congestion, pedestrian delay, crashes, pollution and resource externalities;
the linked cost analysis was not reviewed.[^essay]

The author also reports that Victoria, BC's bikeway network displaced about 200 parking spaces while
bike trips rose by 12,390, personal vehicles fell by about 6,000 and car trips fell by 46,120 after completion.
The article does not supply the comparison years, trip-count interval, geography or causal controls.
These reported changes cannot establish that the network caused the reductions or quantify parking demand
at the affected locations.[^essay]

## Interpretation and limits

Litman recommends compact, multimodal neighborhoods and commercial districts, arguing that access,
transport cost savings and environmental quality matter more than maximizing driving convenience.
He proposes about 4,000 annual vehicle-miles per capita as an approximate optimum, higher in affluent
rural/suburban areas and lower in cities and lower-income neighborhoods. This is his interpretation,
not a threshold established by the article's descriptive plots. He explicitly retains productive uses
of motor vehicles, including farming, trades, visiting nurses, freight and emergency services.[^essay]

The article does not provide sample definitions, uncertainty intervals, model controls or a reproducible
method for the asserted optimum. Regional correlations cannot isolate effects of road construction,
parking removal or density policy, and greater congestion in productive regions does not show congestion
causes prosperity. General claims about business sales and shopping frequency summarize linked literature,
not new independent estimates. No separate Finding is extracted from these mixed illustrative claims.

The final marginal-benefit chart area on PDF p. 9 is blank in the capture; its caption and accompanying
argument are readable, but no unseen plotted values are inferred. Other substantive charts and captions
were inspected. Linked research and data remain unreviewed.
