# How Much Faster Are We Moving?

Walter Hook and Annie Weinstock, People-Oriented Cities, Spring 2025. Supported by the New York Community Trust.
[Original PDF](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/87d8897e-0526-4b64-8769-54eb32823548/How-Much-Faster-Are-We-Moving%20-%20Final.pdf).
The publisher identifies itself as an advocacy and technical-assistance organization.

## Study and reporting basis

The report compares bus speeds before and after NYC bus-priority projects, using MTA route-speed data
available from 2015 and earlier NYC DOT project evaluations. Site visits and Google Earth inform treatment
classification and proposed explanations. The principal comparison uses morning-peak, route-end-to-end speeds,
including stops and signals, rather than only treated segments. Link-level MTA speeds were unavailable before
2023; routes commonly extend beyond treatment limits. The report describes 33 corridors and provides a
corridor appendix. A separate camera analysis extends through October 2024; 2025 congestion-pricing effects
are explicitly excluded.[^hook2025]

## Results and interpretation

Table 2 reports a weighted average increase from 7.0 to 7.5 mph (8.0%). Larger gains generally accompany
Select Bus Service (SBS) packages that combine boarding changes and stop consolidation with street treatments.
The 14th Street busway/SBS case reports 22.3%, but is the only case in its category. Other categories contain
mixed or adverse route changes, not uniform benefits. These uncontrolled, differently timed comparisons do
not isolate the causal contribution of lane alignment, boarding or stop removal.[^hook2025]

The authors find no clear speed improvement in their camera-enforcement comparisons and challenge attribution
of earlier TSP gains to signals alone because SBS changes overlap the evaluation periods. Their B35 time
series does not reverse its declining speed trend after TSP installation, but cannot exclude benefits relative
to an unobserved counterfactual. Claims that TSP has no benefit anywhere in NYC go beyond these comparisons.
The proposed full-BRT average of 13.2 mph is a theoretical benchmark using 3 a.m. Google Maps travel times
and 12 seconds per stop, not an observed upgrade result. Recommendations for full BRT, better stations,
turn restrictions and revised Streets Plan targets are the authors' proposals.[^hook2025]

## Access and limitations

Read the complete extracted text of the 52-page PDF, including methods, Table 2, camera/TSP sections,
and appendix pp. 48–49. No external evaluations, underlying MTA data or graphical/photo details were
independently reviewed. Bundle searches for title, authors, bus priority, TSP report and named busways found
no existing representation of this publication; the existing Baku model and GWB report concern different studies.

No controlled comparison, uncertainty intervals or reproducible weighting procedure establishes causal
category differences. Small categories, repeated routes across projects, differing implementation years,
changing ridership/traffic and mixed older evaluations constrain inference. Internal reporting issues remain:
Table 2 says 2008–2023/current 2023 but the appendix includes 2024 projects; offset-plus-SBS text on p. 25
reports 15% versus Table 2's 10.2%; appendix before/after speeds and stated changes do not always reconcile
(notably M86). S79 is grouped with all-door boarding despite lacking it. The executive summary's 8.5 mph
baseline and 9.2 mph graphic are not interchangeable with Table 2's 7.5 mph current average.
These results measure bus speeds, not bicycle-lane effects, total person-delay, safety or equitable access.[^hook2025]
