# Cutting traffic cuts vandalism, burglary and violence, research shows

University of Leeds, June 29, 2026. Read the complete seven-page capture, including substantive
reporting on pp. 2–6, researcher photographs and captions; remaining material is site navigation.

## Reporting and evidence

The university reports research led by Jose Pina-Sánchez and Toby Davies using longitudinal
Understanding Society survey data from tens of thousands of households in England, Scotland and Wales.
It identifies the paper as [Exploring the effect of motor traffic on street crime](https://doi.org/10.18716/ojs/krimoj/2026.2.3),
published in *Kriminologie* on June 29, 2026. The paper itself was not reviewed.[^leeds2026]

The reported result is that increased heavy traffic accompanies increased resident perceptions of
street crime, with a corresponding decline when traffic falls. Residents were followed over time;
interviewers separately assessed whether the nearest road had heavy traffic. The article provides no
survey years, exact analytic sample, effect sizes, uncertainty intervals or model specification.[^leeds2026]

## Interpretation and limitations

Although the headline names vandalism, burglary and violence as if actual incidents were counted,
the article explicitly identifies the study outcome as perceived crime, not police-recorded crime.
Researchers argue that perceptions avoid police underreporting biases and interpret the longitudinal
relationship as causal. Their assertion that traffic was the only relevant change cannot be assessed
from the methods supplied here. Separate interviewer traffic assessments do not by themselves
establish absence of confounding or measurement bias.[^leeds2026]

Weaker community ties and visible disorder are proposed mechanisms, not quantified mediation results
in this capture. Recommendations to consider traffic in residential design and avoid car-dependent
crime-prevention layouts are researcher interpretations, not evaluated design interventions.
A roughly 10% reduction in recorded crime is attributed to earlier London low-traffic-neighbourhood
research, not this nationwide survey. The article's further-information links identify earlier studies;
they were not reviewed and do not constitute additional independent evidence here.[^leeds2026]
