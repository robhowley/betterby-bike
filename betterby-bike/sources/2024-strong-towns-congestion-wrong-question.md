# “Do Bike Lanes Reduce Congestion” Is the Wrong Question

Emma Durand-Wood, Strong Towns, October 3, 2024.
[Captured publication](../../raw-sources/news/do-bike-lanes-reduce-congestion-is-the-wrong-question.pdf).

## Argument and evidence

This commentary argues that access to destinations, safety, public cost and person-carrying capacity are better
reasons to build bicycle infrastructure than a promise of uncongested driving. It quotes a
[Transportation for America report](https://t4america.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Congestion-Report-2020-FINAL.pdf)
on access as transportation's purpose. Durand-Wood describes changes in routes, times and destinations after
road-space reallocation and rejects a simple one-car-for-one-bike substitution story. These are policy arguments
and secondary explanations, not a new travel-behavior or congestion evaluation.[^durand2024]

The article also argues that narrowing rather than removing vehicle lanes can accommodate cycling; that bicycles'
smaller space requirements make apparent lane emptiness misleading; and that infrastructure can encourage future
riding. It does not measure local throughput, latent demand or the cause of underused lanes. Its suggestion that
poor connectivity usually explains low use is not tested here.[^durand2024]

## Secondary examples and qualifications

- A [Johns Hopkins research announcement](https://publichealth.jhu.edu/2023/narrower-lanes-safer-streets) is cited
  for fewer non-intersection crashes on narrower lanes. No lane-width, speed-stratum or effect-size details are
  supplied, so this is not evidence that any narrowing project necessarily improves safety.
- Customer-spending claims cite [research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3141/2468-06) and Rachel
  Quednau's [business roundup](https://www.strongtowns.org/journal/2018/5/31/how-bike-lanes-benefit-businesses).
  No new business sample or local economic estimate is provided.
- Former Copenhagen councilor Morten Kabell's account presents bicycle infrastructure as a low-cost investment
  and describes a walking/cycling bridge paying for itself in under seven years, without accounting details.
  The article's broad claim that 62% of citizens bike to work or school lacks a precise denominator and year.
  The existing [Copenhagen share Finding](/findings/networks/copenhagen-wef-cycling-shares.md) preserves the
  narrower resident-and-work/study population in other coverage; this article should not broaden that measure.
- The author cites Wesley Marshall and Nicholas Ferenchak for an association between cycling activity and safety
  for all road users, then discusses safety in numbers and traffic calming as explanations. The capture supplies
  no model, sample or uncertainty with which to establish those mechanisms or a causal infrastructure effect.[^durand2024]

## Contribution and access limits

Read all eight captured pages. Substantive text ends on p. 7; p. 8 is site footer material. Visually checked p. 4:
the referenced transport-space graphic is linked rather than reproduced as a usable figure. There are no research
tables, methods appendices or original outcome data. Linked reports, studies and live pages were not reviewed.

No new Finding is extracted: the distinct contribution is the evaluation argument, while the supporting examples
are secondary and insufficiently specified for new empirical claims. The
[street-redesign Topic](/topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md#reported-delay-increases-need-a-counterfactual)
places that argument beside reported delays rather than using broader benefits to dismiss adverse outcomes.
