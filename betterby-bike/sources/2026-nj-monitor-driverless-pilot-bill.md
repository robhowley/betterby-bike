# New Jersey's proposed driverless-car pilot

Dana DiFilippo, New Jersey Monitor, May 13, 2026. Read the complete four-page article capture,
including photo captions. This is legislative and hearing reporting, not a pilot evaluation.

## Reported proposal and legislative status

The Senate transportation committee unanimously advanced a bill on May 11 proposing a three-year
AV pilot. At publication, the Assembly version still awaited a committee hearing. Committee approval
was not enactment or authorization for widespread driverless service.[^difilippo]

The proposal would put the Department of Transportation in charge of testing and establish a safety-focused
task force addressing crashes, cyberattacks, operational disruptions, liability and pedestrian protection.
The task force would report monthly and solicit public feedback. A human driver would be required in the
first testing phase; driverless testing could follow completion without incident. This differs from the
2020 task force's recommendations for Motor Vehicle Commission oversight and a person in the vehicle
during testing.[^difilippo]

Reported amendments shortened the testing period from five to three years, required crash reporting,
barred self-driving trucks and vehicles larger than personal cars, and prohibited driverless cars in
school zones, construction zones and high-pedestrian areas. The article also reports a $5 million
per-vehicle insurance requirement.[^difilippo]

## Support and competing access concerns

All nine hearing witnesses supported the bill, including industry, safety and disability representatives;
that is a hearing count, not a representative public-opinion survey. Linda Melendez of the National
Federation of the Blind of New Jersey described potential independence for blind, low-vision and
transit-dependent residents facing ride denials or unreliable transport. SAVE-US representative Shua
Sanchez praised public crash reporting and characterized the proposed rules as nationally strong.
These are supporters' assessments, not demonstrated accessibility or safety effects.[^difilippo]

Support did not imply agreement on every provision. The New Jersey Business & Industry Association
argued that per-vehicle insurance would raise entry costs and suggested a lower amount or fleet-wide
coverage. The New Jersey Chamber of Commerce warned that excluding pedestrian-heavy areas could
exclude urban and mixed-use communities with high demand. The reporting identifies a tension between
restricting testing around pedestrians and providing access where people need it, without measuring
either effect.[^difilippo]

## Evidence limits and related coverage

The bill text and 2020 task-force report were not reviewed. The sponsor's statements about technical
progress and public acceptance do not establish comparative crash risk. The reported 500,000 weekly
paid Waymo rides across several cities is service context, not a safety denominator or New Jersey
pilot result. No cyclist-specific outcome, crash-rate comparison or benefit estimate is provided.
