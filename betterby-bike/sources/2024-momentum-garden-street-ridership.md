# Bicycle use soars following installation of separated bike lanes, according to new study

Ron Johnson, Momentum, October 23, 2024. Read the full PDF text; substantive reporting is on
pp. 2–4. The article links BCU Labs' volunteer-led analysis for the Boston Cyclists Union,
[If you build it, they will bike](https://bostoncyclistsunion.org/if-you-build-it-they-will-bike).
That underlying analysis was identified from the PDF link but not independently reviewed.

## Reporting and evidence

The article describes a half-mile Garden Street redesign in Cambridge, Massachusetts: door-zone
lanes and sharrows were replaced with separated lanes in both directions, while motor traffic became
one-way and most parking was retained. Cambridge's ordinance requiring 25 miles of separated lanes
by 2026 is policy context, not an achieved network total.[^johnson2024]

According to the BCU analysis as reported, within four months of completion, residential streets
within a half-mile radius had a 300% increase in bicycle mode share and over 500% growth in bicycle
volumes. Johnson also describes increased nearby Bluebikes use and a seasonal adjustment under which
the project's ridership impact "nearly quadruples," but supplies neither the adjustment nor a clear
baseline for that phrase. These are repeated accounts of one analysis, not independent confirmations.[^johnson2024]

The article quotes city official Brooke McKenna's pre-installation comfort and safety rationale,
and a Reddit commenter who says the lanes prompted them and acquaintances to get bicycles. It also
reports dismissal of Aster et al. v. City of Cambridge, a challenge to the lane program, linking a
court decision. These are implementation context and selected testimony, not measured safety or
representative adoption evidence; the court document was not reviewed.[^johnson2024]

## Limits and reconciliation

The capture gives no raw counts, baseline shares, precise observation dates, count durations,
uncertainty or reproducible comparison/seasonal-adjustment methods. Its claim of reduced driving
rests on an increased bike/car share, which cannot alone establish fewer car trips. Nor can nearby
counts distinguish new trips from route redistribution or isolate separation from the one-way
conversion. No crash or congestion outcome is measured.

Existing [Boston Globe coverage](/sources/2024-boston-globe-bike-boom.md) concerns different Boston
corridors, including Cambridge Street, not Cambridge's Garden Street. This is a distinct local
result, but not a replication of those count comparisons or proof of citywide growth caused by lanes.
