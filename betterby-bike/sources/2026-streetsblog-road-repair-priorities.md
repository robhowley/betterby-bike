# Congress Gave States Enough Money to Fix Every Road in America; Some States Set It On Fire Instead

## Publication and reporting basis

Kea Wilson's Streetsblog USA article is dated **May 11, 2026**. The supplied capture's substantive
reporting is on PDF pp. 1–6; later pages contain author details, related stories and footer material.
It reports Transportation for America's [Repair Priorities 2026](https://t4america.org/2026/04/29/new-release-repair-priorities-2026/)
and interviews senior policy associate Mehr Mukhtar. The underlying report was not independently read.
The p. 2 chart was inspected visually; the interactive state-ranking area on p. 4 is blank in the capture.
This is secondary analysis coverage and advocacy, not a controlled policy evaluation.[^wilson-repair2026]

## What it reports

- In 2024, 16.3% of federal-aid-eligible roads were rated poor, alongside $56.8 billion in federal
  transportation funds. An estimated $43.2 billion annual maintenance need is compared with that funding;
  the article does not supply the cost model or demonstrate that all funds could serve the same assets.
- Twenty-four states reportedly spent less than $2.35 on maintenance per dollar of expansion despite
  worse-than-national-average road conditions. Classification and reporting weaknesses can conceal
  expansion within maintenance or unspecified spending.
- North Dakota, South Dakota, Minnesota and Vermont receive favorable repair-to-expansion comparisons.
  Wilson notes rural traffic differences; Mukhtar credits stronger performers with a three-percentage-point
  national improvement between 2018 and 2024. This does not isolate a spending-policy effect.
- The report is said to estimate $47,300 annual maintenance per new highway lane-mile and 119,257
  lane-miles added over its six-year analysis. These are reported estimates, not reviewed cost accounts.
  Mukhtar warns that delaying repairs until reconstruction, illustrated with Michigan, raises costs.
- With the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act due to expire September 30, Mukhtar advocates
  enforceable repair priorities, backlog targets and improved spending/condition reporting. Wilson's
  broader claims about induced traffic, deaths, emissions and access are contextual arguments rather
  than outcomes measured by this article.[^wilson-repair2026]

## Limits and connections

The headline's claim about fixing every road and bridge is stronger than the documented comparison:
annual maintenance needs, existing repair backlogs, bridge costs and broadly allocated federal dollars
are not interchangeable. Inflation, geography, traffic and classification differences limit comparisons.
The chart cites FHWA Highway Statistics (2009–2024), tables FA-4, HM-64 and HM-63, plus 2009 recovery-act
implementation guidance; its dual-axis trends do not establish causation. No cycling or redesign effect
is evaluated.[^wilson-repair2026]

The [road-condition and funding Finding](/findings/us-road-repair-spending-reported.md) preserves the
bounded comparison. It complements [NPR's pavement-damage interviews](/sources/2026-npr-climate-road-damage.md),
which do not quantify national spending needs, and informs
[street-redesign evaluation](/topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md#design-guidance-is-not-an-outcome-evaluation).
