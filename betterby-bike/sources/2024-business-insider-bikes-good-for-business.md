# Bike lanes are good for business

Adam Rogers, Business Insider, March 7, 2024, Discourse article.
[Original article](https://www.businessinsider.com/bike-lanes-good-for-business-studies-better-streets-2024-3).

## Reporting basis

Rogers examines merchant opposition to parking removal and street redesign. He says he read 32 research articles
since 1984, then emphasizes roughly six studies using financial records and comparison areas rather than surveys.
This is a reported argument, not a reproducible systematic review or pooled estimate. Interviews with researcher
Joseph Poirier, Portland State's Jenny Liu and consultant Larisa Ortiz add interpretations about business vulnerability,
street context and adaptation.[^rogers2024]

## Evidence and overlap

- A [2012 Los Angeles study](https://nacto.org/docs/usdg/yorkblvd_mccormick.pdf) supplies a merchant/customer
  arrival-mode mismatch: more than half of merchants on the bike-laned section thought most customers drove,
  whereas the reported customer driver share was 15%. This adds a survey outcome to the existing
  [York business account](/findings/business/york-road-diet-business-results.md), not a sales effect.
- [Rowe's Seattle study](https://bikewalkkc.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Bikenomics_v4.pdf) repeats the existing
  [Greenwood](/findings/business/seattle-greenwood-retail-sales.md) and
  [65th Street](/findings/business/seattle-65th-street-retail-sales.md) cases. Rogers says sales quadrupled in the
  latter, but supplies no new series to resolve earlier percentage/index ambiguity. Rowe explicitly declines to
  connect mode choice to economic performance.
- The linked [NYC economic report](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/dot-economic-benefits-of-sustainable-streets.pdf)
  reportedly covers seven areas with combined pedestrian, transit, traffic-calming, landscaping and cycling changes.
  Rogers reports sales outperformance of 84 percentage points in Brooklyn, 9 in Manhattan and 32 in the Bronx
  relative to borough trends, without enough detail to reconstruct these comparisons. This report overlaps the
  basis of the [existing NYC retail comparisons](/findings/business/nyc-pbl-retail-comparisons.md); the article's
  chronology places it a year after 2013, while the existing NYC Source identifies the economic report as 2013.
- [Poirier's 2018 study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0361198118792321) adds mixed results across three San Francisco
  retail neighborhoods, with differences by local versus wider customer base and a sales-per-employee decline.
- A [2019 San Francisco study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0361198119850465) adds median sales declines for painted-lane,
  signed shared-street and non-bike-lane businesses, alongside differing sector and business-age patterns.
  Neither account establishes a uniform gain. The 2019 treatments are not protected lanes; the article does not
  specify the 2018 lane designs.
- Rogers's account of [Liu and Shi's 2020 report](https://archives.pdx.edu/ds/psu/33394) describes sales and employment
  analyses in four cities and interviews Liu about high-speed/high-volume streets remaining uninviting after
  improvements. This overlaps the bundle's [six-city summary](/sources/2020-economic-impacts-street-improvements.md),
  not an independent replication. Its favorable overall narrative and broad food-versus-retail contrast do not
  replace the summary's more varied corridor results.[^rogers2024]

## Interpretation and limits

Rogers argues that better streets generally help businesses, but acknowledges losses among individual firms,
particularly older businesses or those selling heavy goods or drawing customers from farther away. Ortiz's adaptation
account and Liu's street-context explanation are interpretations, not tested mechanisms. Rogers proposes compensating
adjacent businesses for sales losses through project budgets; this is an unevaluated policy proposal. Poirier's account
of opposition fading after six months or a year does not establish universal merchant satisfaction.[^rogers2024]

Read all 13 supplied PDF pages as text and recovered embedded study links. Underlying linked studies were not separately
reviewed for this ingestion. The article omits sample sizes, uncertainty and important comparison details for several
results. Its historical pedestrianization narrative and contextual network-mileage/ridership claims are not extracted
as independently established effects. Photos supply no quantitative evidence. Repeated research is linked above rather
than counted as new confirmation; the NYC dating discrepancy remains unresolved.
