# Queensboro Bridge pedestrian-path construction reporting

Kevin Duggan, Streetsblog NYC, July 30, 2024. Read the complete captured article, PDF pp. 1–4,
including the fencing photo caption; pp. 5–6 contain site material. The embedded video is unavailable
in the capture. The [publication PDF](../../raw-sources/news/dot-starts-work-to-open-queensboro-roadway-to-pedestrians-streetsblog-new-york-city.pdf)
is the reviewed source.

## Work begun, not a completed conversion

The article reports installation of suicide-prevention fencing on parts of the Queensboro Bridge's
South Outer Roadway, an initial step toward converting a car lane into pedestrian space and separating
walking from cycling. It describes the project as seven years after DOT first revealed the plan and
two years behind schedule. DOT had promised a late-summer opening, but the article does not establish
that the path opened.[^duggan2024queensboro]

According to the reporting, DOT delayed conversion to retain the roadway for drivers during upper-deck
rehabilitation. Fencing and approach infrastructure were prerequisites: a concrete pedestrian landing
in Long Island City, plus crosswalks and sidewalk extensions connecting to Second Avenue in Manhattan.
Some approach work could continue after opening. Four other Queens-bound driving lanes would remain;
the South Outer Roadway had already been closed to cars overnight for a decade following speeding
and off-ramp crashes.[^duggan2024queensboro]

## Reporting basis and limitations

The article cites DOT's April community-board presentation for nearly 10,000 daily cyclists and
pedestrians, compared with fewer than 8,200 daily drivers on the South Outer Roadway. It separately
describes nearly 3,000 daily pedestrians and 7,000 cyclists. These are reported daily volumes, not
unique users, a comparison with all bridge driving traffic, or a measure of passenger throughput.
Counting dates, methods and uncertainty are not supplied.[^duggan2024queensboro]

Transportation Alternatives organizer Laura Shepard describes recurring shared-path crashes and argues
that more space would prevent injuries. Council Member Julie Won welcomes construction but questions
whether DOT will meet its deadline. The article also cites a recent social-media crash account and
characterizes delays as prioritizing motorists. DOT declined comment. These are implementation reporting,
advocacy and anecdotal injury accounts, not systematic crash surveillance or a before/after evaluation.
The article's claim about avoiding 5% more car congestion lacks supporting methods or a defined metric
in the capture and is not an observed conversion effect.[^duggan2024queensboro]

No new Finding is extracted. The article adds a dated delivery milestone, not measured conflict reduction,
new cycling, completed pedestrian access or a transferable lane-capacity comparison. Linked agency
materials, crash accounts and subsequent implementation were not independently reviewed. The existing
[Brooklyn Bridge count announcement](2021-nyc-dot-brooklyn-bridge-ridership.md) concerns a different bridge
and provides post-conversion counts; it does not establish outcomes for this planned conversion.
