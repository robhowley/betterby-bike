# Upper West Side Smart Curbs implementation reporting

Kevin Duggan, Streetsblog NYC, October 29, 2024.
[Original article](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2024/10/29/what-happened-to-the-metered-parking-promise-in-dots-upper-west-side-smart-curbs).
Read the complete captured article, PDF pp. 1–6, including the pilot map and photo captions; later pages are site material.

## Reporting and plans

The article examines NYC DOT's phased Smart Curbs pilot in a 14-block Upper West Side area, shown between
West 72nd and West 86th streets on the map. It draws on agency announcements, a June presentation,
spokespeople's clarifications, local advocate Carl Mahaney and parking researcher Donald Shoup.
StreetopiaUWS, which Mahaney directs, shares a parent company with Streetsblog. The capture includes an
editor's note that DOT supplied additional information after initial publication.[^duggan2024curbs]

The important distinction is between converting free parking and reassigning existing paid spaces.
DOT said about 200 free spaces would become metered passenger parking, while 120 existing paid passenger
spaces would become commercial-only, leaving 80 net additional metered passenger spaces in 2025.
For 2024, 100 existing Columbus Avenue metered spaces were to become commercial spaces during the day,
with additional changes on Broadway and Amsterdam Avenue in 2025. These categories and phases should
not be added together as independent new parking spaces.[^duggan2024curbs]

The article also reports a plan for 175 fewer car-storage spaces out of an estimated 1,700 in the pilot
area, including space for 27 loading zones, 30 bike racks and one street seat. Six truck loading zones
would allow metered passenger parking in the evening and free parking overnight. The article does not
supply a complete space-by-space reconciliation between removals, conversions and time-dependent uses.
At least one of three proposed local delivery microhubs had been expected before winter but was delayed
until 2025; DOT attributed the delay to unfinished regulations. The hubs were intended to provide package
staging space and reduce illegal delivery parking, not reported as already achieving that result.[^duggan2024curbs]

## Advocacy and evidence limits

Mahaney argued that retaining substantial free parking weakened the pilot's ability to test pricing at
scale. Shoup proposed directing some new meter revenue to neighborhood improvements or transit passes
to build support. DOT previously said meter revenue legally goes to the citywide general fund and raised
fairness concerns. These are attributed policy arguments, not an independent legal analysis or measured
public-support effects. Shoup's claim that market pricing would eliminate cruising traffic is not tested
in the article.[^duggan2024curbs]

This is original implementation reporting, not a before/after evaluation. It supplies no measured parking
occupancy, delivery obstruction, cycling uptake, crashes, revenue or cruising reduction. The map defines
the proposed area; the street-seat construction photo does not establish completion of the full program.
Linked agency documents were not independently reviewed, and subsequent implementation is unverified.

The existing [Open Plans curb-management report](2023-curb-the-chaos-solutions-for-cities-at-the-curb.md)
provides broader policy cases and separate loading-zone observations. This article adds a dated local
implementation and accounting example, not independent confirmation of those outcomes. No new Finding
is extracted from the proposals and interviews.
