# Commuting in the United States: 2022

## Publication and access

## What it measures

The brief compares 2019, 2021 and 2022 ACS 1-year estimates for workers aged 16 and older. The ACS samples about
3.5 million addresses annually across the United States and Puerto Rico, including group quarters. National
figures exclude Puerto Rico, which has a separate section using the Puerto Rico Community Survey. Respondents
select their usual way to work in the previous week, using the method used most days if it varies by day.
2020 is omitted because pandemic nonresponse prevented release of standard 1-year estimates; the three included
years used consistent weighting methods.[^census2024]

## Results and interpretation

Combined bicycling/walking represented **3.2%, 2.6% and 2.9%** of workers in 2019, 2021 and 2022, respectively.
Transit shares were 5.0%, 2.5% and 3.1%; driving alone was 75.9%, 67.8% and 68.7%; home-based work was 5.7%,
17.9% and 15.2%. Thus 2022 was a partial rebound, not a return to prepandemic commuting patterns. These are
worker shares, including home-based workers in the denominator, not shares of all trips or a bicycle-only
series. The brief also reports carpooling recovery and no significant change since 2019 in the taxicab,
motorcycle or other-means category.[^census2024]

Transit commuter counts in all seven examined transit-heavy metros rose from 2021 but remained below 2019.
Mean one-way commute time among workers not working from home was 26.4 minutes in 2022, versus 25.6 in 2021
and 27.6 in 2019 (Table 1 reports ±0.1 minute margins of error for each national estimate). Morning departures
from 6–8:59 a.m. rose to 83.3 million in 2022 from 77.0 million in 2021, below 92.1 million in 2019. The authors
interpret persistent home-based work as a likely contributor, not an independently identified causal effect.[^census2024]

## Limitations

The combined walking/bicycling category cannot establish bicycle-only change. Usual-mode reporting misses
less-frequent home working and does not measure all hybrid schedules or all-purpose active travel. Changes in
worker composition and working from home matter when interpreting shares; no street-treatment effect is
estimated. Survey estimates have sampling and nonsampling error. The report says comparative statements are
significant at 90% confidence unless noted otherwise; Figure 2 does not display mode-share margins of error.
Metro definitions differ between 2019 and later years. Puerto Rico's separate results should not be pooled
with the national series. Its summary mentions increased solo-driver counts while the earlier discussion
reports a reduced share; counts and shares are not interchangeable.[^census2024]
