# Bike lanes have actually sped up car traffic in New York City

Joseph Stromberg, Vox, September 8, 2014.
[Original article](https://www.vox.com/2014/9/8/6121129/bike-lanes-traffic-new-york).

## Reporting basis and main claims

The article explains why Manhattan protected-lane redesigns need not delay drivers. It credits Eric Jaffe at CityLab
for drawing attention to [NYCDOT's September 2014 report](/sources/2014-nyc-protected-bicycle-lanes.md), reproduces
agency graphics and interprets their results. It presents no new traffic measurements or original interviews.[^vox]

- Vox reports 14% shorter Eighth Avenue travel times (23rd–34th Streets) and 35% shorter Columbus Avenue morning
  travel times (77th–96th). It also reports a small First Avenue taxi-speed decline, from just over 13 to just over
  12 mph, and roughly unchanged central-business-district taxi speeds. These are different measures and windows,
  not a citywide causal effect.
- Stromberg proposes narrower driving lanes and left-turn pockets as explanations, rather than a substantial
  reduction in car volumes. His Eighth Avenue example retains four full-time driving lanes but removes a rush-hour
  driving lane. These are design interpretations, not separately tested mechanisms.
- Vox reports 17% fewer injury crashes, larger declines in pedestrian and vehicle-occupant injuries, and nearly
  unchanged cyclist injury counts despite increased cycling. It describes reduced cyclist risk on seven of eight
  corridors; the reproduced table also shows an 11.2% increase on Broadway (18th–23rd) and only a 2.4% decline on
  Eighth Avenue. Its safety-in-numbers explanation is a hypothesis, not an additional study result.[^vox]

## Qualifications from the underlying report

The primary report was also read for this ingestion. Its existing Findings remain the evidence record; this article
is not an independent confirmation. Several compressions in Vox should not be carried into quantitative claims:

- **Geography and traffic:** Vox places the CBD boundary at 86th Street; NYCDOT's taxi measure runs from Bowling
  Green to 59th Street. NYCDOT describes First Avenue speeds as level and shows separate segments, rather than
  supplying Vox's single avenue-wide speed comparison. Short, seasonally different travel-time samples and taxi-only
  measures do not establish that lanes alone sped traffic or never caused delay.[^nyc2014]
- **Risk definition:** Vox introduces the citywide killed/severely-injured indicator before interpreting the corridor
  table as death/serious-injury risk. NYCDOT's corridor table instead normalizes cyclist injuries by representative
  12-hour facility counts. The separate citywide indicator uses an indexed six-location count denominator, not all
  cyclists on the street. Neither is an absolute per-trip risk.[^nyc2014]
- **Design and crash scope:** Vox's explanation emphasizes retained driving lanes and separate turn signals, but
  NYCDOT documents both lane removal and narrowing, and both split-phase signals and mixing zones. Its aggregate
  injury-crash chart is not restricted to crashes involving bicycles, despite Vox's bicycle-centered description.
  The evaluation does not isolate protected lanes, turn pockets or signals from the combined redesign.[^nyc2014]

## Access and existing Findings

All 15 printout pages were read as extracted text; article content ends on p. 11. The travel-time chart (p. 3),
Eighth Avenue cross-section (p. 5), injury chart (p. 9) and corridor-risk table (p. 10) were checked visually.
The local 31-page NYCDOT report was read as text. No live article, intervening CityLab coverage or underlying
traffic/crash datasets were independently reviewed. No new Finding is needed for this repeated evidence.
