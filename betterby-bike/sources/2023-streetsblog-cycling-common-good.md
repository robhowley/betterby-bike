# Academics Conclude What We Already Know, Cyclists Are More Neighborly than Drivers

## Reporting and evidence

Gersh Kuntzman's October 31, 2023 Streetsblog NYC article covers German research on transport use and
orientation toward the common good. All substantive article text on PDF pp. 1–4 was read. It links
[“Orientation Towards the Common Good in Cities”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272494423001731),
by Harald Schuster, Jolanda van der Noll and Anette Rohmann in the *Journal of Environmental Psychology*.
The underlying study was not independently reviewed.[^article]

The article describes analysis of German residents' social-organization involvement and political activities,
including volunteering, political discussion and boycotting products. It reports small positive, statistically
significant relationships between bicycle-use frequency over time and social-organizational and political
participation. It also reports positive relationships with local helpfulness and neighborhood solidarity.
Car-use frequency reportedly had no significant effect in those models, not a demonstrated negative effect.[^article]

## Interpretation and limits

The researchers propose direct contact with surroundings, neighborhood attachment and trust as explanations.
These are proposed mechanisms, not demonstrated mediation in this account. Kuntzman's claims that cyclists
are “better people” and have “better ethics” are advocacy framing, not measured moral rankings. The embedded
road-rage example supplies no comparative population evidence.[^article]

The article gives no sample size, observation dates, recruitment details, model covariates, effect coefficients
or uncertainty intervals. It cannot establish that switching from driving to cycling causes civic engagement,
or separate residential and personal selection from transport effects. Its quoted funding declaration says
no specific grant supported the research; that does not resolve these methodological limits.[^article]
