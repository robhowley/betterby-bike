# Could delivery robots help pay for better city sidewalks?

Maylin Tu, Next City, May 18, 2026.
[Original article capture](../../raw-sources/news/could-delivery-robots-help-pay-for-better-city-sidewalks.pdf).
The full capture was read: substantive reporting on PDF pp. 1–4, followed by author information,
related stories and site material. Reporting received Solutions Journalism Network support.

## Reporting

Interviews with city staff, Cityfi and robot operators describe competing sidewalk uses, accessibility
barriers and emerging data-sharing and funding arrangements. Companies frame robots as alternatives to
motor-vehicle delivery; critics describe blocked pedestrian and wheelchair access. Santa Monica's program
manager reports infrequent complaints with 50–100 Coco robots, but supplies no complaint count or exposure
denominator. This is not a measured comparison of delivery-mode safety or accessibility.[^article]

West Hollywood's revised pilot agreement includes annual fees, penalties for ADA and geofencing violations,
and advertising revenue sharing of **$4 per device per day** from Serve and Coco. Advertising revenue,
fines and program fees are earmarked for accessibility improvements. A December report projects
**$40,000–$80,000 annually**; the model was only three months old and receipts were not yet established.[^article]

Coco describes a BlindSquare partnership sharing temporary obstruction information, live in Los Angeles,
Chicago, Miami and Jersey City after starting in Helsinki. Its claim that data are fully anonymized is not
independently audited here. Robot.com reports roughly 35,000 sidewalk data points collected the previous
year and obstruction mapping in D.C. and Arlington; these are not 35,000 repairs or unique hazards.[^article]

The article reports little evidence that cities use these data for accessibility improvements, with one
concrete exception: Coco identified three missing curb ramps on Abbot Kinney Boulevard in Venice, Los
Angeles, and approached the council district office, which coordinated installation with city departments.
The routing-based account identifies a repair pathway, not a measured change in wheelchair access.
Traffic-signal coordination and mobile air-quality sensing are prospective applications, not evaluated benefits.[^article]

## Limits and relationship to existing coverage

This is original interview reporting, not a controlled evaluation. The December revenue report, operating
agreements and accessibility tools were not independently reviewed. Neither collected revenue nor repair
costs, injury rates, emissions changes or user-access outcomes are measured. Photographs illustrate robot
advertising, not its financial performance.

The [NJ Bike/Ped commentary](2026-nj-bikeped-delivery-robots.md) covers a Jersey City injury and a separate
Hoboken mapping pilot. This article adds named funding arrangements and the Venice repair account rather
than independent confirmation of robot safety. Its reported data-sharing activity also qualifies any broad
suggestion that ordinary delivery operators never share sidewalk data; collection still does not demonstrate
systematic repairs or improved access.
