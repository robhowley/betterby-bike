# An Interstate Highway Program, but for Sidewalks?

Angie Schmitt, Strong Towns, September 2, 2026. [Captured publication](../../raw-sources/news/an-interstate-highway-program-but-for-sidewalks-strong-towns.pdf).
All substantive article text on PDF pp. 1–8 was read. Linked reporting, government material and Michael
Pollack's book *Sidewalk Nation* were not reviewed. The exact original article URL was not recovered.

## Reporting and argument

Schmitt uses a visit to Wheeling, West Virginia, and her Columbus, Ohio, family connections to argue
that transportation funding should respond to local access needs rather than continued highway expansion.
This is commentary with personal observations, interviews and secondary reporting, not a study.[^schmitt2026]

- In Wheeling, older women told her transit supported their independence. She reports that retail growth
  near highway exits and social-service agencies on the Ohio side sit beyond bus routes without connecting
  sidewalks, leaving providers to arrange expensive transportation. No sample or cost estimates are supplied.
- Citing the Columbus Dispatch, she reports that only 45% of Columbus streets have sidewalks. Dean Jehorek,
  whose daughter Anastasia was killed while riding an e-scooter to work, describes discontinuous sidewalks.
  The article gives no coverage inventory method or date and does not establish the crash's causal factors.
- She contrasts these gaps with a reported $1.4 billion I-70/I-71 interchange project. Her rough estimate
  that this sum could fund 5,000 sidewalk miles supplies no costing method and is not a feasible project budget.
- Pollack advocates predictable sidewalk funding instead of small competitive grants and costs shifted to
  property owners. Schmitt's proposed highway-program-scale sidewalk investment is an argument, not an
  evaluated intervention or a quantified health-benefit comparison.[^schmitt2026]

## Limits and reconciliation

The article also reports roughly 140 annual Ohio pedestrian deaths and a national claim that about three
quarters of pedestrian deaths occur on roads without sidewalks. It supplies neither exposure denominators
nor sufficient methods to treat these as sidewalk risk ratios or preventable casualties. Its historical
transport narrative and claims about highway funding formulas are author framing, not independently
verified funding accounts. No cycling, injury, health or cost-saving effect is measured.

[Existing older-adult coverage](2026-streetsblog-aging-transportation.md) reports national transit use;
Wheeling adds local accounts of inaccessible destinations, not another prevalence estimate or independent
confirmation of that study. The [USDOT highway-finance source](2024-usdot-highways-bridges-transit-25th-report.md)
uses 2018 administrative accounts; Schmitt's funding critique does not update those figures or demonstrate
how much highway spending could legally or practically be redirected.
