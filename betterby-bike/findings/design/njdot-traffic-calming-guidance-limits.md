# NJDOT traffic-calming guidance and speed-effect limits

## In brief

- NJDOT's supplied 2015-titled manual says bicycle lanes may calm traffic by narrowing or removing motor-vehicle lanes;
  this is design guidance, not a measured effect from a reported study.
- It says mid-block curb extensions have not proven to slow traffic, and colored/textured pavement, transverse rumble
  markings and forced-perspective striping do not measurably affect speed on their own. The chapter supplies no
  study-level data to establish universal null effects.[^manual]

## Treatment and purpose matter

Section 15.3.4 separates crossing and visual-cue purposes from speed control. It recommends considering curb
extensions at existing or planned mid-block crosswalks, despite not recommending them specifically to slow traffic.
For colored/textured pavement it recommends combination with other treatments; rumble markings are for alerting or
warning motorists, and forced perspective should precede and accompany other treatments.[^manual]

The same section says colored bicycle lanes *may* enhance calming from lane narrowing or removal. Preserve that
qualified statement alongside the warning about color/texture alone rather than converting either into a quantified
paint effect. The chapter does not identify supporting samples, speed differences, comparison groups or precision.
Its minimum 10-foot lane allowance is specific to traffic-calming areas, with cyclist provision also required; it is
not a universal lane-width rule.[^manual]

## Interpretation

This historical chapter bears BDC15MR-01 within a later-revised file. Current applicability was not checked.
Its recommendations are not independent confirmation of the [Asbury Park observed speed comparisons](asbury-park-directional-speed-comparisons.md),
which concern particular movements, temporary treatments and measured vehicle speeds. Nor do these statements
measure crashes, rider comfort or corridor travel delay.
