# Youth-focused street design is guidance, not a measured network benefit

## In brief

- PBIC's 2024 U.S. planning brief recommends separating children from motor traffic, simplifying crossings and
  lowering speeds to accommodate developing judgment, shorter sight lines and vulnerability to crash forces.
  It does not evaluate a completed network or estimate youth-specific benefits of each treatment.[^brief]
- The recommendations retain accessible sidewalks/crossings, attention to at-grade path crossings and responsible-adult
  accompaniment for young children. Engineering provision alone is not evidence of safe independent travel or
  measured equitable access.[^brief]

## How the recommendations address children's needs

The authors describe variation in children's development, difficulty consistently choosing safe vehicle gaps below
age 14, younger children's delay in acting on a crossing decision, and older youths' limited experience with
unfamiliar roads. These are summaries of cited developmental research, not a universal age threshold for independent
travel or original tests conducted for this brief.[^brief]

Their countermeasure framework separates users in space and time, physically slows traffic, enforces speed limits
and improves driver awareness. It recommends:

- Sidewalks wide enough for children and accompanying adults to walk side by side, with more space near gathering
  places and compliance with accessibility guidelines.
- Curb extensions to shorten crossings and improve visibility; crossing islands to let pedestrians assess one
  traffic direction at a time. Crossings and curb ramps should meet accessibility requirements.
- Shared-use paths and connectors that avoid busy roads, while addressing at-grade crossings with measures such
  as curb extensions and islands. Path separation does not remove all vehicle interactions.
- Turn restrictions/protected phases, smaller corner radii, road diets, narrower lanes and raised crosswalks to
  reduce conflicts, crossing distance or speed, according to the treatment.
- Daylighting, crossing beacons and lighting to improve visibility or yielding, including school and after-school
  travel in low light.[^brief]

## Limits on using this guidance as evidence

The brief gives design reasoning, illustrations and secondary research examples, not comparative tests of these
options for children or users with disabilities. It supplies no pooled effect, implementation audit, equity outcome
or exposure-based youth risk. The separate camera-program percentages describe speeding measures in specific
jurisdictions, not effects of curb extensions, road diets or bicycle lanes. Its statement that education alone is
insufficient is the authors' Safe System argument, not a head-to-head trial of education versus engineering.[^brief]

The authors explicitly say young children still need responsible-adult accompaniment. Their nonbinding guidance
does not establish current legal requirements. School Streets are recommended, but the citation is numbered 42
while the reference list ends at 41, leaving that attribution unresolved.

[Philadelphia's reported youth crash distribution](/findings/safety/philadelphia-youth-crash-network-coverage.md)
adds a separate targeting concern: all-user crash priorities may omit many youth injury locations. Neither finding
is a measured benefit from building a youth-oriented network. See
[Evaluating street redesign outcomes](/topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md).
