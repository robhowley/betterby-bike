# CityLab reported less sidewalk riding alongside more cycling on six selected corridors

Eric Jaffe's August 2014 CityLab article reports that sidewalk riding fell after bicycle lanes were created on six
corridors in Washington, D.C., Denver and New York, while total cycling rose. It draws on Washington Post reporting
and a PeopleForBikes/America Walks compilation, whose chart describes protected lanes. The underlying observations
were not separately reviewed.[^citylab2014]

| Corridor as named in the article | Reported sidewalk-riding decrease | Reported total bicycle-use increase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15th Avenue, Washington, D.C. | 70% | Increase, exact value not stated in prose |
| Pennsylvania Avenue, Washington, D.C. | 52% | Increase, exact value not stated in prose |
| L Street, Washington, D.C. | 27% | Increase, exact value not stated in prose |
| 15th Street, Denver | 61% | 12% |
| Columbus Avenue, Manhattan | 46% | 56% |
| Prospect Park West, Brooklyn | 81% | 190% |

These are reported percentage changes, **not percentage-point changes or an average treatment effect**. The table
retains the article's D.C. “15th Avenue” label rather than silently correcting it. Observation periods and baseline
counts are not supplied.[^citylab2014]

## The sidewalk denominator is unresolved

For D.C., the prose says the **share of cyclists** on sidewalks declined; the chart's legend instead says change in
**number of bikes on sidewalk** for all six corridors. The other cities' prose uses less specific sidewalk-use
wording. A falling share does not necessarily mean fewer sidewalk riders when total cycling rises. Preserve these
as reported reductions without choosing a denominator until the underlying observations resolve the discrepancy.
The D.C. total-use bars have no numerical labels, so exact increases were not inferred.[^citylab2014]

## What the comparison supports

The reported pattern is consistent with riders moving from sidewalks to lanes, but it does not track individual
riders or establish that mechanism. Jaffe explicitly calls the sample small and hand-picked by advocates. Missing
collection methods, dates, controls and uncertainty prevent estimating typical effects or ruling out other changes.
Corridor growth may reflect route diversion rather than new citywide cycling. These selected cases are not a pooled
estimate or evidence that every lane design eliminates sidewalk riding.[^citylab2014]

No pedestrian injuries, conflicts or exposure-based safety rates are reported. Jaffe's preference for infrastructure
over enforcement is not a measured comparison of their safety or cost-effectiveness.
