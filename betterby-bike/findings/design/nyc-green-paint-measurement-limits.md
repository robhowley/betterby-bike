# NYC's green-paint evaluation cannot isolate safety or ridership effects

NYCDOT's 2011 evaluation compared behavior on green and unpainted lanes during September 2010 afternoon peak periods. It did not estimate a treatment-specific collision or injury rate, and explicitly could not separate paint's influence on cycling from wider network expansion and citywide growth.[^nyc2011]

The report cites a 58% increase in Clinton Street, Brooklyn, cycling counts between September 2007 and September 2010. Its footnotes say the earlier count was one block from the later count at unpainted location 8. This is not a matched same-location before-and-after estimate of green paint's effect. Likewise, citywide commuter-cycling growth and declining injuries, presented in the conclusion and appendix, cannot establish which change resulted from paint.[^nyc2011]

## Internal reporting discrepancies

- Methods describe four green sites and three controls; Charts 1 and 2 list five green sites and three controls.
- Chart 2's five green rows sum to 800 cyclists and 4,012 cars, but “All Green” reports 623 cyclists and 3,027 cars. Those totals match the first four rows, excluding Bleecker Street's 177 cyclists and 985 cars. Which sites contribute to each aggregate percentage is not explained.
- Chart 2 reports the unpainted aggregate lane as clear 93% and obstructed 13% of the time. The unpainted Clinton Street row reports 83% clear and 13% obstructed. Neither pair sums to 100%, beyond an ordinary rounding discrepancy.
- Findings and Chart 2 give 96% clear for green lanes and 93% for controls, whereas Conclusions give a 93–95% range.[^nyc2011]

These discrepancies are in the PDF, not just text extraction. The row sums above are arithmetic checks, not replacement estimates. Raw event counts for each behavior and the clear-lane measure are unavailable, so the published aggregates should not be silently repaired or used to calculate precise pooled effects.

## What remains usable

The report provides [descriptive behavior comparisons](/findings/design/nyc-green-paint-driver-behavior.md), including mixed site-level results and comparable standing/parking. They suggest a possible compliance benefit, but unmatched lane layouts, traffic differences and no reported uncertainty estimates limit causal interpretation. The authors' broader claim that paint contributed to cycling growth and safety is an interpretation, not an isolated measured effect.[^nyc2011]

[Interpreting cycling injury risk](/topics/cycling-injury-risk.md) places these behavior measures alongside studies that actually count injuries.
