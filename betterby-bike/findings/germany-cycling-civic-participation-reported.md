# German cycling frequency was associated with civic participation in Streetsblog's study coverage

## In brief

- Streetsblog's October 2023 account of German residents reports **small positive, statistically significant**
  relationships between cycling frequency over time and social-organizational and political participation.[^article]
- More frequent cycling also reportedly related positively to local helpfulness and neighborhood solidarity;
  car-use frequency had **no significant effect** in the reported models, not a demonstrated harmful effect.[^article]
- The article supplies neither effect sizes nor sufficient methods to establish that cycling caused these
  differences. The underlying study was not independently reviewed.[^article]

## What was reported

The article identifies Schuster, van der Noll and Rohmann's *Orientation Towards the Common Good in Cities*,
published in the *Journal of Environmental Psychology*. Activities discussed include involvement with schools,
churches, arts centers and clubs, charitable or political organizations, volunteering, boycotts, political
conversation and letters to editors. These participation measures are not direct measures of moral character.[^article]

The authors suggest that cyclists' contact with their surroundings could support trust and awareness of
neighbors' needs. That explanation is not a tested causal pathway in the material reviewed here. The article's
“better people” framing goes beyond its reported evidence.[^article]

## Limits and relationship to access

Sample size, observation years, recruitment, covariate adjustment, coefficients and confidence intervals are
absent. The report cannot rule out selection by prior engagement or residential circumstances, establish a
numerical cycling-versus-driving effect, or show that a bicycle-lane intervention increases participation.
Null car-use results do not prove the absence of any relationship.[^article]

[Active commuting and access](../topics/active-commuting-and-access.md#social-participation-is-a-separate-outcome)
places this social outcome alongside, but not in place of, evidence about who can use active transport.
