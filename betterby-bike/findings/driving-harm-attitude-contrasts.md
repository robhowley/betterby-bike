# Reported driving-harm attitude contrasts

## In brief

- Streetsblog's September 2024 coverage reports that 75% of US survey respondents accepted serious
  injury as a natural risk of driving, versus 34% for working; British figures were 61% and 31%.
- Acceptance was not universal: 55% of US respondents rejected simply accepting driving's consequences
  because reducing driving is supposedly pointless, versus 31% in Britain.
- These are reported survey responses, not observed behavior or measured street-policy support;
  sample sizes, recruitment and uncertainty are absent from the article.[^article]

## Comparisons and interpretation

The first comparison combines “somewhat” and “strongly” agreeing with matched statements about
accepting possible serious injury. Tara Goddard's US research replicated a 2023 British study;
these percentages describe respondents to each statement, not the percentage of residents with a
clinical or individually measured condition called “car brain.” The article does not establish whether
the same people answered both versions.[^article]

For fumes, 94% of US and 75% of British respondents reportedly endorsed smokers keeping emissions
away from populated places, compared with approximately one-fifth in each country for drivers.
For the statement about simply accepting consequences, disagreement was greater for alcohol than
for driving: 70% versus 55% in the US, and 52% versus 31% in Britain. The latter result qualifies a
blanket claim that Americans see no prospect of reducing driving.[^article]

The p. 3 chart reports differences in percent agreement across five paired questions, not prevalence
of bias. Its caption describes absolute-value coding for the private-property and emissions questions.
The article gives no full questionnaire, sample denominators, fieldwork dates, weighting or confidence
intervals. Goddard's explanations involving smoking campaigns, workplace protections and limited
travel choices are interpretations, not causal tests.[^article]

## Use and limits

The responses suggest that the subject of a harm-related question matters to reported acceptance.
They do not quantify effects on investment decisions, explain national crash-rate differences, or show
that an attitude intervention changes travel or injuries. The primary paper and British study remain
unreviewed; this news account is not independent confirmation of their findings.

See [street-redesign outcomes](../topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md#comfort-support-and-correct-use-are-different-outcomes)
for the distinction between general attitudes, local support and measured behavior.
