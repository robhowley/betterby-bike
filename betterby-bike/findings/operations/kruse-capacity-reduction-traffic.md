# Reported traffic disappearance after road-capacity reductions has unclear denominators

## In brief

- Kruse's account of a March 1998 review of 60 worldwide capacity-reduction cases reports that an average 25% of
  traffic disappeared, with 60% in some cases. The paper does not define the percentage denominator or averaging method.
- Local displaced traffic and wider-area traffic are different measures: Nürnberg's nearly 80% disappearance account
  accompanies a 7% overall reduction. Neither figure is a general forecast for a new closure.
- The paper acknowledges that some reductions may yield no measurable traffic reduction; it does not establish
  that all closures reduce driving or leave congestion unchanged.[^kruse]

## Evidence and scale

The review covered maintenance/repair, natural disasters and policy-led reductions. For Nürnberg, Kruse describes
pedestrianization from the 1970s and closure of the last through street in 1988. Officials said nearly 80% of car
traffic could not be accounted for on parallel streets; the reported overall reduction for 1988–1992 was 7%.
The paper does not define the wider counting boundary.[^kruse]

For New York's West Side Highway, Kruse describes counts three years before and two years after the 1973 closure,
reported in a 1976 NYDOT study: 53% of trips disappeared, and 93% of those did not reappear elsewhere, with 7%
diverting to parallel roads. The British review's reported overall reduction was instead 8%. The paper does not
supply the count tables or geographic denominators needed to reconcile these percentages. Do not multiply them
or describe 53% as a citywide driving reduction.[^kruse]

## The book account repeats the West Side Highway case

The newly reviewed cropped chapter opening dates the collapse to December 15, 1973. Its claim that this
proved congestion was only tangentially linked to road space is the author's interpretation, not a measured
capacity–congestion relationship. The opening supplies historical background but no traffic or delay results;
it is part of the same book account, not another study.[^carmageddon]

*Carmageddon*, printed p. 114, reports roughly 70,000 daily vehicles before the December 1973 collapse and a
severe initial jam. It quotes Sam Schwartz that the predicted subsequent traffic disaster never appeared, but
also that where the cars went was unknown. The book says total travel into southern Manhattan's business district
later increased while fewer people arrived by car, without supplying counts, observation dates or methods.
This is the same closure already represented above, not independent evidence or proof of cycling substitution.
It neither reconciles Kruse's percentages nor establishes that closure caused no short-term delay. Page 120
repeats the claim that more people could enter Manhattan, without new counts, dates or methods. Its argument
that removing road space need not reduce mobility is not a second observation or a quantified access effect.[^carmageddon]

## Interpretation limits

This is secondary reporting of the Goodwin-led *Traffic Impact of Highway Capacity Reductions* review, not an
independent replication or a newly reviewed primary dataset. Kruse gives no case-selection procedure, weighting,
confidence interval or full distribution. Her “60% in some cases” headline is not a documented universal maximum,
and cannot be compared directly with Nürnberg's differently scoped 80% account.

The reported absence of traffic on counted roads does not itself reveal how much shifted mode, changed destination,
changed time or was no longer undertaken. Network conditions, incentives and daily activities qualify transferability.
The quoted researchers argue that well-designed reductions need not inevitably cause unacceptable congestion;
that is narrower than guaranteeing less traffic or no delay at every project.[^kruse]
