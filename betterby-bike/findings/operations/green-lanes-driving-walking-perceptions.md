# Green Lane neighbors reported mixed driving and walking experiences

## In brief

- In the 2013 five-city Green Lane resident survey, 59% of driving responses reported unchanged travel times,
  31% longer and 10% shorter; 44% reported worse parking availability.[^monsere2014]
- Walking satisfaction increased in 36% of responses and decreased in 15%. Crossing safety perceptions worsened
  more often than they improved near Chicago's Dearborn and Milwaukee lanes.[^monsere2014]
- These are recalled experiences, not measured delays or injuries. Questions depended on route use and applicability;
  some residents answered for multiple streets, so percentages have no single unique-person denominator.[^monsere2014]

## Survey basis

Nearby residents described experiences and attributed changes on recently redesigned streets. The survey did not
measure parking occupancy.[^monsere2014]

## Driving and parking

Among resident driving responses, 31% reported longer driving times and 10% shorter times; 59% reported no change.
Negative congestion effects were reported by 36%, versus 11% positive. Parking was a common concern: 44% reported
worse ability to find a space and 45% worse parking stress. Conversely, 52% reported fewer bicyclists in vehicle lanes,
and 53% said bicyclists acted more safely and predictably.[^monsere2014]

Adverse responses were not uniform. Longer driving times were reported by 54% near Dearborn and 63% near Milwaukee
in Chicago, versus 15–32% at other route entries. On Portland's NE Multnomah, 30% reported worse parking availability
although the project added 27 spaces. This does not invalidate perceived difficulty, but space counts and parking
experience are different measures.

Table 9-4 lists 2,174 affirmative route-driving responses. Questions were conditional on use and applicability;
some residents answered for multiple streets, and the displayed total is not a unique-person denominator for every
percentage. Missing/neutral categories are omitted from several table rows.

## Walking and crossing

Table 9-5 lists 1,979 affirmative route-walking responses. Walking satisfaction increased in 36% of responses and
decreased in 15%; 49% reported fewer cyclists on sidewalks. However, perceived crossing safety fell more often than
it rose on Dearborn (45% versus 18%) and Milwaukee (37% versus 17%). Among responses about unsignalized crossings
on applicable routes, 59% said cyclists rarely or never stopped for pedestrians.[^monsere2014]

## Limits

These selected-neighborhood surveys had a 23% resident response rate, demographic differences from local Census
populations, and no before survey or matched controls. Respondents' causal attribution to lanes is not independently
verified. The data neither prove that protected lanes generally increase congestion nor show that pedestrian concerns
are absent when overall public support is positive.
