# Reported global SUV sales growth adds energy context, not cycling risk

## In brief

- Slate reported SUVs made up nearly half of worldwide car sales in 2023, versus 20% about 15 years earlier; sales share
  is not the share of all vehicles on the road.
- Citing the IEA, the article attributed 500,000 additional barrels/day of global oil consumption and 20% of the 2023
  increase in human-caused CO2 emissions to growth in large cars, not 20% of total emissions.
- These secondary figures do not estimate cyclist injury risk or the benefits of SUV fees, advertising restrictions or
  replacing car trips with cycling.[^zipper2024]

## Scope and interpretation

The worldwide headline concerns SUVs. Slate's separate four-in-five US new-car-sales figure combines
SUVs and pickups and supplies no explicit reference year; it is not a directly matched international
comparison. Larger categories also hide within-model growth in weight and dimensions. No uncertainty
intervals, classification rules or detailed energy calculations appear in the article.[^zipper2024]

The underlying [IEA commentary](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/suvs-are-setting-new-sales-records-each-year-and-so-are-their-emissions)
is identified through the PDF link but was not separately reviewed. The reported energy estimates
must not be treated as original Slate analysis or independently confirmed here. Neither new-sales
shares nor aggregate oil-demand changes supply a travel-exposure denominator for pedestrians or cyclists.

## Related evidence

[The Economist's crash-conditional occupant analysis](/findings/safety/us-vehicle-weight-crash-fatalities.md)
measures a different outcome from sales and energy demand. Slate's safety narrative does not replicate
that analysis. [Interpreting cycling injury risk](/topics/cycling-injury-risk.md) distinguishes both
from exposure-based injury rates and evaluated vehicle-policy effects.

[^zipper2024]: Slate, December 10, 2024, PDF pp. 1–2 for sales shares and definitions; p. 3 for
  IEA-attributed oil/CO2 estimates; pp. 4–5 for policy accounts, without safety-effect estimates.
