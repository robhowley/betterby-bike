# Mini-Holland high-dose areas were associated with more weekly active travel

## In brief

- TfL reports that residents of substantially implemented Mini-Holland areas had 41 extra minutes of past-week
  walking/cycling in 2017 and 44 in 2018, relative to 2016 baseline and changes in other outer-London boroughs.
  These are adjusted longitudinal associations, not randomized or cycling-only effects.[^report]
- Walking increased more than cycling. Increased cycling participation was statistically significant only in
  2017; the broader proximity-related driving-time decline in 2018 was nonsignificant. Favorable cycling-environment
  perceptions coexisted with growing views that too much was spent on cycling.[^report]

## Design and comparison

Enfield, Kingston and Waltham Forest each received £30 million for networks and neighborhood changes, including
protected routes, traffic filtering, crossings, public space, parking and training. Borough officers mapped
“high-dose” areas where residents were likely to be affected by implemented work; other intervention-borough
areas were “low-dose”. Waltham Forest was furthest advanced. The second follow-up also examined objective route
proximity and residence in neighborhoods with restrictions on through-motor traffic.[^report]

The University of Westminster/TfL online panel recruited from random household sampling, Oyster users and
registered cycling-news recipients. May–June surveys had 3,435 participants in 2016, 1,712 in 2017 and 1,610
in 2018. Travel diaries recalled trips over the past week. Figure 5.29 adjusts for demographic differences,
sets control-area change to zero and plots 95% confidence intervals. High-dose intervals are above zero;
low-dose intervals cross zero. Exact interval endpoints are not tabulated and are not estimated here.[^report]

## Mixed and distinct outcomes

- TfL reports a 24% increase in cycling participation in Wave 1, not a 24-percentage-point mode-share gain.
  The participation trend in Wave 2 was not statistically significant. Walking participation reached significance
  only in Wave 2 among those closer to a Mini-Holland route.[^report]
- The narrower Wave 2 comparison for residents of modally filtered neighborhoods reports 20% lower likelihood
  of any past-week car use and 39.5 fewer minutes in the car versus controls. This is not the same comparison
  as the nonsignificant driving-time trend for proximity to interventions generally, nor a traffic-count result.[^report]
- Cycling-related neighborhood perceptions improved, while ten non-cycling items showed no consistent change.
  In Mini-Holland boroughs, the share saying there was too much cycling investment rose from 27% at baseline
  to 33% and 36%; too little support fell from 28% to 20% and 19%. TfL reports significant growth in the
  too-much perception, unlike controls. Better travel outcomes did not imply uniformly greater support.[^report]

## Limits and provenance

Young adults aged 16–24, non-white people and people not employed were underrepresented. TfL notes similar
skew in intervention/control groups, but this does not remove selection, attrition, recall or residual-confounding
concerns. Areas and treatments were not randomized; the package does not identify a protected-lane-only effect
or prove a reduction in injuries. Intercept surveys of current route users, also reported in section 5.5, are
not representative resident adoption estimates.[^report]

This is the result as summarized by TfL, including 2018 follow-up work. The linked
[Aldred et al. first paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2018.05.018), later draft and panel data were not
independently reviewed. Multiple waves and outputs from this panel are not independent studies.
