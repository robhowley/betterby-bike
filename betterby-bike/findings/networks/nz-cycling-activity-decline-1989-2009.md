# New Zealand survey data show cycling time per person fell 51% from 1989–90 to 2006–09

## In brief

- Clarke’s Table 1 reports average cycling time declining from 11.4 to 5.6 hours per person per year between
  1989–90 and 2006–09, while estimated total cycling time fell from 39 million to 24 million hours.[^clarke2012]
- The review quotes Ministry survey figures of 28 to 8 minutes per week for ages 5–12 and 52 to 12 minutes for ages
  13–17 between 1989/90 and 2005/08. Clarke’s unweighted average gives a 75% decline, from 40 to 10 minutes;
  this is not a population-weighted estimate for all children aged 5–17.[^clarke2012]
- These are survey trends, not a measurement of the helmet law’s effect. Clarke attributes the decline to the law, but
  the review does not compare jurisdictions, helmet use, or other travel and road-safety changes.[^clarke2012]

## Survey measures

| Period | Estimated population | Cycling hours per year | Average cycling hours per person | Change from 1989–90 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1989–1990 | 3,407,000 | 39 million | 11.4 | Reference |
| 1997–1998 | 3,770,000 | 26 million | 6.9 | −40% |
| 2003–2006 | 4,080,000 | 22 million | 5.4 | −53% |
| 2006–2009 | 4,250,000 | 24 million | 5.6 | −51% |

The table combines 1989–2006 data from *Sustainable and safe land transport trends and indicators* with 2006–09 data
from *Cycling for transport: Ongoing New Zealand Household Travel Survey 2006–2009*. The periods are survey reporting
windows, not a continuous annual series. Clarke also quotes a separate Ministry comparison ending in 2005/08, so that
child estimate should not be treated as the same endpoint as the Table 1 total. Average hours divide estimated total
activity by the estimated national population, not by the number of cyclists. The 39-to-24-million-hour decline is
about 38.5%, distinct from the 51% per-person decline. Average walking time fell from 56 to 50 hours per person per
year (11%), although total walking time increased from 191 to 212 million hours.[^clarke2012]

## What this does not establish

The comparison covers the years before and after the 1 January 1994 law but has no untreated comparison, law-compliance
measure, counterfactual trend or uncertainty estimate. Changes in transport choices, population, road conditions,
health policy and survey measurement could contribute to the observed decline. It therefore describes reduced cycling
activity, not a causal estimate of compulsory helmets or a current cycling rate.

This measure also concerns average travel time, not trips, distance, work-only commuting or the quality of available
cycling routes. [Tin Tin et al.’s injury-rate study](/findings/safety/nz-cycling-injury-rates-1988-2007.md) uses overlapping
historical travel-survey evidence for a different question and should not be counted as an independent confirmation of
this secondary compilation.
