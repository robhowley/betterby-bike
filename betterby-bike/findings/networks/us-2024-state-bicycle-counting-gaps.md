# Reported state bicycle-counting gaps limit interpretation of cycling deaths

## In brief

- Streetsblog's December 2024 coverage of the League's state assessment reports **22 states operating or
  funding bicycle surveys or counts**, described as four fewer than in 2022.[^wilson2024]
- This measures reported program presence, not rider numbers or cycling distance. The article calls it a new
  survey question without explaining historical comparability, so a confirmed decline in counting capacity
  cannot be inferred.[^wilson2024]
- Without comparable riding exposure, increased cycling deaths cannot establish whether risk per trip or mile
  increased. Program presence alone also does not establish adequate exposure measurement.[^wilson2024]

## Reporting basis and uncertainty

The League combines public datasets with a state-DOT survey for its rankings. The article supplies no question
wording, respondent coverage, program definitions, count-site representativeness or exposure estimates. The
underlying national assessment and survey responses were not independently reviewed. Its reported 2022
comparison is retained as secondary reporting, not reconstructed from an assumed unchanged instrument.

League policy director Ken McLeod suspects that states increasingly outsource measurement to companies using
cellphone and other mobility data. That is an interview hypothesis, not a demonstrated reason for the reported
change. His preference for established counting technologies is not a comparative accuracy evaluation.

## Relationship to other evidence

The [New Jersey card's commuter-normalized fatality indicator](../safety/new-jersey-report-card-safety-denominator.md)
comes from the same League program; this article does not repair its missing exposure and observation-year detail
or independently confirm its values. The [Washington count-expansion study](washington-count-expansion-limits.md)
separately shows why having counts is not sufficient for representative annual mileage estimates.
