# 2018 highway revenue shares do not measure who subsidizes whom

## In brief

- Across U.S. governments in **2018**, **$121.3 billion (51%) of $237.8 billion** raised for highways
  came from highway-dedicated motor-fuel taxes, vehicle taxes/fees and tolls. The remaining
  **$116.5 billion** included general funds and borrowing, not just taxes paid by non-drivers.
- Local governments raised **$70.1 billion**, of which **$7.1 billion** was user charges, but these
  collection-level accounts do not identify where road users paid taxes or establish a local-to-state
  highway subsidy. They also do not isolate cycling spending.[^usdot]

## What the accounts measure

USDOT's administrative compilation draws predominantly on state reports using state/local accounting
systems. User charges are taxes and fees imposed on motor-vehicle owners/operators, not all taxes
those people pay. Only the portion used for highways enters the $121.3 billion figure. Revenues
allocated to transit or other nonhighway purposes are excluded.[^usdot]

Exhibit 2-4 divides the remaining $116.5 billion into general-fund appropriations ($39.4 billion),
property taxes/assessments ($11.6 billion), other taxes/fees ($21.8 billion), investment income/other
receipts ($22.0 billion) and bond proceeds ($21.7 billion). These are 2018 dollar amounts. Borrowing
provides current resources but requires future repayment; this table does not assign repayment to
particular road users or taxpayers.[^usdot]

Exhibit 2-5 reports local general-fund appropriations of $26.9 billion and local property taxes of
$11.6 billion. Those amounts belong to the $70.1 billion locally raised total. Exhibit 2-3 separately
reports **net transfers into local governments of $20.1 billion** from other government levels.
A net transfer is not a gross flow from one named level, nor a measure of which residents ultimately
paid for roads. Government collection level, road ownership and taxpayer residence are distinct.[^usdot]

## Interpretation limits

These results show that dedicated motor-vehicle charges were not the sole highway revenue source.
They do **not** show that non-drivers paid the entire remainder: drivers also pay general taxes,
and bonds may be repaid from multiple sources. Nor do local collections include every state/federal
charge paid by local residents. Establishing local-road-user subsidies would require additional
information on tax payments, travel, allocation rules and benefits.

Revenue is not expenditure. The report records $244.5 billion of 2018 spending and $6.7 billion
of net reserve drawdown. Therefore 51% is a **revenue share**, not the reported share of spending
covered by user fees. The aggregates are historical, pre-pandemic and pre-BIL, not current funding
terms or evidence of cycling-project returns. Nearby source prose and gross-receipt notes conflict;
the Source records these unresolved discrepancies rather than combining incompatible totals.[^usdot]
