# 1970s U.S. gasoline-tax support depended on the question and promised benefits

## In brief

- In a November 1974 Harris survey of **1,525 U.S. households**, as reported by Knittel, **13% favored** an
  unqualified ten-cent-per-gallon tax increase and **74% opposed** it.
- Support reached **51%** when the question promised both an income-tax credit and an end to Middle East oil
  dependence. This conditional preference is not evidence that the tax could deliver that outcome or improve access.
- Historical objections emphasized low-income and car-dependent travelers, but the paper does not measure
  distributional burdens, changes in driving or cycling, or today's public support.[^knittel2013]

## What the poll compared

Knittel's Table 4 reports the following shares of respondents, in percent. Amounts are nominal 1974 U.S. cents
per gallon, not inflation-adjusted present-day prices.[^knittel2013]

| Proposed increase and conditions | Favor | Oppose | Not sure |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 20 cents, unqualified | 7 | 88 | 5 |
| 10 cents, unqualified | 13 | 74 | 13 |
| 10 cents, income-tax credit | 35 | 56 | 9 |
| 10 cents, ending dependence on Arab oil (table wording) | 39 | 47 | 14 |
| 10 cents, both credit and oil-independence conditions | 51 | 38 | 11 |

The credit-only question refers to credit for gasoline taxes paid on the next year's income tax. It should not
be interpreted as a tested equal-per-person dividend. Knittel calls the favorable combined conditions unrealistic.
The publication does not supply sampling uncertainty, weighting, question order or complete fieldwork details
for this poll, so the contrasts are descriptive, not experimental estimates of a rebate's effect on support.

## Alternative policies and changing responses

A separate December 1974 ORC poll of **604 telephone interviews** reported **70%** favoring a tax on large,
inefficient cars versus **13%** a gasoline tax when asked which would better limit their gasoline use.
These are responses to a policy choice, not measured conservation or proof that respondents understood full costs.

Knittel also reports Cambridge surveys preferring rationing to $2-per-gallon gasoline: **65% versus 15%** in
1977, and **49% versus 25%** in 1980. The 1977 survey had 1,500 respondents; the paper does not state the 1980
sample size. Current gasoline prices had roughly doubled between surveys and wording changed. The declining
rationing preference qualifies any claim of unchanging opposition, but does not identify why attitudes changed.
These separate surveys should not be pooled or counted as independent replications of the Harris question.[^knittel2013]

## What this adds to access evidence

Statements by policymakers and labor groups linked opposition to low incomes, rural travel and commuting
constraints. Knittel's interpretation that distributional concerns may outweigh efficiency gains remains a
hypothesis, not a finding that gasoline taxes necessarily worsen equity or that rationing improves it. Actual
burdens depend on household circumstances, alternatives and revenue use, none evaluated by these polling shares.
The historical review supplies no measured benefit from substituting cycling or building bicycle lanes.

See [commuting and access](/topics/active-commuting-and-access.md#historical-tax-preferences-do-not-measure-equitable-access)
for the distinction between stated affordability concerns and observed transport choices.
