# Reported US youth driving trips fell while driving distance rose, 2009–2017

## In brief

- Streetsblog reports that U.S. teenagers' median weekly driving trips fell 16% between 2009 and 2017, while median
  weekly driving distance rose nearly 12%.
- Its account of Kailai Wang's youth-travel study does not establish that fewer licenses or trips meant more walking,
  cycling or transit use; exact trip/distance denominators and primary methods were not reviewed.[^wilson2024]

## What the report supports

The article describes 65% of 16–20-year-olds as licensed in 2017, “down 8 percent” from 2009.
It does not resolve whether that change is relative or in percentage points, so no earlier licensing
share is reconstructed. It also reports double-digit declines in overall trips for purposes other than
school and work. Less travel can therefore reflect fewer activities outside the home rather than
successful replacement of driving with sustainable modes.[^wilson2024]

The juxtaposition of fewer driving trips and greater driving distance is a reason not to treat licensing
or trip counts as interchangeable with distance. The prose calls both measures median weekly values
but supplies no absolute values or clear licensed-only denominator. It does not establish a change in
total youth vehicle mileage, emissions or car dependence caused by any particular policy.

## Interpretation and limits

Wang reports a negative density–licensing association and interprets a remaining adjusted generational
difference as partly attitudinal. His recommendations for destinations within a 10–15-minute walk and
better transit are proposals, not measured treatment effects. Economic and documentation barriers,
reduced outings and ride-hailing can also complicate the interpretation of lower licensing.
The news account lacks sample size, model details and uncertainty estimates; the underlying study
was not independently read. The December 2024 republication is not an additional observation period.
