# London's cycling demographic estimates differ between surveys

## In brief

- TfL's LTDS account puts non-white/mixed-race people at about 25% of those cycling at least annually, against
  37% of London's population. Its separate 2017 Attitudes to Cycling table gives 36% BAME among annual cyclists
  against 37% in the population. These are different survey results, not independent confirmation of one gap.[^report]
- LTDS reports about 15% of annual cyclists in households earning under £20,000, versus about 25% of the
  population. That describes cyclist composition, not the percentage of low-income residents who cycle.
  Small cycling samples and unreconciled survey differences limit generalization.[^report]

## Measures and survey basis

LTDS is a continuous household survey with about 8,000 London households annually. TfL warns that the small
cycling subgroup can produce large year-to-year changes unrepresentative of the population. Figures 5.7–5.11
cover 2010/11–2017/18; the income result describes the latest profile. The separate Attitudes to Cycling survey's
Table 5.2 is labelled 2017 and uses BAME (black, Asian and minority ethnic) categories.[^report]

The distinction is not confined to ethnicity. LTDS describes just over half of annual cyclists as male,
compared with over 70% of cycle commuters since 2010/11. Table 5.2 instead reports men as 63% of annual cyclists,
versus 49% of the population. Commuting and any cycling during a year select different groups. Table 5.2 also
reports 82% of annual cyclists under 45 versus 59% of the population, and 53% inner-London residents versus
40% of the population.[^report]

## What remains uncertain

The report does not reconcile the household and attitudes estimates or supply sufficient sampling, weighting
and respondent-base detail here to explain the differences. Do not average them or select only the survey that
supports a preferred equity conclusion. Figures 5.13–5.14 explicitly mark a change in cyclist definition and from
telephone to online collection within the attitudes time series, qualifying claims of stable participation.[^report]

TfL interprets the household-income trend as possibly reflecting a lifestyle image of cycling. It also reports
safety-related barriers and increasing agreement that cycling is “not for people like me”. These are interpretations
and reported attitudes, not tests showing income or ethnicity causes low cycling, nor estimates of who benefits
from a new route.[^report]
