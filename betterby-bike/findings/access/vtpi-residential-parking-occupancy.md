# Selected residential studies report unused parking, with local exceptions

## In brief

- Litman's 2025 synthesis reports **30% of spaces unoccupied at peak** at nearly 200 Boston-area multifamily
  buildings and **461 of 883 spaces occupied (52%)** at 19 recent Denver-area affordable developments.
- These are secondary accounts of selected properties, not a national surplus estimate. New Jersey's rental
  summary includes **0.10 spaces per unit of underprovision** for two-bedroom high-rise units, alongside
  average overprovision in other categories.[^litman]

## What the report describes

The Boston result is attributed to MAPC's *Perfect Fit Parking Initiative* (2019); unused parking was greater
where supply, transit access and affordable housing were greater. The Denver result is attributed to Fox Tuttle
(2021). Its $9.3 million unused-space cost and equivalent 40 supportive units are estimates, not homes actually built.
The account does not state the Denver observation window.

For 23 recently completed Seattle-area multifamily buildings, London and Williams-Derry (2013) reportedly found
**37% of spaces empty at peak** and no sampled property recovering estimated full parking costs through parking
fees. The reported average $246 monthly shortfall per occupied apartment is a cost allocation, not an observed
rent cut or a direct measurement of who paid it.

Davis et al.'s 2023 New Jersey rental study is summarized as average excess requirements of **0.56 spaces per
low-rise unit** and **0.32 per high-rise unit**. High-rise category estimates range from 0.24 excess spaces for
studio/one-bedroom units to 0.10 too few for two-bedroom units and 0.82 excess for units with three or more bedrooms.
This comparison of standards with estimated ownership/usage is not identical to a peak-occupancy survey.[^litman]

## Limits

Primary studies were not independently read. Litman does not reproduce enough sampling, timing, uncertainty or
model details to assess representativeness or causal attribution. Different cities, housing types and denominators
must remain separate. Vacant spaces alone do not establish that minimums caused all excess supply, that spaces
can practically become homes, or that removing minimums reduces rents by the same proportion. Local shortages,
visitor needs and spillover management remain relevant; no cycling effect is estimated.
