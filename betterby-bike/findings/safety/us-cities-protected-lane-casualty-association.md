# Streetsblog reports lower all-user casualties in cities with separated bike lanes

Aaron Short's May 29, 2019 Streetsblog article reports that a study of **12 large U.S. cities**, analyzing
**17,000 fatalities and 77,000 severe injuries in 2000–2012**, estimated **44% fewer deaths and 50% fewer serious
injuries** with protected/separated bicycle facilities than in an **“average city.”** The reported association
concerns **all road users**, not a cyclist-only risk per trip or kilometre. New York was excluded, according to
interviewed co-author Wesley Marshall.[^short2019]

The article contrasts this favorable association with a reported **lack of road-safety improvement for painted lanes**.
It says infrastructure, rather than simply more cyclists, explained the favorable results, but does not provide
model coefficients or uncertainty for either comparison. Slower driving is Marshall's proposed explanation, not a
separately measured mechanism in the article.[^short2019]

## Interpretation and limits

- This is secondary reporting of the [linked study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jth.2019.03.004), not an independent
  analysis or a primary paper reviewed for this ingestion.
- The article does not define the comparator, outcome denominators, facility exposure, model adjustments or confidence
  intervals. The percentages should not be treated as guaranteed reductions from installing a lane or combined with
  cyclist-specific route-risk estimates.
- Its update flags gentrification-related safety disparities and improvements in largely gentrified areas. Without
  subgroup estimates or adjustment details, it cannot establish equally distributed benefits.
- The painted-lane report supplies no estimate or precision, so it does not establish zero benefit in every setting.
  Marshall's warning about cars parking between bollards and forcing cyclists to merge is a design concern, not a
  quantified adverse effect or a tested comparison with concrete planters.[^short2019]
