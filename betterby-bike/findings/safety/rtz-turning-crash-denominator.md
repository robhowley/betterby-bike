# Turning-crash odds and visibility inference limits

## In brief

- Among intersection crashes in which a crossing pedestrian died, Road to Zero reports left-turn versus no-turn
  odds about twice as high for SUVs, three times for vans and four times for pickups as for cars.[^rtz2024]
- These are comparisons within fatal crashes, not pedestrian deaths per turn. Blind zones are a proposed explanation;
  the cited comparison does not establish that they caused the excess turning share.[^rtz2024]

## Evidence and denominator

The report attributes the result to Hu and Cicchino (2022),
[*Relationship of pedestrian crash types and passenger vehicle types*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsr.2022.07.006).
For right turns versus no turn, it reports odds 89% higher for pickups and 63% higher for SUVs than for cars.
It places turning crashes in the context of more than 900 of roughly 5,800 fatal pedestrian crashes at or near
intersections during 2014–2018. Neither the underlying paper nor the linked IIHS account was independently read.[^rtz2024]

This comparison concerns the type of crash among deaths. It does not supply numbers of turns, pedestrians encountered,
vehicle miles or nonfatal encounters for each vehicle category. It therefore cannot estimate pedestrian death risk per
turn, crash likelihood per mile or the benefit of changing A-pillars. The report omits the model controls and uncertainty
intervals for these odds.[^rtz2024]

## Visibility evidence is related, not interchangeable

The report describes high hoods, wider A-pillars and large mirrors as potential obstructions. Separately, it reports
that UK heavy-truck drivers responded twice as fast through direct window vision as through mirrors/cameras.
It explicitly cautions that this is heavy-duty-truck evidence and asks for light-truck reaction-time research.
The UK result cannot be assigned to SUVs or used to turn the fatal-crash odds into a measured blind-zone effect.[^rtz2024]
