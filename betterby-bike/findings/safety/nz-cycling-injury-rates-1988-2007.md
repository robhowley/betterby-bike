# New Zealand cycling injury rates rose after 1996–99 while other travel-mode rates fell

Among New Zealand road users aged five or older, cycling deaths and first public-hospital injury admissions lasting at least one day fell per hour travelled from 1988–91 to 1996–99, then rose in 2003–07. Rates for drivers, passengers, motorcyclists and pedestrians declined across all three periods.[^nz2010]

| Cycling measure | 1988–91 | 1996–99 | 2003–07 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annual cases | 941 | 512 | 682 |
| Cases per million cycling hours (95% CI) | 25.61 (23.97–27.25) | 21.38 (19.53–23.23) | 30.74 (28.43–33.04) |
| Serious cases per million cycling hours (95% CI) | 10.27 (9.23–11.30) | 4.86 (3.98–5.75) | 6.24 (5.21–7.28) |

Serious injuries were estimated as consistent with Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) scores ≥3, indicating a significant threat to life. Their rate rose after 1996–99 but remained below the earliest period. Annual case counts also remained below 1988–91, despite the higher overall exposure-based rate.[^nz2010]

In 2003–07, cases per million travel hours were 107.64 for motorcyclists, 30.74 for cyclists, 2.89 for car/van passengers, 2.38 for pedestrians and 2.10 for car/van drivers. Cycling ranked second for both overall and serious injury rates among these categories.[^nz2010]

## What this comparison does not establish

These are rates per hour, not per trip, kilometre or resident. They combine deaths and inpatient injuries, not fatalities alone. The study did not adjust comparisons for road types used, evaluate infrastructure changes, or estimate cycling's net health effects. It does not establish why cycling risk rose or what present-day rates are.

Travel-survey estimates supply the denominators; relatively few cycling trips and changing response rates introduce uncertainty. Reported confidence intervals assumed Poisson case counts. Coding changes and admission practices can affect trends, and less severe injuries outside inpatient care were excluded.[^nz2010]
