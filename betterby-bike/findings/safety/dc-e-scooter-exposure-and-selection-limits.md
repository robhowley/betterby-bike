# DC scooter injury case counts cannot identify the safest riding facility

## In brief

- The 2019 Washington, DC hospital study counted 60 sidewalk, 24 road and eight bike-lane rider incidents, but did not
  measure riding exposure on those facilities. Fewer bike-lane cases cannot establish lower injury risk.
- Greater severity among road cases does not establish that sidewalks are safer overall; the study also missed some
  overnight and severely injured patients, and did not measure risk to other users per encounter.[^cicchino2021]

## What the denominators mean

Of 105 injured riders, 104 had known facility locations: 60 sidewalks (58%), 24 roads (23%), ten trails/off-road
locations (10%), eight bike lanes (8%) and two alleys (2%). These percentages divide by known injury cases, not
trips, hours or distance ridden. Where the exact site was known, only two road cases and six sidewalk cases had a
bike lane in the direction of travel. Availability and use could therefore explain low bike-lane case counts without
any lower per-trip risk. The authors explicitly leave that question open.[^cicchino2021]

Similarly, 37% of injured riders reported a first ride, but no denominator for all first rides or later rides was
collected. The authors' suggestion that injury incidence might fall as experience grows, even while injury severity
rises, is a hypothesis, not an observed longitudinal result.[^cicchino2021]

## Selection and outcome definitions

Recruitment at one hospital ran 9 a.m.–10 p.m. during March–November 2019 and required interview participation.
Three people too injured to communicate were excluded; overnight presentations could be missed, although researchers
followed admitted patients to reduce loss. Only two of 103 assessed riders had AIS ≥3 injuries. This is not a census
of scooter casualties or an adequate basis for fatality comparisons.[^cicchino2021]

Seven assessed riders (7%) had head injuries, but lacerations, contusions and abrasions were coded as external injuries
regardless of body location. That definition, recruitment differences and possibly DC's 10 mph shared-scooter cap could
explain differences from other studies, according to the authors. The study did not isolate a speed-cap effect or
establish a directly comparable cross-city head-injury rate.[^cicchino2021]

## Implication and related evidence

Facility policy needs both crash/fall incidence and severity, plus harm to other users. The authors suggest separated
space but do not establish which type is safest; lower-severity sidewalk cases could coexist with more pedestrian
conflicts. This study cannot measure an installation effect or transfer its scooter results to bicycles.[^cicchino2021]
