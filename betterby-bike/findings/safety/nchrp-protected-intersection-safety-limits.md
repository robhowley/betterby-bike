# Two protected-intersection pairs did not establish a crash-safety effect

## In brief

- May 2018 observations at one protected and one comparison intersection in each of Berkeley and Salt Lake
  City showed mixed behavior, not a consistent safety advantage or proof of harm.[^report]
- Motorists yielded in 80% versus 100% of selected bicycle/turning-vehicle yielding interactions in Berkeley,
  and 68% versus 77% in Salt Lake City. These small, selected interaction denominators are not all drivers or
  injury-risk denominators.[^report]
- Two treated sites and sparse crash histories could neither prove nor disprove crash benefits; the authors
  described the overall results as mostly equivocal.[^report]

## What the observations measure

Researchers coded road users crossing the same potential collision area less than five seconds apart, with
observable behavior informing yielding and near-miss classification. A short time gap alone was not proof of
an unsafe interaction. Video covered three partial daylight days per site; glare and camera limitations left
missing observations. Visible slowing or stopping was required for intentional yielding, potentially missing
earlier yielding outside camera view.[^report]

Table 29's motorist-yield counts were 12/15 versus 9/9 selected yielding interactions in Berkeley and 34/50
versus 27/35 in Salt Lake City (protected versus comparison). Using *all* bicycle–motorist interactions instead,
Salt Lake City's shares were 28% versus 23%, reversing the direction. Neither denominator means the fraction
of all motorists legally required to yield.[^report]

Movement-specific results also differed. Salt Lake City right-turn motorists yielded in 23/31 selected
interactions at the protected site versus 7/13 at the comparison, while left-turn counts were 11/19 versus
20/22. Berkeley's comparison left-turn count conflicts: Table 30 says one yield, but nearby prose says both
of two interactions yielded and Table 29's total implies two. This discrepancy remains unresolved.[^report]

Table 29 recorded one versus zero bicycle/turning-motorist near misses in Berkeley and five versus one in
Salt Lake City. Such sparse, nonrandomized observations cannot identify a causal increase. The authors also
report more two-stage bicycle left turns, but worse bicycle and pedestrian signal compliance, at protected
sites. Favorable and adverse indicators must be retained together with the
[movement-specific speed findings](../design/nchrp-protected-intersection-turning-speeds.md).[^report]

## Why the crash history is not an effectiveness estimate

Berkeley's protected site had four recorded pedestrian crashes in 11 pre-treatment years and none in 1.9
post-treatment years, with zero bicycle crashes in either period. Salt Lake City's had one pedestrian and
two bicycle crashes in 5.8 pre-treatment years versus one of each in 2.8 post-treatment years (Table 45).
These are small counts over unequal durations, not exposure-adjusted risks. The authors explicitly reject
formal before/after effectiveness analysis or a crash modification factor from this sample. Underreporting,
site differences and limited follow-up compound the problem.[^report]
