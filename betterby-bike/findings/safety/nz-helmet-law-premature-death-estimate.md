# Clarke’s estimate of 53 premature deaths per year is an assumption-based helmet-law counterfactual

## In brief

- Clarke estimated that the helmet requirement had caused about 53 premature deaths per year by treating reduced
  cycling as lost health benefit, not by observing deaths attributable to the law.[^clarke2012]
- The calculation starts with 5.8 fewer cycling hours per person per year across an assumed 4 million people, or 23.2
  million lost hours. Clarke converts this to about 278 million kilometres at 12 km/h, with a similar 277-million-kilometre
  alternative based on assumed trips.[^clarke2012]
- The estimate assumes the law caused the activity decline and cites a WHO health-assessment method, but does not
  show enough inputs to reproduce the 53-death result. It is not a measured law effect.[^clarke2012]

## Calculation basis

Clarke uses average cycling time of 11.4 hours per person in 1989–90 and 5.6 hours in 2006–09, then assumes the
5.8-hour difference applies to an NZ population of 4 million. He converts the resulting 23.2 million hours using a
12-km/h cycling speed, and separately checks the distance with an assumed 190,000 trips per day at 4 km each. He then
attributes the estimate to the World Health Organization’s health-assessment method. The preceding discussion also
cites Hillman’s estimate that cycling’s life-years gained outweigh accident losses by 20 to 1, but does not show that
ratio being used to calculate 53 deaths.[^clarke2012]

These are the review author’s assumptions and conversions. The article does not report the full mortality-calculation
inputs or an uncertainty interval. The cited WHO and Hillman materials were not independently reviewed here; neither
the final estimate nor its applicability to New Zealand was independently validated.

## What it does not establish

The observed survey decline could have multiple causes. The review does not show that compulsory helmets caused all of
the lost cycling time, that people replaced cycling with no physical activity, or that the cited health-benefit method
applies to this population and period. It also does not compare observed mortality with a credible no-law counterfactual.
The 53 figure should therefore be reported as Clarke’s modelled estimate, not as deaths caused by New Zealand’s helmet law.
