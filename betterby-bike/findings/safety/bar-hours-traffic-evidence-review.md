# Later bar hours have mixed reported traffic outcomes across settings

## In brief

- Alcohol Justice's 2018 review reports 51.3% more alcohol-related road crashes in a Perth hotel comparison
  after some hotels extended closing from midnight to 1 a.m. It also reports adverse results elsewhere in Australia.
- A contrary England/Wales study found fewer crashes relative to Scotland after liberalization, according to
  the same review. U.S. studies reported DUI arrests/stops rather than crash reductions or increases.
- These are secondary accounts of differing studies, not a pooled causal effect, a forecast for California,
  or a measured change in cyclist risk.[^latenight2018]

## Evidence and comparisons

The report attributes the Perth result to Chikritzhs and Stockwell's 2006 *Addiction* paper (reference 30).
It describes a 1988 selective extension and compares standard-hour and extended-hour hotels. It also reports
Smith's historical Australian studies as finding 11.5% more crash injuries after a four-hour extension and an
11% increase in traffic injuries after Tasmania allowed flexible hours. Sample sizes, uncertainty and adjustment
specifications are not supplied in the report's summaries.[^latenight2018]

New York and Minnesota results concern first-time DUI arrests and police stops. Enforcement activity and offending
are not interchangeable, and these outcomes cannot be combined with crash-injury percentages. Green, Heywood and
Navarro's 2014 England/Wales study (reference 34) is reported as finding a crash decline, primarily in younger
drivers, relative to Scotland. The review says the initial analysis was statistically insignificant and a favorable
result emerged after weighting; it notes a different preceding Scottish trend. That characterization is the
review authors' account, not an independent appraisal of the primary model.[^latenight2018]

## Limits and use

No primary study was independently read. The review has no documented systematic selection method, and the
reported changes differ in jurisdiction, hours, population and outcome. Its California Figure 1 is an assumed
shift of the timing of 2013 fatalities, not a measured effect of extending last call or a validated commuter-harm
forecast. Neither the historical studies nor the illustration supplies cyclist-specific exposure or a bike-lane
comparison. The report's acknowledgment of international context and enforcement/transit differences limits
transfer to another city.[^latenight2018]
