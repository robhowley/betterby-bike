# Adding assumed non-intersection crashes changed a sidewalk-bikeway risk comparison

## In brief

- An intersection-only Palo Alto comparison cannot establish whole-route sidewalk-bikeway versus roadway crash
  risk: it omits non-intersection roadway crashes.[^lusk2011]
- Lusk and colleagues' reanalysis changed estimated sidewalk risk from 76% higher to 7% higher, with no statistically
  clear difference, by adding assumed roadway crashes rather than observed cases.[^lusk2011]
- The assumed allocation is uncertain. The original Palo Alto paper was not reviewed for this finding.[^lusk2011]

## Reanalysis and crash coverage

Lusk and colleagues (2011) reanalysed published data from Wachtel and Lewiston (1994), a Palo Alto comparison of
bicycle–motor vehicle crashes on sidewalks designated as bikeways versus adjacent roadways. As reported by Lusk et al.,
the original comparison included only intersection crashes, omitting roadway hazards such as overtaking, sideswipes and
door collisions.[^lusk2011]

| Crash coverage                                     | Sidewalk versus roadway RR, all cyclists (95% CI) | RR for cyclists travelling with the closest traffic lane (95% CI) |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Original intersection-only data                    | 1.76 (1.16–2.68)                                  | 0.87 (0.47–1.63)                                                  |
| Reanalysis adding assumed non-intersection crashes | 1.07 (0.73–1.56)                                  | 0.53 (0.29–0.96)                                                  |

The reanalysis added roadway crashes so that non-intersection events constituted 26% of total crashes, matching a
city-wide proportion reported in the earlier publication. For all cyclists, roadway crashes rose from 48 to 79 while
sidewalk crashes stayed at 41; rider counts remained 2,005 roadway and 971 sidewalk. This changed the overall comparison
from significantly elevated sidewalk risk to no statistically significant difference.[^lusk2011]

## Scope and limitations

This is a published reanalysis with an assumed allocation of additional crashes, not newly collected route-level
evidence. Applying the city-wide non-intersection proportion to these routes may not reproduce their actual crash
distribution. The earlier primary paper was not reviewed for this ingestion; these figures and its methods are described
through Lusk et al.

The result illustrates why an intersection-only comparison need not represent whole-route risk. It does not establish
that sidewalks and bicycle-exclusive cycle tracks are equivalent, that every separated facility is safer, or that injury
severity is lower. Lusk et al.'s suggestion that accounting for severity would further favour tracks was an
interpretation, not a measured result.[^lusk2011]
