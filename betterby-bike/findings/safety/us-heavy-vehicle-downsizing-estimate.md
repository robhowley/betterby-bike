# Downsizing the heaviest vehicle decile was estimated to avert 12% of U.S. multi-car crash deaths

## In brief

- The Economist estimated that moving the heaviest tenth of the U.S. fleet from at least 5,000 lb into the
  4,500–5,000 lb class could avert about 2,300 deaths, or 12% of the 19,081 national multi-car crash deaths
  reported for 2023. This is a back-of-the-envelope counterfactual, not an observed reduction.[^economist2024]
- In its 14-state 2013–2023 sample, the heavier decile had 20.2 partner-vehicle deaths per 10,000 crashes versus
  10.3 in the next decile, while own-vehicle rates were 5.9 versus 5.4. Similar own-vehicle rates underpin the
  article's claim of no safety sacrifice, but do not prove that an intervention would protect every occupant.[^economist2024]

## Basis and uncertainty

The comparison comes from the same approximately 7.5 million police-reported two-vehicle crashes as the
[vehicle-weight association](us-vehicle-weight-crash-fatalities.md), not another independent study. The article
extrapolates sample weight-group differences to national multi-car fatalities. It does not supply a complete
calculation, uncertainty interval or model of changes in driving, crash frequency, vehicle design and collision
pairings following downsizing.[^economist2024]

Observed differences between existing weight classes do not establish what happens when particular vehicles are
replaced. This targeted proposal is also not a proposal to shrink every vehicle: the same article recounts historical
research associating broader fleet downsizing with increased deaths, and its lightest vehicles have the highest
own-vehicle death rate. Those historical studies were not independently reviewed.[^economist2024]

The 12% denominator is multi-car crash deaths, not all road deaths. Neither this estimate nor its underlying
occupant comparison quantifies cyclist/pedestrian benefits, single-vehicle outcomes or an effect of bicycle lanes.
