# Driver-attributed critical reasons do not assign 96% of intersection crash causation to drivers

## In brief

- In NHTSA's selected US 2005–2007 intersection crashes, **96.1%** had a driver-attributed critical reason,
  but the report explicitly says a critical reason may not be the cause and does not imply fault.
- Generally one reason was assigned per crash: the immediate reason for the event making collision imminent,
  not every contributing condition. The percentage cannot establish that infrastructure caused little harm
  or that driver training would prevent 96% of crashes.[^choi2010]

## What was counted

The 2,183 observed intersection cases represent a weighted 787,236 crashes. Table A6 assigns 756,570
(96.1%) to drivers, 2,932 (0.4%) to vehicles, 10,813 (1.4%) to environment and 16,920 (2.1%) to unknown
critical reasons. Rounding affects sums. Driver categories include recognition, decision, performance,
non-performance and other/unknown errors, not a uniform measure of misconduct.[^choi2010]

The survey required a crash between 6 a.m. and midnight and a damage-towed light passenger vehicle among
the first three vehicles. The report's intersection definition is based on left-turn, crossing or right-turn
pre-crash events, not simply every collision geographically near an intersection. These restrictions also
prevent treating the percentage as a share of all US crashes or all cyclist injuries.[^choi2010]

## What the classification cannot establish

The report describes critical reason as subjective and often the last failure in a simplified causal chain.
A driver-attributed immediate reason does not rule out road design, visibility, vehicle design or other
contributing conditions. Nor does it identify what would happen if one condition were changed. The later
models compare reason patterns among crash cases, not crashes against safe trips or treated against
untreated intersections.[^choi2010]

The [reason-composition finding](nmvccs-intersection-critical-reason-composition.md) retains the observed
patterns without reinterpreting them as fault. See [cycling injury risk](/topics/cycling-injury-risk.md) for
why classification, exposure-based risk and evaluated treatment effects answer different questions.

[^choi2010]: Methods and causal-chain definitions pp. 1–2; explicit subjectivity/cause/fault warning p. 5;
