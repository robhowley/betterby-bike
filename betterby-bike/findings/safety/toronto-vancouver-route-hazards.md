# Rail tracks, downhill grades and construction were associated with higher cycling injury odds

In 690 adult cycling injury trips in Toronto and Vancouver, May 2008–November 2009, three site characteristics were
associated with higher emergency-department injury odds. Each injury site was compared with a randomly selected point
along the same trip. The conditional logistic model included route type and all three features below.[^teschke2012]

| Site characteristic and comparator | Injury/control sites with feature | Adjusted OR (95% CI) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Streetcar or train tracks present versus absent | 150/98 | 3.04 (1.80–5.11) |
| Downhill versus flat grade | 333/231 | 2.32 (1.72–3.13) |
| Construction versus none | 85/46 | 1.93 (1.27–2.94) |

Uphill versus flat grades were inconclusive: OR 1.13 (0.79–1.63). Intersection location was also inconclusive in the
unadjusted analysis (OR 0.96, 0.76–1.2) and was not retained in the final model. That aggregate result does not establish
that every intersection design is safe or that intersections and non-intersections have equivalent risk.[^teschke2012]

## Interpretation and limitations

Hospital recruitment included falls and collisions without motor vehicles. Tracks present at a site do not necessarily
mean the injury involved striking a rail: these are site associations, not counts of rail-caused crashes. Almost all
observed rail-track locations were in Toronto (98%). Within-trip matching controls stable rider and trip factors,
but cannot establish the causal benefit of removing tracks, changing grades or providing construction detours.[^teschke2012]

Temporary conditions are particularly vulnerable to misclassification because site observations occurred after injuries;
interviews also queried temporary features. Children, fatal cases and people unable to recall the trip were excluded.
The authors recommend low-grade routes and safe construction detours, but these interventions were not tested.[^teschke2012]
