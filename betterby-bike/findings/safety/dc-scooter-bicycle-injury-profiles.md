# DC scooter patients had different injury types but no clear overall severity difference from cyclists

## In brief

- Among 99 scooter patients (2019) and 337 cyclists (2015–2017) at one DC ED, knee/lower-leg/ankle/foot injuries
  were more common in scooter patients (13.1% versus 3.0%), while chest/abdomen/spine injuries were less common
  (3.0% versus 14.0%). These compare injured patients, not risk per mile.
- Head/neck injury shares (7.1% versus 7.4%), admissions/transfers (9.1% versus 8.0%) and mean injury severity
  did not differ significantly. Concussion with loss of consciousness was more common for scooters, but based
  on only four scooter and two bicycle cases.[^paper]

## Injury and treatment comparisons

Reported age/gender-adjusted relative risks, obtained by converting logistic odds ratios, were:

| Outcome | Scooter cases / 99 | Bicycle cases / 337 | Reported adjusted RR (95% CI) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distal lower extremity injury | 13 | 10 | 2.76 (1.79–3.54) |
| Proximal upper extremity injury | 9 | 69 | 0.49 (0.24–0.92) |
| Chest, abdomen or spine injury | 3 | 47 | 0.24 (0.07–0.70) |
| Concussion with loss of consciousness | 4 | 2 | 3.03 (1.20–4.09) |
| Splinting | 47 | 109 | 1.69 (1.23–2.20) |
| Fracture/dislocation reduction | 12 | 15 | 2.06 (1.22–2.94) |

Mean Injury Severity Scores were 2.6 versus 3.4; maximum AIS ≥3 occurred in 2/99 scooter and 17/333 cyclist
cases with known severity. The lack of a significant overall severity difference is not proof of equivalence.[^paper]

The surface/infrastructure subgroup (40 scooters, 66 cyclists) showed stronger proximal-upper-limb differences
(1/40 versus 18/66; adjusted RR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.72). Vehicle-involved subgroups were small (15 scooters,
170 cyclists), with wide intervals for injury-region comparisons. Proposed explanations involving falls, feet,
handlebar position or speed were not directly tested.[^paper]

## Limits and definitions

External injuries (abrasions, contusions, lacerations) were classified separately regardless of body location;
head/neck and limb counts exclude them. Helmet use was 2/99 versus 223/336, but the study does not isolate a
helmet effect. Some severe patients unable to communicate, refusals and after-hours presentations were absent
from the interview sample; scooter incentives and bicycle racing exclusions differed. Age/gender adjustment
does not remove differences in years, experience, trip purpose or incident mechanism.[^paper]
