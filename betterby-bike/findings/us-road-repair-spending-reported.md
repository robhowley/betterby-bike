# Reported US road-repair needs and federal funding are not a complete backlog cost comparison

## In brief

- Streetsblog reports that **16.3% of US federal-aid-eligible roads were in poor condition in 2024**,
  despite **$56.8 billion** in federal transportation funds that year.
- Its **$43.2 billion annual maintenance estimate** is lower than that funding, but this does not
  establish the cost or feasibility of repairing every deficient road and bridge. The underlying
  report and cost assumptions were not independently reviewed.[^wilson-repair2026]

## What the comparison measures

Kea Wilson's May 2026 article attributes condition and spending analysis to Transportation for America's
Repair Priorities 2026. The p. 2 figure labels its outcome as the percentage of federal-aid roads in poor
condition; its caption identifies federal-aid-eligible centerline miles, not the share of all streets,
bridges, lane-miles or travelers. It cites FHWA Highway Statistics tables FA-4, HM-64 and HM-63
(2009–2024), plus 2009 recovery-act implementation guidance.[^wilson-repair2026]

The article also reports 24 states with worse-than-national-average road conditions spending less than
$2.35 on maintenance per dollar of expansion. It quotes concerns about expansion classified as
maintenance or “other” spending. These are reported accounting limitations, not independently quantified
misclassification rates. The article does not explain the ratio threshold's derivation.[^wilson-repair2026]

## Interpretation limits

The annual maintenance estimate is not a documented one-time backlog-removal cost, and federal funding
is not shown to be wholly available for the same assets. Bridge needs, inflation assumptions and the
estimate's asset coverage are not resolved in the article. Road conditions and appropriations plotted
on different axes cannot show what a repair mandate would cause. Rural traffic differences also limit
state comparisons.[^wilson-repair2026]

This informs [street-redesign evaluation](/topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md#design-guidance-is-not-an-outcome-evaluation)
by separating maintenance obligations from evaluated project benefits. It does not quantify money freed
for cycling infrastructure or establish that a particular redesign reduces maintenance costs.
