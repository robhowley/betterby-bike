# Scooter study did not detect non-restaurant spending displacement, but cannot exclude it

## In brief

- In the U.S. scooter-entry card-panel study covering 2017–February 2020, estimated local non-restaurant
  spending change was positive but **not statistically significant** relative to matched non-entry controls.
- The model found no significant compensating decline, but this is not proof that restaurant gains displaced
  no other spending or that scooters generated a positive net economic benefit.[^wheels]

## What was compared

The study compares eventual riders in 49 cities introducing scooters in June–October 2018 with matched people
in 49 cities without programs. Its “local spending excluding in-store restaurants” includes all in-store purchases
plus restaurant and grocery delivery, minus the focal in-store restaurant purchases. It is a constructed card-panel
measure, not a complete set of local business accounts.[^wheels]

Table 5 reports an entry coefficient of **0.03818**, city-clustered SE **0.03081**, and 7,695,765 person-week
observations for this outcome. The corresponding restaurant coefficient is 0.05172 (SE 0.01711, p<0.01).
Both models use log(spending + $1), individual/week fixed effects and city trends. A positive but imprecise
coefficient permits negative effects as well as positive ones; the paper reports no equivalence test that could
establish a negligible decline.[^wheels]

The authors interpret the result as no offset to restaurant gains and suggest any budget-driven reduction may
instead occur in nonlocal categories such as e-commerce. That is an interpretation, not an observed e-commerce
reduction. A pooled outcome can also conceal losses within particular non-restaurant sectors. The study does
not measure complete household budgets, local profits, program costs or injury/environmental externalities.[^wheels]
