# Street-level revenue and employment changes do not measure the same economic response

## In brief

- In the six-city street study, higher revenue need not mean more purchases: prices could rise. Jobs and wages adjust on
  different timescales, limiting their usefulness for measuring immediate customer responses.[^national2020]
- Growth after a redesign need not be a project effect: some apparent gains reflected earlier or regional trends.[^national2020]
- These limits qualify the [retail](national-street-improvements-retail.md) and [food-service
  results](national-street-improvements-food-service.md); they do not prove gains or losses were absent.

## Evidence and limits

The National Street Improvements Study used LEHD employment, QCEW employment/wages, NETS employment/sales and retail
sales tax data to examine 14 improved corridors and 14 control corridors in six U.S. cities.[^national2020]

- **Revenue can rise through volume or prices.** More purchases can increase sales, but so can higher prices,
  potentially responding to rising lease costs. Revenue growth alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms; the report
  offers them as possible explanations, not measured causes on every corridor.
- **Employment and wages adjust on different timescales.** The authors regard them as useful long-term activity
  indicators but ineffective measures of immediate changes in consumer activity following travel-pattern changes.

Model choice also changes attribution. On Seattle's Broadway (2014), observed retail-employment growth may simply
continue a prior trend according to interrupted time series (ITS). On Minneapolis's Riverside Avenue (2009), ITS
attributes retail-employment gains to regional growth rather than the project. A favorable before/after trend is
therefore not automatically a favorable estimated intervention effect.[^national2020]

The authors recommend multiple datasets and methods. They describe ITS as generally more robust than their other
approaches, but requiring more post-intervention observations for meaningful estimates. They recommend comparable
business-district controls and identify sales tax data as most appropriate, when available, for examining employment
and retail-sales trends in tightly defined areas. These are the report's methodological judgments, not demonstrated
universal rankings of estimators or proof that sales tax directly measures jobs.[^national2020]

The summary names aggregated trends, difference-in-differences and ITS but omits model specifications, matching
checks, observation windows and uncertainty estimates. It cannot establish whether their assumptions hold. Agreement
among methods strengthens consistency within this study, not the number of independent studies; disagreement should
remain visible rather than being collapsed into a single positive headline.
