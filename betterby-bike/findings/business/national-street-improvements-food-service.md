# Food-service gains after street improvements were common but not universal

The National Street Improvements Study summary describes **food service as the sector benefiting most** across
14 improved corridors in six U.S. cities. Its before/after analyses used control corridors, multiple employment,
wage and sales datasets, and trend, difference-in-differences and interrupted time series (ITS) methods. The authors
say food sales and employment tended to rise even where projects removed travel lanes or parking; they do not provide
a pooled effect size or isolate removal itself as the cause.[^national2020]

The corridor table reports favorable results including:

- **Indianapolis, Massachusetts Avenue (2009):** significantly positive employment results, with growth outpacing the
  city. **Virginia Avenue (2009):** significant growth across food-service employment, sales and wages.
- **Memphis, Broad Avenue (2010):** positive employment findings supported by trends and ITS in LEHD and QCEW data.
  **Madison Avenue (2011):** an immediate significant employment-level drop followed by recovery above previous levels
  over the next two years, despite the table's overall positive employment interpretation.
- **San Francisco, Polk Street (2000, 2009):** significantly positive food sales-tax receipts relative to Van Ness Avenue.
- **Seattle, Broadway (2014):** a reported significant food-service employment increase after installation.[^national2020]

Important exceptions accompany these gains:

- **San Francisco, 17th Street (2011):** NETS and LEHD differ, but results are generally negative or null for food
  service. The summary does not give metric-specific coefficients or magnitudes.
- **Seattle, Second Avenue (2014):** no statistically significant food-service effect, despite favorable retail results.
- **Minneapolis, Riverside Avenue (2009):** restaurant sales and employment grew, but econometric analyses do not
  clearly attribute the growth to the redesign; the authors call the evidence inconclusive.
- **Portland, Stark/Oak (2009):** positive or null employment/sales results depending on the data; LEHD shows no
  food-employment effect.[^national2020]

This supports a qualified favorable pattern, not a guarantee for restaurants or proof that parking removal increases
sales. The summary lacks observation windows, establishment counts, effect coefficients and uncertainty intervals.
Sector-level sales and employment are not profits, customer counts or outcomes for every existing business. These
corridors and their multiple analyses are one study, not separate independent confirmations.
