# Scooter restaurant-dollar estimates depend on population scaling and permitted-fleet counts

## In brief

- For 49 U.S. cities introducing scooters in June–October 2018, the authors estimate **$11.3 million in additional
  annual spending at 391 tracked restaurant companies** by scaling modeled card-panel effects to the population.
- Their **$179.1 annually per permitted scooter** uses a 32-city subset with 58,380 permitted devices, not actual
  deployed scooters, rides or an effect caused by adding one device.
- Representativeness assumptions can raise or lower the true population effect. These figures are neither a
  guaranteed lower bound nor measured net economic or municipal returns.[^wheels]

## Calculation and denominators

The authors predict treated individuals' post-entry spending with the entry indicator switched on versus off,
keeping fitted parameters fixed, and sum the difference across one post-entry year. They then multiply panel
uplift by **24.58**, because the panel represents about **4.1% of the working-age-and-older population** in the
49 treated cities. Scaling assumes representative panel effects and no effect on nonusers.[^wheels]

For the per-device calculation, the numerator is population-scaled uplift in the **32 cities with available
permit data**, not all 49 cities' $11.3 million. Appendix I uses each city's maximum available permitted fleet
count during observation when multiple counts exist. Permission is not actual deployment or device utilization;
the ratio is not a marginal causal return on another scooter.[^wheels]

The 36-public-brand sales comparison supports similarity of sales trends, not representativeness of all residents,
riders or treatment effects. Coverage excludes untracked restaurants, but that alone does not make the estimate
a proven lower bound: the paper itself acknowledges that violated scaling assumptions could move it either way.
No city-specific confidence intervals for these dollar illustrations are supplied.[^wheels]

## Dallas illustration is not an audited benefit-cost analysis

The authors estimate $423,000 annual restaurant sales lift for Dallas. Applying their stated 8.25% sales-tax
rate yields about $34,896; applying an average **22.3% contribution margin** from selected public restaurant
companies yields about $94,000 in contribution profit. These are modeled amounts, not observed city collections
or restaurant profits. The manuscript does not establish what share of the stated tax rate accrues to the city.
Its $67,848 in 2019 scooter fees serves as a rollout-cost proxy, not a complete cost ledger.[^wheels]

The reported 51.4% tax-to-fee and roughly 139% profit-to-fee comparisons therefore cannot establish fiscal
payback or overall social benefit. Contribution profit is not net profit, fees are transfers as well as a cost
proxy, and other costs and externalities are outside these calculations.
