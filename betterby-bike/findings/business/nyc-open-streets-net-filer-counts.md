# Restaurant and bar filer counts grew more or fell less on five NYC Open Streets

## In brief

- In NYC DOT's March 2020–August 2021 pandemic study, restaurant/bar tax-filer counts rose 4–18% on four
  selected Open Streets but fell 8% in Chinatown; every corridor did better than its control.[^recovery]
- Park Slope grew 18% versus a 24% control decline; Chinatown's 8% decline was smaller than its control's 29%.
- These are net counts after closures, not gross openings, and do not establish citywide business creation
  caused by Open Streets.

## Evidence

The chart compares filer counts with a March 1, 2017–February 29, 2020 pre-pandemic baseline. It labels the
study period through August 31, 2021 but does not separately specify the exact count-averaging rule.[^recovery]

| Neighborhood | Open Street net change | Control net change | Borough net change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Astoria | +18% | 0% | −5% |
| Park Slope | +18% | −24% | −7% |
| Prospect Heights | +16% | −24% | −7% |
| Koreatown | +4% | −25% | −11% |
| Chinatown | −8% | −29% | −11% |

The executive summary says Open Streets saw a 10% increase in “new businesses” versus a 20% contraction on
controls. The detailed discussion explicitly subtracts closures, so this headline must not be read as a gross
entry rate. “All” outperforming controls does not mean all gained businesses: Chinatown contracted.[^recovery]

## Limits

No absolute filer counts, confidence intervals or detailed adjustment methods are supplied. Areas needed at
least ten filers each quarter to protect confidentiality, excluding smaller clusters. Nonrandom corridor
selection, differences in outdoor-dining participation and uneven pandemic recovery limit attribution.
Filer counts do not measure employment, profitability, business quality or net creation across the city;
new activity could reflect relocation. These outcomes concern restaurants/bars, not every retailer or
all Open Streets configurations.
