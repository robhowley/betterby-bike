# Chicago transit model's 13-to-1 comparison is an illustrative valuation, not marginal return

## In brief

- A complete-transit-removal Chicago metropolitan simulation values annual losses at $35.4 billion, including
  $20.4 billion from an imposed 1.9-million-car ownership increase, not observed household purchases.
- Dividing by $2.7 billion in annual transit operating funding yields roughly 13:1. This is not evidence
  that each additional transit dollar produces $13, nor a measured GDP or public-budget return.[^verbas2024]

## What is counted

| Component | Annual reported value | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spending lost through activity cancellations | $4.6 billion | $2.4 billion entertainment, $1.6 billion restaurants, $0.6 billion sales |
| Net valued travel-time loss | $10.4 billion | $15.1 billion highway loss less $4.7 billion removed transit-time value |
| Additional car ownership | $20.4 billion | 1.9 million assumed extra cars at $10,728 annually |
| Total | $35.4 billion | Sum of the three illustrative components |

The spending calculation applies modeled reductions to BLS 2021–2022 Chicago household expenditure categories
and 3.8 million households. Shopping and errands map to apparel/services, leisure to entertainment and eating
out to food away from home. It assumes corresponding spending reductions, not directly observed receipts or
modeled substitution to other purchases or delivery.[^verbas2024]

Time valuation uses 1.3 million additional highway vehicle-hours/day, occupancy 1.48, $30/person-hour and
261 weekdays. It subtracts 0.7 million removed transit passenger-hours/day at $25.50/hour over 261 weekdays.
The car calculation uses the cited 2022 AAA cost rather than the paper's higher 2023 figure. Its applicability
to all assumed additional vehicles is not tested. The $2.7 billion comparator is annual operating funding
including federal relief, not the cost of a specified investment package.[^verbas2024]

## Limits

The combined value is a scenario accounting exercise, not a comprehensive welfare or traditional economic-impact
analysis. Spending, household ownership costs and monetized time are different measures; none establishes a
net GDP loss or fiscal payback. The result is particularly dependent on assumed vehicle purchases. No sensitivity
range or uncertainty interval is given. Do not transfer the ratio to cycling infrastructure, incremental transit
funding, service cuts or other regions. The authors identify traditional economic-impact analysis and gradual
service decline as further work.[^verbas2024]
