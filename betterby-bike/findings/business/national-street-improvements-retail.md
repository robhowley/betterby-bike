# Six-city street study reported retail gains alongside mixed employment results

## In brief

- A six-city U.S. study summary classified 14 improved corridors as nine with positive retail sales and/or employment
  results, two with no impact and three mixed.[^national2020]
- Sales and jobs sometimes diverged, and some gains reflected existing trends. The tally is not a percentage sales gain
  or national success rate.
- The study excluded car-oriented businesses. Its combined street projects do not isolate bike-lane effects, and the
  summary gives no effect sizes.

## Evidence and limits

The National Street Improvements Study compared before/after activity with control corridors using trend analysis,
difference-in-differences and interrupted time series (ITS). The 9/14 tally is the authors' case classification,
not a count of universally positive metrics.[^national2020]

Selected results show why sales and jobs must remain distinct:

| Corridor (listed improvement year) | Reported retail result |
| --- | --- |
| Lyndale Avenue South, Minneapolis (2008) | All three approaches indicate significantly positive retail-sales effects. |
| Stark/Oak, Portland (2009) | Trend and ITS analyses of QCEW and NETS data consistently indicate significantly positive employment and sales results. |
| Madison Avenue, Memphis (2011) | Significantly positive sales; employment rose slightly then fell, performing worse than two of three controls and the city, suggesting a possible negative employment effect. |
| Broad Avenue, Memphis (2010) | Data sources disagree; retail sales/employment evidence is inconclusive. |
| Riverside Avenue, Minneapolis (2009) | Employment rose but ITS attributes it to regional growth; retail sales fell while restaurant sales rose. Overall retail analysis is inconclusive. |
| Massachusetts and Virginia Avenues, Indianapolis (2009) | No significant retail-employment effect; zero employment in some years prevents growth-rate calculation on Massachusetts Avenue. |
| North Second Street, Minneapolis (2011) | No causal employment effect reported; a large sales jump could reflect a one-time change such as a new store opening. |
| Broadway, Seattle (2014) | Employment growth could continue a pre-existing trend rather than reflect the protected lane. |

These results come from the summary's corridor table, not independently reviewed model output. San Francisco's Polk
Street also illustrates dataset dependence: NETS and sales records give positive significant results, while LEHD does
not. Minneapolis's Franklin Avenue has faster apparent employment/wage growth but a non-significant ITS employment
relationship.[^national2020]

The summary provides no effect sizes, confidence intervals, establishment counts or full observation windows. Its
positive-or-non-significant overview should not erase Madison's adverse employment signal or the mixed corridors.
The results concern selected combined street projects, not isolated bike-lane effects, profits or business survival.
