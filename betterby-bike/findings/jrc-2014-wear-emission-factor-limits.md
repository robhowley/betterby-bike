# Historical brake and tyre emission factors are not universal project coefficients

## In brief

- JRC's 2014 review tables report average PM10 emission factors of **6.7 mg per vehicle-km for brakes** and
  **6.3 mg per vehicle-km for tyres**, for light-duty/passenger vehicles. These summarize heterogeneous
  historical studies and inventories, not a universal present-day vehicle factor.[^jrc2014]
- Brake factors vary strongly with setting, while tyre-source detection can fail. Internal text-table
  disagreements further prevent selecting one precise coefficient for a cycling-project benefit.

## What the review reports

In its account of Swiss receptor modelling, light-duty brake PM10 factors are **8.0 ± 4.0 mg per vehicle-km**
in Zürich-Weststrasse's urban street canyon versus **1.6 ± 1.1** at Reiden's highway site. Heavy-duty values
are 81 ± 39 and 9.0 ± 7.0 respectively. These are separate site estimates, not a before/after intervention;
the review passage does not define the ± statistic. Braking frequency is the authors' explanation, not an
isolated causal effect of street design.[^jrc2014]

Tables 5 and 10 combine inventories, laboratory measurements and receptor models. Their stated averages lack
a reported pooled uncertainty measure and should not be treated as a meta-analysis. Section 3.5 reports no
detectable tyre contribution in some studies, discussing electrostatic sampling loss and nonunique chemical
tracers as possible explanations. A nondetection does not establish that tyre wear emits no PM10.

The section 3.5 text assigns Panko et al. (2013) 7.0 mg per vehicle-km, while Table 10 assigns 2.4. The
executive summary gives brake/tyre ranges of 2.0–8.8 and 3.5–9.0; conclusions instead give 1.0–9.0 and
4.0–13.0, and section 2.5 includes still larger roadside estimates. These discrepancies remain unresolved,
so the summary range cannot be read as an exhaustive bound.[^jrc2014]

## Use and limits

Mass emitted per vehicle-km differs from total wear mass, ambient concentration, inhaled dose and health loss.
Multiplying a historical factor by assumed displaced driving does not itself demonstrate actual travel change,
local exposure reduction or a net health benefit. Fleet composition, braking, road/tyre materials and measurement
methods need explicit assumptions. This review does not compare present electric and combustion fleets.
Underlying studies were read only through the review, not independently checked.
