# Better by Bike

Research summaries and findings for understanding cycling and street design. Current coverage includes historical New
Zealand injury trends and a 2012 secondary evaluation of New Zealand's all-age helmet law, a Montreal cycle-track
comparison, NYC green-lane behavior observations, FHWA's 2011 green-pavement
interim approval, a NYC mayoral memo's
attributed policy claims, NYCDOT's 2012 street-redesign case studies, a Toronto–Vancouver within-trip infrastructure
study, a 2014 five-city U.S. protected-lane evaluation, NYCDOT's 2014 Manhattan protected-lane analysis and ULI's 2016
active-transportation and real-estate profiles, FHWA's New Jersey road-diet emergency-access accounts, a 2024 Iowa
responder survey and Cedar Rapids response-rate analysis, and Seville's
citywide network, trip and collision-risk time series, plus Chicago's sharrow block-group injury comparison and a
six-city summary of economic outcomes on 14 improved corridors and PeopleForBikes/Alliance business interviews and
secondary economic research, CityLab's Portland customer-spending, Seattle retail-sales and six-corridor sidewalk-riding
coverage and its 2015 multi-city business roundup (including adverse Vancouver reports and untested conversion
predictions), Vox's secondary account of NYCDOT's 2014 traffic and safety results, plus an
Asbury Park temporary-lane speed
study and Streetsblog's coverage of U.S. Census commuting patterns by household income and vehicle availability,
plus its Broadway redesign reporting on sales, merchant responses and bicycle traffic (overlapping ULI's municipal
case), and Copenhagen Post's 2018 municipal-review coverage of commuting, economic valuations and infrastructure
proposals, plus WEF's Copenhagen account of trip-purpose shares, perceived safety and retail spending (repeating
those economic values), and CBC's 2019 economic roundup with later Bloor reporting, Delaware path proximity values
and project jobs per dollar, plus Streetsblog's 2019 FDNY response-time reporting and competing explanations
without an identified bike-lane effect, and its reported 12-city protected-lane casualty association with
painted-lane null reporting and equity caveats, plus Urban Institute's 2022 account of protected construction in
13 cities, Pittsburgh connectivity and Los Angeles library proximity, plus NYC DOT's 2023 East New York project
announcement and all-user/senior-pedestrian serious-casualty claims without supporting analysis, plus the Boston
Globe's 2024 mixed corridor counts and rider accounts of comfort and network gaps, plus Business Insider's 2024
business review with mixed San Francisco sales and Los Angeles merchant/customer arrival comparisons, overlapping
earlier Seattle, NYC and national-study evidence, plus NerdWallet's U.S. new-car cost estimates and car-cost
inflation index, without measured cycling savings; it is not a comprehensive review.

## Topics

- [Interpreting cycling injury risk](topics/cycling-injury-risk.md): exposure, severity, crash mechanisms, behavioral
  measures, local turning speeds, short conflict observations, policy claims and limits on causal interpretation.
- [Evaluating street redesign outcomes](topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md): safety, business, bicycle volumes,
  comfort, mixed driving/walking experiences, emergency-access accounts, Iowa response evidence and six-city economic
  comparisons, without isolating bike-lane effects; customer spending, merchant perceptions and employer accounts remain
  distinct measures, as do local traffic calming and corridor travel times; per-kilometre economic valuations do not
  establish project returns; construction and connectivity measures do not establish travel or safety benefits;
  Boston's short fall counts and experienced-rider accounts do not establish lane effects or beginner adoption;
  mixed San Francisco business results qualify universal no-harm claims without isolating lane effects.
- [Active transportation and real estate](topics/active-transportation-real-estate.md): property-value associations,
  development accounts and car-optional housing, without assuming causal premiums or affordability benefits.
- [Who walks and bikes to work?](topics/active-commuting-and-access.md): historical household-income and
  vehicle-availability patterns and Copenhagen's reported commute and trip-purpose shares,
  with limits on comparing denominators and
  measuring all active travel and access quality; library proximity does not establish usable or equitable routes,
  and car ownership costs do not establish savings from cycling.
