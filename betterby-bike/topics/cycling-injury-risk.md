# Interpreting cycling injury risk

Injury counts, rates per hour or kilometre, and life-threatening injury rates answer different questions. This bundle
contains a historical New Zealand population study and a Montreal comparison of six two-way cycle tracks with streets
without bicycle facilities, plus a Toronto–Vancouver within-trip infrastructure study. It also includes a New York City
green-paint evaluation of motorist behavior, not injury risk. These studies address different questions, not independent
confirmations of one effect.[^nz2010][^lusk2011][^teschke2012][^nyc2011]

## What the evidence supports

[Exposure-based rates](/findings/safety/nz-cycling-injury-rates-1988-2007.md) show that cycling deaths and inpatient
injuries per million hours increased between 1996–99 and 2003–07 while rates for the other travel modes studied
declined. Cycling's annual case count in 2003–07 was nevertheless lower than in 1988–91. Serious cycling injuries
increased after 1996–99 but remained below the earliest period. Neither counts alone nor one comparison interval fully
describes this history.

[Crash mechanisms and injury profiles](/findings/safety/nz-cycling-crash-mechanisms-2003-2007.md) show why cycling
injury prevention is not solely about collisions with cars. Non-collision crashes made up about two-fifths of death and
inpatient cases, while cars, pick-ups and vans accounted for a larger share of serious cases than of all cases. These
shares describe recorded casualties, not the probability of injury in each crash environment.

Severity also changes subgroup comparisons.
[Children aged 5–14 had the highest overall rate](/findings/safety/nz-child-cycling-injury-severity.md), especially in
crashes without motor vehicles, but the same pattern did not hold for serious injuries. A broad injury measure should
not be presented as a measure of life-threatening harm.

## Route comparisons need matched measures

[Montreal's cycle tracks had lower pooled injury risk](/findings/safety/montreal-cycle-track-relative-injury-risk.md)
than nearby reference streets (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.60–0.85). Three routes had significantly lower risk; three did not
differ significantly, including one with a higher point estimate. This supports a favourable pooled association in the
studied routes, not a guarantee for every track or a causal estimate of installing one. Background traffic danger
differed between route pairs, and short 2009 cyclist counts supplied relative exposure for injuries recorded in
1999–2008. The approximately 2.5-fold track use did not measure growth caused by construction.[^lusk2011]

[Absolute Montreal rates](/findings/safety/montreal-cycle-track-absolute-rates.md), 8.5 EMR injuries and 10.5
police-recorded bicycle–motor vehicle crashes per million estimated bicycle-kilometres, cannot be directly ranked
against New Zealand deaths and inpatient injuries per million hours. Both the event definition and denominator differ;
Montreal also lacked injury-severity data. Neither study alone establishes effects on fatal or life-threatening injuries
from protected infrastructure.

Event coverage matters as well. A
[reanalysis of published Palo Alto sidewalk-bikeway data](/findings/safety/intersection-only-sidewalk-risk-reanalysis.md)
changed the overall risk comparison when assumed non-intersection roadway crashes were added. That is a sensitivity
demonstration using a city-wide crash proportion, not another independent cycle-track study or proof that sidewalks and
cycle tracks are interchangeable.

## Within-trip comparisons strengthen matching, not causal attribution

The Toronto–Vancouver study matched each of 690 adult emergency-department injury sites in 2008–09 with a random point
along that rider's trip. This controls stable rider and trip characteristics and samples exposure in proportion to
route length. [Cycle tracks had adjusted OR 0.11 (95% CI 0.02–0.54)](/findings/safety/toronto-vancouver-cycle-track-injury-odds.md)
relative to major streets with parking and no bicycle infrastructure, but with only two injury and ten control sites.
This independently supports a favorable cycle-track association alongside Montreal, not a shared effect size: Montreal
used EMR records, different comparison streets and separately counted exposure. Neither design measures the causal
change from installing a track.[^teschke2012]

[Other route comparisons](/findings/safety/toronto-vancouver-route-type-comparisons.md) favor local streets and major
streets without parking or bike infrastructure. Adjusted estimates for painted lanes, shared lanes, traffic-calmed local
routes, sidewalks and other paths were inconclusive. These results do not justify treating all off-street facilities as
one intervention, claiming every lower point estimate proves benefit, or interpreting traffic-calmed versus uncalmed
point estimates as a test of adding traffic calming.[^teschke2012]

[Rail tracks, downhill grades and construction](/findings/safety/toronto-vancouver-route-hazards.md) had higher adjusted
injury odds. This extends the prevention question beyond motor-vehicle collisions: the study included falls and other
crashes. However, post-injury site observations may misclassify temporary conditions, and exclusions of children, fatal
cases and riders unable to recall their trip limit conclusions about those groups. Its aggregate inconclusive
intersection association does not resolve individual intersection-design risks.[^teschke2012]

## Behavior is not an injury outcome

[NYC's green-lane observations](/findings/design/nyc-green-paint-driver-behavior.md) reported less driving on or inside
lane boundaries overall, but comparable standing/parking and mixed site-level results. This September 2010 comparison
concerns unprotected painted lanes, not the physically separated Montreal tracks. It offers evidence about compliance,
not an estimate of injury reduction.[^nyc2011]

The report's [measurement limits](/findings/design/nyc-green-paint-measurement-limits.md) include inconsistent aggregate
totals, differing curbside layouts and no treatment-specific injury analysis. Concurrent citywide cycling growth and
falling injuries do not isolate green paint's effect; the report also could not separate its ridership contribution from
network expansion. Thus, neither favorable behavior observations nor broad city trends should be substituted for matched
injury and exposure measures.

## Short conflict observations and perceived safety are not injury rates

The [2014 Green Lane evaluation](/findings/safety/green-lanes-conflicts-and-risk-limits.md) found no collisions or
near collisions in 144 hours of 2013 intersection video covering nearly 12,900 bicycles. Low-level conflicts did occur,
and riders separately reported collisions outside that window. This does not conflict with Montreal's nonzero injury
rates: observation periods, event definitions, sites and denominators differ. With no matched before/after injury
comparison, zero observed collisions cannot establish zero risk or an installation effect.[^monsere2014]

The same study's favorable [buffer-comfort ratings](/findings/design/green-lanes-buffer-comfort.md) and perceived
safety gains may help explain route appeal, but are not injury outcomes. Its
[intersection behavior findings](/findings/design/green-lanes-intersection-behavior.md) reveal positioning differences
and signal violations even where conflicting movements were separated by signals. These are useful design observations,
not independent estimates of injury reduction or proof of one universally safest intersection design.[^monsere2014]

## Policy claims need supporting analyses

[NYC's March 2011 mayoral memo](/findings/safety/nyc-wolfson-protected-lane-crash-claims.md) reports that protected
lanes typically reduce injury crashes for all road users by 40%, with reductions above 50% at some locations. Unlike the
Montreal comparison, it provides no sites, periods, counts, exposure or uncertainty. This is an attributed policy claim,
not a cyclist-specific risk estimate or an independently documented installation effect. Its attribution of falling
citywide cycling casualties to the network also lacks a causal analysis.[^wolfson2011]

The memo's
[four pedestrian deaths in bike collisions in 2001–05 versus three in 2006–10](/findings/safety/nyc-wolfson-pedestrian-fatality-counts.md)
concern citywide fatalities, not lane-specific crashes. Small counts, absent exposure and no nonfatal injury data
prevent interpreting the difference as proof of reduced pedestrian risk. Neither set of memo figures should be treated
as independent confirmation of Montreal's result or of green paint's effectiveness.[^wolfson2011]

## Site-specific injury changes are not cyclist risk estimates

[NYCDOT's 2012 report gives 35% and 58% fewer injuries to all street users on 8th and 9th Avenues](/findings/safety/nyc-eighth-ninth-injury-changes.md).
Unlike the 2011 memo, it names locations, but still omits observation periods, baseline counts, exposure, uncertainty
and controlled comparisons. The projects combine protected bicycle lanes with pedestrian and turn/signal changes. These
injury figures are neither the memo's injury-crash measure nor Montreal's cyclist-specific exposure-based risk, and
their independence from the memo's underlying data is unknown.[^nyc2012]

[Evaluating street redesign outcomes](/topics/street-redesign-outcomes.md) places these safety changes alongside
business and mobility measures without attributing every change to bicycle lanes.

## What remains unresolved

The New Zealand study does not establish why cycling injury rates changed. Its safety-in-numbers discussion is a
possible explanation, not a measured causal effect. Likewise, the
[decline in traumatic brain injury](/findings/safety/nz-brain-injury-trends-helmet-law.md) does not isolate an effect of
the 1994 helmet law from wider road-injury trends.

Cross-mode comparisons use travel hours, do not control for road types used and do not assess net health benefits.
Coding, admission practices and uncertain travel-survey exposure limit comparisons; injuries outside inpatient care are
missing. These New Zealand results cannot supply current risk estimates or demonstrate the effects of protected lanes,
traffic calming, road-surface improvements or training. The authors recommend prevention alongside cycling promotion,
but this study did not test those interventions.[^nz2010]

Montreal adds a direct infrastructure comparison, but its six two-way tracks cannot identify which design features
explain safety differences or establish effects for one-way tracks. The authors recommended not discouraging cycle-track
construction; that recommendation is broader than the measured route-specific associations.[^lusk2011]

The Toronto–Vancouver study likewise did not distinguish one-way from two-way cycle tracks or measure ridership growth.
Its authors' recommendations for safer infrastructure and construction detours go beyond the observational comparisons;
these interventions were not tested.[^teschke2012]
