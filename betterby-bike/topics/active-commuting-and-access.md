# Who walks and bikes to work?

Historical U.S. commuting evidence challenges the idea that walking and cycling are mainly affluent recreational
activities. It does not establish whether people have good transport choices or how the benefits of new
infrastructure would be distributed.

## Usable alternatives and changing habits are different questions

[Slate's commuting-intervention review](../findings/networks/commuting-intervention-timing-reported.md)
adds mixed behavioral evidence to the population shares below: workplace commuting tools reportedly
failed, while free transit for recent movers or job changers and information before UCLA student arrival
reportedly increased transit use. Different programs and populations do not establish a causal ranking
of timing strategies, and the article supplies no effect sizes or persistence estimates. These are not
measured cycling gains. Zipper's proposed transit/bikeshare welcome packages remain proposals; Clark's
recommendation to combine behavioral support with good alternatives does not make safe routes or
frequent service optional. Aggregate commute shares cannot identify these habit-change mechanisms.

## Funding votes do not establish delivered access

[Reported 2024 U.S. transit ballot results](../findings/access/us-2024-reported-transit-ballot-approvals.md)
add political and funding context, not observed travel behavior: Streetsblog reports 46 of 53 annual
initiatives passing and separately $24.9 billion approved across 18 November measures. These counts
cover measures put to voters, not national public opinion. Packages including sidewalks and bike lanes
also fund other purposes, so their totals are not active-travel allocations. Local defeats and a
nullified San Francisco approval qualify the aggregate success account. Unlike evidence of destination
access or ridership, authorization alone says nothing about delivery, usability or who benefits;
the underlying election inventory and its treatment of the San Francisco correction remain unreviewed.

## Purchase intentions do not establish usable alternatives

[Newsweek's May 2025 survey coverage](../findings/access/us-2025-car-ownership-alternative-intentions.md)
adds stated preferences to the ownership-cost evidence below: 48% of Harris/Turo respondents were open
to alternatives to buying or leasing, and 42% of surveyed owners expected financial relief from giving
up ownership. Car sharing and rentals can preserve driving, while weaker purchase intentions can mean
postponement rather than relinquishing a car. These measures do not establish cycling demand, realized
savings or access to destinations. Missing survey methods and the car-sharing sponsor's commercial
interest further limit interpretation; the underlying surveys remain unreviewed. This is different
evidence from AAA's standardized costs, not independent confirmation of an affordability benefit.

## Lower youth licensing need not mean less driving distance

[Streetsblog's account of U.S. youth travel in 2009–2017](../findings/access/us-youth-driving-trips-distance-reported.md)
adds observed-behavior reporting rather than purchase intentions: median weekly driving trips reportedly
fell 16% while median weekly driving distance rose nearly 12%. Lower licensing and fewer outings do
not demonstrate substitution toward cycling or transit, and may coexist with longer remaining journeys
or reduced participation. The article's exact trip/distance denominators and underlying study methods
remain unreviewed. Wang's recommendation to improve nearby destinations and transit is consistent with
the access question, but is not an evaluated effect. Neither historical licensing trends nor current
ownership preferences establish that young people have usable alternatives.

[SSTI's coverage of Fung et al.'s 2001–2022 NHTS analysis](../findings/access/us-youth-personal-travel-vmt-reported.md)
adds a different age group and period: its chart covers ages 15–29, with reported personal mileage
41% lower in 2022 than 2017 despite roughly unchanged youth VMT in the prose. Missing VMT denominators
prevent reconciliation; personal mileage is not vehicle mileage. Realtor.com's housing coverage repeats
this same UCLA study rather than confirming it independently. Together these accounts reinforce the
need to separate trip frequency, personal distance and driving distance before inferring active-travel
substitution, without establishing that fewer outings improved access.

## Social participation is a separate outcome

[Streetsblog's German research coverage](../findings/germany-cycling-civic-participation-reported.md) adds
reported small positive associations between cycling frequency and social-organizational and political
participation, plus positive helpfulness and solidarity results. Car-use frequency had no significant effect
in the reported models, not a measured negative effect. These are social outcomes, unlike the travel-use and
access measures below. They do not establish that cyclists have better morals, that transport access improved,
or that building lanes causes civic engagement. Missing methods and effect sizes in the news account leave
selection and magnitude unresolved; the underlying study has not been independently reviewed.

## Pandemic commute shares are not all-trip cycling trends

The Census Bureau's [2019–2022 ACS comparison](../findings/networks/us-2019-2022-active-commute-share.md)
puts combined walking/bicycling at 2.9% of U.S. workers in 2022, above 2021's 2.6% but below 2019's 3.2%.
Unlike the older income and vehicle-availability comparisons below, this is a directly reviewed national
publication, not another account of the 2014 report. It does not update those demographic associations.

Transit and solo-driving shares also rose between 2021 and 2022 while home-based work fell. All-worker
shares therefore cannot be read as direct substitution between cycling and driving. The combined category
cannot show bicycle-only recovery, and [usual-mode reporting](../findings/networks/acs-active-commuting-measurement-limits.md)
omits other travel and less-frequent home working. These results describe partial commuting recovery, not
all-purpose cycling demand, improved access or an infrastructure effect.

## Trip purposes and distances reveal what commute shares leave out

ORNL's [2017 NHTS tabulations](../sources/2022-transportation-energy-data-book-40.md) give a separate
national measure: work accounts for [20% of bicycle person-trips and 6% of walking person-trips](../findings/networks/us-2017-active-travel-trip-purposes.md).
These percentages describe purposes within each mode, not the share of workers using it. Shopping and
social/recreational trips make work-only indicators incomplete measures of active travel. They do not
establish unmet demand or independently confirm the older NHTS inputs used in Washington's estimation study.

[Work-trip averages](../findings/networks/us-2017-active-commute-distance-duration.md) also differ sharply:
2.72 miles by bicycle versus 12.21 by car, with mean durations of 21.79 and 25.47 minutes. These are
different journeys, not evidence that switching a given car commute to cycling costs only a few minutes.
Without route, distance-distribution and access information, they cannot estimate feasible mode shift.
The survey's changed distance-mapping method further limits comparisons over time. The data book's
inconsistent ACS year/source labels are not used to extend the commute-share trend above.

## Observed use differs from access quality

In the 2008–2012 Census comparisons reported by Streetsblog, workers in the
[lowest household-income category](../findings/access/us-household-income-active-commuting.md) had the highest
walking and bicycle commute rates. Rates fell through much of the income range, then rose somewhat at the top.
Workers with [no available vehicle](../findings/access/us-vehicle-availability-active-commuting.md) also had much
higher active-commuting rates than those with one vehicle.

These are two associations from the same Census report, accessed through one news article, not independent studies.
Neither separates income from vehicle availability, location or other influences. High use can coexist with limited
alternatives; it is not evidence of safe routes, affordable journeys or satisfied travel preferences. Snyder's case
for serving low-income users with safe infrastructure is a policy interpretation, not a measured distribution of
infrastructure benefits.

London adds a contrasting measure, not a direct rebuttal of the U.S. income pattern. TfL's
[annual-cyclist profiles](../findings/access/london-cycling-survey-profile-limits.md) put people in households below
£20,000 at about 15% of annual cyclists versus 25% of the population in its LTDS account. This is the composition
of riders, not a within-income-group commute rate. Its household and attitudes surveys also disagree materially
on minority-ethnicity representation (about 25% versus 36% of annual cyclists, against a 37% population share).
Small cycling samples and unreconciled survey bases rule out treating these as one precise, independently
confirmed access gap. Neither survey estimates the distribution of new infrastructure benefits.

TfL's later [2021/22 weekly profile](../findings/access/london-2021-cycling-profile-denominators.md) found women
were 34% of past-week cyclists and retained lower-income underrepresentation. A separate 2021 survey found no
significant ethnicity differences in past-year cycling. These do not cancel each other out: rider composition,
within-group participation, weekly and annual recall are different measures. Pandemic telephone interviewing
also limits historical LTDS comparisons. More cycling therefore does not by itself establish broader inclusion.

NABSA's [shared-micromobility user profiles](../findings/access/nabsa-user-representation-gaps.md) add a distinct
population: pooled 2019–2021 city surveys, not all cyclists or commuters. Households above $100,000 were
on average 20 percentage points overrepresented among users, while the $15,000–$49,999 band was 18 points
underrepresented. These composition gaps do not contradict low-income workers' higher within-group active-commute
rates. City selection, category coverage and omitted groups limit inference, and the simultaneous presence of
discount programs does not demonstrate that access gaps narrowed because of them.

Hoboken's undated [carless-household comparison](../findings/access/hoboken-carless-household-comparison.md)
adds household circumstances, not another commute measure: the plan excerpt reports 15.7% in the city versus
13.7% in Hudson County and 12.5% in North Jersey. These are shares of households without cars, unlike the Census
comparison's commute rates within vehicle-availability groups. The excerpt supplies neither observation years nor
data methods, so it does not establish present conditions or independent confirmation of the commuting evidence.
Its call to target green infrastructure toward flood-prone housing communities is a planning judgment, not measured
flood protection or transport-access improvement. It does not locate carless households within those communities.

## Assistance and pricing affect usable bikeshare access

[Streetsblog's July 2025 Citi Bike interviews](../sources/2025-streetsblog-citi-bike-speed-cap.md) add
service-quality concerns to the distinction between shared-fleet use and usable access. A rider who relied
on electric assistance after serious illness described the 18-to-15-mph cap reduction as making riding in
motor traffic feel less safe. Other riders also expressed discomfort, but selected accounts do not establish
increased crash risk, widespread dissatisfaction or actual membership losses.

Minute-based pricing creates a possible cost consequence distinct from safety: the article's hypothetical
six-mile trip takes 20 versus 24 minutes if average speed equals the respective cap, increasing the time
charge by 20%. Real journeys include stops and lower-speed segments; its quoted dollar totals also exceed
the stated rate's simple calculation without explanation. Neither the illustration nor a researcher's
prediction of reduced attractiveness measures actual fares, ridership change or equitable access. The
reporting supports considering assistance, comfort and pricing together, not a conclusion about which
cap is safest or how many users were excluded.

## Transit coverage and usable service differ

[Schmitt's 2026 Wheeling accounts](../findings/access/wheeling-reported-sidewalk-transit-gaps.md)
show why destination location and sidewalk connections belong beside service availability in the access
question: older women credited transit with independence, yet she reports outlying retail and Ohio-side
social-service agencies beyond bus routes without sidewalk connections. Agencies reportedly arranged
expensive transportation themselves. Unlike the national older-adult participation estimate below, these
are qualitative local accounts without sample sizes, route inventories or cost measurements. They do not
quantify unmet need or show that sidewalks alone would solve it. Her proposal for highway-scale sidewalk
funding is advocacy, not an evaluated access gain or a demonstrated transfer of highway budgets.

[Barillas's 2022 Transbay commute account](../sources/2022-barillas-bay-bridge-bus-lane.md)
illustrates another gap between route availability and practical access. Direct F/NL buses served his
East Bay homes, but he reports switching to BART when bridge congestion made later bus departures
unattractive, despite a longer station walk. His prediction that a bus lane would raise his own bus use
from roughly 60% to 100% is hypothetical, not measured mode shift. This complements the Seattle account
below without establishing how common either constraint is. The bus-lane proposal supplies no evaluated
travel-time, equity or congestion benefit, and its international examples cannot quantify Bay Bridge effects.

[Sixth Tone's May 2025 Shanghai reporting](../sources/2025-sixth-tone-shanghai-customized-buses.md)
adds an implemented response to route gaps: residents propose customized buses, typically requiring
15–20 passengers per trip to launch. More than 220 routes reportedly existed across all 16 districts;
this is not a count of routes created by the platform opened the previous day. Unlike the Wheeling
and Transbay accounts, this describes a way to aggregate requests, not just a service constraint.
Demand thresholds and market fares without student or senior discounts mean customization alone does
not establish equitable access. An operator's reported DZ301 daily flow of 250–260 people lacks a
counting period and unique-rider definition, so it cannot show new transit users or reduced driving.
Officials acknowledge uneven demand, low awareness and manual planning; neither faster approval nor
better resource use is evaluated. No bicycle-access effect is measured.

[Swan's May 2026 BART reporting](../sources/2026-sf-chronicle-bart-fare-gates.md) adds station entry as a
separate access question. Faster-closing gates were being tested to prevent tailgating, but BART had not
collected or shared piggybacking data. A laboratory demonstration and one unstruck scooter rider do not
establish reduced evasion or safe passage for people with bicycles or mobility aids. Reported maintenance
savings concerned the earlier gate installation, not the faster-closing upgrade. Route availability and
station security claims therefore remain distinct from demonstrated accessible entry.

## Stop inventories and maintenance affect usable access

[Next City's March 2026 reporting](../sources/2026-next-city-bus-stop-data.md) adds a distinction between
having nearby transit and being able to approach and wait at a stop. An
[MBTA inventory](../findings/access/mbta-reported-stop-accessibility-inventory.md) reportedly identified
280 completely inaccessible stops among roughly 7,000 and led to remediation of most, including attention
to adjacent crossings and curb ramps. This adds an implementation account to the older-adult amenity
concerns below, not independent evidence about transit participation or a measured rider-access gain.

The same reporting describes NYC snow-clearance tracking and rider feedback informing Santa Monica
cleaning and Vancouver lighting priorities. These accounts show why maintenance and divided municipal
responsibility matter alongside service coverage. Proposed GTFS amenity fields could help share information,
but were still early-stage at publication; LA Metro's reportedly nonpublic stop analysis illustrates that
collecting data does not guarantee public access or operational use. Neither inventories nor a common data
format establish equitable improvements, completed trips or substitution away from driving.

## Cycling can conceal poor alternatives

[Fucoloro's 2025 Seattle-area account](../sources/2025-streetsblog-week-without-driving-or-biking.md)
illustrates a distinction the commute statistics cannot measure: a car-free cyclist can have convenient
bicycle trips but poor alternatives when cycling is unavailable. He describes 30-minute waits after missed
Tacoma buses, a cancelled public-transit school connection, a long school-bus journey home and the burden
of carrying hockey gear or groceries on transit. He still bikes his child to school and anticipates rather
than completes some other changes. This is one parent's experience, not a measured week-long trial or
representative evidence about people with disabilities. His acknowledgment that planning and familiarity
could improve transit trips also prevents reading the account as a general travel-time ranking of modes.
Together with the older-adult coverage below, it broadens the access question beyond bicycle availability,
without establishing how common these constraints are or which intervention resolves them.

## Older-adult mobility needs extend beyond commuting

[Streetsblog's January 2026 aging coverage](../sources/2026-streetsblog-aging-transportation.md) adds an
older-adult perspective: a 2022 study reportedly found [about one in ten urban older US adults used transit
in the preceding month](../findings/access/us-older-adults-reported-transit-use.md). This is a participation
measure, unlike worker commute shares or NABSA's rider composition. Its primary methods, age threshold and
observation year were not reviewed; it cannot estimate how many non-users lack usable alternatives to driving.

The article discusses missing bus-stop amenities; Sidara executive Balsam Nehme proposes accessible transit,
walkable or rollable access to destinations and intergenerational housing. This broadens the access question
beyond getting to work or having a nearby bicycle route. Her firm's Seattle, Culver City and Chicago examples
are not evaluations of older-adult benefits. Neither low transit use nor those proposals establishes reduced
isolation, safer cycling or lower emissions, and the article does not independently confirm the bundle's
commuting or shared-micromobility findings.

[Realtor.com's January 2026 coverage](/sources/2026-realtor-transit-housing-driving.md) adds a different study's
[reported stop access and nondriver participation](/findings/access/us-older-adults-reported-stop-access.md):
nearly 58% of older adults lacked neighborhood transit stops, while more than one-quarter of older nondrivers
used transit and nearly 17% used it frequently. Missing age, proximity and frequency definitions prevent pooling
these figures with the earlier monthly-use estimate. Reported associations with walkability and stop density do
not establish that building transit-adjacent housing reduces isolation.

[ABC12's March 2026 Michigan reporting](../sources/2026-abc12-michigan-older-driver-testing.md) adds a
proposed licensing restriction rather than evidence of transportation access: Senate Bill 847 would
require in-person vision, knowledge and driving-skills tests every four years from age 75 and annually
from age 85. It had not received a vote at publication. The reported fatal-crash motivation does not
establish age-specific risk or testing effectiveness. Unlike the transit studies above, this article
provides no mobility-use measures or alternative-transport assessment; neither safety benefits nor
loss of access can be inferred from the proposal alone.

## Policy packages do not establish access or health gains

Opong's [January 2026 New Jersey policy memo](../sources/2026-renovating-new-jersey-built-environment.md)
connects protected-lane maintenance, neighborhood-to-trail access and parking reform near transit.
It adds a proposed response to access barriers, not an evaluation: destinations, affordable housing,
maintenance and eligibility for employer benefits can determine whether nearby routes are usable.
The memo explicitly raises unequal benefit access, displacement concerns and possible road-diet bus
and congestion trade-offs, but does not quantify them or show that its package resolves them.

Its physical and mental health rationale draws on secondary associations across settings. It does
not demonstrate New Jersey health gains, equitable cycling uptake or a lane-induced reduction in
loneliness. Its repeated 12-city safety claim is already represented through Streetsblog, not new
independent evidence; its statewide motorist-distrust percentage lacks a usable sample denominator.
These recommendations therefore complement, rather than strengthen, the observed barrier evidence below.

[NPR's May 2024 health reporting](../sources/2024-npr-cycling-knee-health.md) adds individual-level
associations rather than a policy evaluation. In an elevated-risk sample averaging age 64, bicycling
history was reportedly associated with [21% less X-ray evidence plus symptoms of knee osteoarthritis](../findings/cycling-knee-osteoarthritis-reported.md).
A separate diabetes study reportedly associated one hour of weekly cycling with [about 22% lower
premature mortality](../findings/diabetes-cycling-mortality-reported.md). These concern different
populations and outcomes, not two confirmations of one benefit. The knee comparison is cross-sectional;
the mortality account omits its precise comparator and follow-up. Neither primary paper was reviewed.

Together they support considering potential health benefits without treating observational associations
as prevention effects or transferring percentages to all commuters. They do not evaluate the New Jersey
policy package, measure additional exercise induced by routes or quantify net health gains after traffic
and overuse injuries. Researcher explanations and one cyclist's reported joy add context, not measured
mechanisms or mental-health effects. These outcomes also differ from UCS's modeled air-quality health
benefits below and cannot simply be added to them.

## Housing finance proposals are not delivered access

[Realtor.com's reported housing-growth comparison](/findings/access/us-reported-transit-housing-growth.md)
adds historical context: 2 million units near transit versus 17.6 million away in 2000–2019, with faster growth
near newer stations than comparable neighborhoods but slower growth around older station cohorts. Proximity
boundaries and starting-stock denominators are missing. These counts do not establish how many households
have usable transit, nor whether proposed financing reforms will meet housing demand.

[Streetsblog's January 2026 Build HUBS coverage](../sources/2026-build-hubs-housing-near-transit.md)
adds a federal financing constraint to the local parking and trail-access proposals above. Supporters
argue that procedures designed for major transport projects hinder housing development through TIFIA
and RRIF. The reported proposal would simplify financing and explicitly include development within a
half-mile of bus rapid transit or multimodal paths. This extends the policy discussion beyond building
routes to locating homes near them, not evidence that proximity guarantees usable, affordable travel.

The claimed potential for up to 100,000 homes comes from proponents, with no reported estimation
method or delivery horizon. The article's account of only one prior transit-oriented building project,
a library, was not checked against primary program records. Neither account establishes how many
homes reform would actually add, whether existing residents could afford them, or whether cycling
and transit use would increase. These are proposed mechanisms, not independent confirmation of
access or emissions benefits from the bundle's other land-use recommendations.

[Better Blocks' May 2026 NJ-08 candidate questionnaire](../sources/2026-better-blocks-nj08-candidate-questionnaire.md)
shows that support for housing near transit can still differ over funding conditions: Ali endorses
requirements tied to federal transit investment, while Menendez stresses incentives, tenant protections
and avoiding penalties for fiscally constrained communities. Both support local bus rapid transit
investment, but neither response evaluates access gains. The PDF ends before street-safety answers,
so it cannot establish their cycling commitments. These positions add political context, not evidence
that the financing proposals above deliver affordable travel.

## Reported barriers extend beyond having a nearby route

Rutgers' [2015 New Jersey intercept surveys](../sources/new-jersey-black-hispanic-bicycle-barriers.md)
add direct accounts of cycling constraints in communities selected for large Black and Hispanic populations,
not another representative commute-rate estimate. Collision fear is prominent, but the
[barrier table conflicts with the narrative](../findings/access/nj-bicycle-barrier-survey-limits.md):
police profiling, not pavement, ranks second in Table 5. Multiple-response questions and inconsistent
sample totals prevent turning these reported distributions into precise population prevalence.

[Perceived unfair stops](../findings/access/nj-bicycling-perceived-unfair-police-stops.md) and focus-group
accounts of avoiding particular towns add an access dimension that proximity measures miss. Figure 40
reports such stops among 23% of Black past-year riders and 14% of Hispanic riders, but does not adjust
for cycling exposure or independently establish why stops occurred. Theft and storage concerns in the
same report further distinguish possessing a bicycle from being able to rely on it.

[Streetsblog's June 2025 Brooklyn rider interview](../sources/2025-streetsblog-brooklyn-cyclist-double-summons.md)
adds a different kind of enforcement evidence: one e-bike user's account of a criminal red-light summons
plus a disputed $190 pedestrian-yield ticket during a work trip. She anticipated losing a workday to
court despite relying on the bicycle for work and school transport. Unlike the New Jersey survey,
this is a single incident with no prevalence denominator or adjudicated outcome. It illustrates a
potential time and money burden beyond route availability, not measured cycling deterrence,
discrimination or the safety effects of enforcement.

The [reported 77% preference for a lane on one's street](../findings/access/nj-bicycle-lane-stated-preferences.md)
coexists with practical constraints and doubts that lanes alone would change travel. It concerns unspecified
bicycle lanes, not the protected treatment advocated in the recommendations. Unlike observed commuting,
these are stated preferences; unlike an intervention evaluation, they cannot establish additional riding,
safety or equity gains. This supports considering routes, security and feasible destinations together,
without proving the effectiveness of any proposed package.

## Rail bicycle service is not the same as a usable itinerary

The League's [2026 Amtrak benchmark](../findings/access/amtrak-bicycle-access-benchmark.md) adds a
service-availability measure, not another commute-share estimate. It identifies 39 of 46 lines with carry-on
or trainside checked bicycle service, but station staffing only approximates where passengers can actually
board with bikes. Dual-service corridors score higher partly through a built-in bonus and broader state
investment, not an isolated effect of offering two carriage types. Disputed Capitol Corridor capacity could
change its score from 46 to 83 without any physical change, illustrating sensitivity to the input data.

A [mixed-itinerary booking test](../findings/access/amtrak-mixed-itinerary-bicycle-booking.md) shows why
line-level availability is insufficient: an ineligible Acela return suppressed the bike option for a round
trip with an eligible Northeast Regional outbound, without explaining the incompatible leg. This dated
single test does not measure abandoned journeys, current service or unequal impacts across population groups.
Together these findings extend the distinction between available infrastructure and usable access, without
establishing rail-induced cycling, equity gains or mode shift.

[Frontier Group's reported Amtrak growth](../findings/networks/amtrak-reported-ridership-growth-2025-2026.md)
adds actual passenger-use counts to this service-availability discussion, but not evidence of bicycle-to-rail
journeys. It reports 5.1% FY2024–FY2025 growth and 9% state-supported growth year to date through May
FY2026. These secondary operator statistics do not identify unique travelers, prior modes or avoided car
journeys. New-service comparisons also mix forecasts and potentially unequal operating periods. Thus rail
use can grow without demonstrating improved bicycle access, equitable access or the author's proposed
shift away from cars and planes; continued-investment advocacy is not an evaluated causal effect.

[Business Insider's January 2026 reporting](../sources/2026-business-insider-amtrak-boom.md) supplies
34.5 million FY2025 customer trips from the same operator evidence stream, not an independent study.
Its revenue growth must not be read as passenger growth. Interviews emphasize shorter corridors, while
the reporter's scenic long-distance journey cost nearly four times as much as flying and took about
38 hours longer. That single experience illustrates why enjoyable rail travel and a practical, affordable
alternative are different questions; neither the anecdote nor aggregate growth measures bicycle-to-rail access.

## Destination proximity is another incomplete access measure

Urban Institute reports that [fewer than one-fifth of Los Angeles libraries were within a quarter mile of protected
cycling infrastructure](../findings/access/los-angeles-library-protected-bike-proximity.md). Unlike commute shares,
this concerns destinations near infrastructure, not how people travel. Its denominator is libraries rather than
residents, and the article supplies neither an observation date nor a distance method. Proximity to a segment cannot
establish a connected, safe route from home, library use or equal access across income and racial groups.

The authors' recommendations to prioritize underserved communities, involve existing residents in route decisions
and offer classes and bike-purchasing assistance identify possible responses, not evaluated remedies. Gentrification
concerns are acknowledged but displacement is not measured. Together with commuting evidence, this argues for
keeping use, available alternatives and route quality distinct rather than treating any one indicator as equity.

## Planning potential is not observed demand

[NJTPA's 2023 revised trip-potential analysis](../findings/networks/njtpa-trip-potential-score-limits.md) adds a
planning measure rather than another observation of travel. It combines spatial indicators into 0–100 scores,
without reported validation against observed trips. Phillipsburg's high-scoring bicycle area extends farther than
its pedestrian area partly because the model searches one mile rather than a quarter mile, not because it measured
more cycling. These scores cannot be pooled with commute shares or all-purpose travel estimates.

Poverty and no-vehicle households raise potential scores by construction. This acknowledges transport need as a
planning consideration but does not independently confirm the Census income associations or show equitable access.
As with library proximity, nearby destinations do not establish usable routes. NJTPA considered barriers, safety
screening and environmental-justice data separately when developing a conceptual network. Low modeled potential
does not rule out unmet needs, and high potential does not demonstrate delivered benefits.

## Reduced-driving scenarios are not observed access gains

UCS's [Freedom to Move scenarios](../findings/us-freedom-to-move-energy-health-scenarios.md) add a
prospective comparison to historical travel measures: all pathways reach net zero in 2050, while two
assume less light-duty driving. Table 1 estimates $110/$201 billion in energy capital savings and
$32–64/$63–128 billion in air-quality health benefits for low/high reductions over 2025–2050.
These results illustrate conditional resource savings, not measured responses to cycling investment;
internal narrative/table differences and limited supplementary methods constrain precision.

Its [household savings estimate](../findings/access/us-freedom-to-move-household-savings.md), above
$5.9 trillion cumulatively through 2050 in the high-reduction scenario, includes fuel, maintenance and
preserved vehicle value. It differs from observed car costs below and is not an independent confirmation
of savings from switching to a bicycle. Fuel overlaps the report's separate energy-account savings, and
alternative-mode implementation costs are not fully netted out. Neither national savings nor the assumed
driving reductions establish maintained access or equitable distribution. Community accounts in the
[report](../sources/2024-freedom-to-move.md) motivate those goals but do not evaluate their achievement.
[Streetsblog's December 2024 republication](../sources/2024-streetsblog-freedom-to-move-savings.md)
repeats this model under a $6.2 trillion headline, rather than independently confirming it. Lead author
Kevin Shen's interview illustrates retaining some driving while shedding a second car or substituting
e-bike trips. Those examples explain the proposed choices, not observed uptake, household cash savings
or equitable access; the article's pocketbook language should not erase depreciation or investment costs.

Maryland's [reported RMI scenario](../findings/access/maryland-reported-mode-shift-savings.md) adds
an annual state household estimate: over $3,000 in fuel, maintenance and depreciation savings if
climate and mode-shift goals are achieved. Coalition coverage does not supply the scenario year,
reference case or full alternative-travel costs. This is not observed savings from the proposed
TCA Act or governor's executive order, and cannot be added to UCS's national cumulative total.
Neither supportive institutional testimony nor modeled average savings establishes equitable access.

Streetsblog's [Transportation for America coverage](../sources/2026-streetsblog-world-class-transit.md)
adds a different [conditional ownership-savings scenario](../findings/access/us-transit-moonshot-ownership-savings.md):
over $5.4 trillion during 2026–2045 if roughly $4.6 trillion in transit investment reduces the ownership
rate by one-quarter of a percent annually. This is an assumed response reported secondhand, not measured
payback or confirmation of UCS's estimate. The different horizons and accounting do not support adding
these totals. The article's $4.6/$4.7-trillion discrepancy and missing financial methods remain unresolved.
Its 27 versus 133 vehicles per 100,000 residents comparison is a rough US/selected-city service proxy,
not direct destination access: fleet definitions, land use, frequency and reliability matter, and walkable
or bikeable neighborhoods may need fewer transit vehicles. Neither larger fleets nor projected avoided
ownership costs establish equitable access or savings from a cycling project.

## Car costs add context, not measured savings from cycling

NerdWallet's [reported AAA new-car estimate](../findings/access/us-new-car-ownership-cost-2023.md) of $12,182 in
2023 annual ownership costs adds an affordability consideration to the vehicle-availability evidence. It describes
a new vehicle driven 15,000 miles annually, with a five-year ownership assumption, not the vehicles or budgets of
the historical Census workers. It includes depreciation and must not be equated with the article's differently
defined, internally inconsistent monthly expense calculator.

AAA's own [2024 fact sheet](../findings/access/us-new-car-ownership-cost-2024.md) adds a later standardized
estimate of $12,297 annually under five-year, 75,000-mile assumptions, including $4,680 annual depreciation.
Its average costs of $1.06, $0.82 and $0.70 per mile at 10,000, 15,000 and 20,000 annual miles illustrate why
full ownership averages cannot be treated as savings from each driving mile replaced. This is another annual
AAA estimate, not independent evidence of cycling benefits or a review of the underlying 2023 study.

MoneyGeek's [later AAA-based estimate](../findings/access/us-new-car-ownership-cost-2025-reported.md) reports
$11,577 annually for **2025**, despite its 2026 headline, at 15,000 miles per year. Its $6,400 annual gap
between half-ton pickups and small sedans shows why vehicle choice matters to cost comparisons. It does not
measure avoidable trip costs or benefits of cycling. The [article's real-cost analysis](../sources/2026-moneygeek-car-ownership-cost.md)
reports an 8.2% inflation-adjusted annual decline and a 13.8% ratio to estimated median household income,
not an observed household budget share. Income estimation and an inconsistent 2023 trend percentage limit
precision. This extends the AAA time series rather than adding independent confirmation of earlier estimates.

NerdWallet's [car-cost inflation index](../findings/access/us-nerdwallet-car-cost-inflation.md) reports 38% growth
since April 2019 in its June 2024 snapshot. Price changes, full ownership costs and costs avoidable by replacing
individual trips are different measures. Neither this index nor the AAA estimate establishes what a household
would save by cycling, whether it can relinquish a vehicle or whether safe routes serve its destinations. Together,
these sources describe cost context and commuting associations, not an evaluated affordability benefit of bike lanes.

LendingTree's [Q2 2026 Experian coverage](../findings/access/us-2026-reported-car-payments.md) adds financing
commitments: $765/$542 average monthly new/used payments and 69.5/67.9-month terms. These are neither
AAA-style total ownership costs nor averages for all households. Credit-tier differences also reflect different
loan sizes and terms, not an isolated credit-score effect. This later-period reporting extends NerdWallet's
payment context without independently confirming its earlier figures or demonstrating that cycling can replace
ownership. Loan payments cannot simply be added to depreciation-based ownership estimates without accounting
for the different treatment of vehicle purchase costs.

## Parking prices do not establish equitable access

[Berkeley's June 2026 parking-fee coverage](../sources/2026-sfgate-berkeley-parking-fees.md) adds a local
policy mechanism rather than another ownership-cost estimate. SFGATE reports an initial council vote to
replace $85-per-vehicle residential permits with $100/$125/$150 first/second/third-car tiers for 2028 permits,
extend metered hours and introduce Sunday charges. A final ordinance vote was still pending at publication.
Officials projected revenue and better turnover; the dissenting councilmember warned of regressive burdens
and business losses. Neither account measures realized household or business effects, reduced car ownership
or increased cycling. Planned cashless meters with eventual reloadable Clipper-card acceptance also separate
payment accessibility from fee levels: the article does not establish that an alternative for people without
credit cards or smartphones was operational. These proposals add access questions, not evidence that higher
parking charges by themselves provide usable, affordable alternatives.

## Transit removal can suppress activities rather than merely change modes

A [Chicago regional simulation](../findings/access/chicago-transit-removal-activity-loss.md) adds a counterfactual,
not another observed commuting pattern. Removing all transit while assuming 1.9 million extra cars reduces
modeled daily activities 8.6% regionally and 19.9% in the city, with larger non-work than work/school losses.
Travel times still rise. Unlike mode shares among completed trips, cancelled activities expose potential lost
access; reduced traffic from abandoned journeys need not indicate better mobility.

The model's [gender and income shares of cancellations](../findings/access/chicago-transit-removal-equity-shares.md)
are not within-group cancellation rates. Women account for 53.0% of city cancellations and the bottom two
household-income quintiles for 48.0%, but population and household shares do not supply baseline activity
exposure. As with cyclist profiles, composition must not be mistaken for individual likelihood or a measured
benefit distribution from new infrastructure.

Its [approximately 13:1 economic comparison](../findings/business/chicago-transit-removal-economic-accounting.md)
likewise differs from observed household savings: $20.4 billion of the $35.4 billion annual valuation comes
from assumed extra cars, with spending and valued time making up the rest. This extreme joint scenario does
not establish marginal funding returns, the effects of smaller cuts, or cycling benefits. It adds a reason to
track unfulfilled activities alongside completed trips, not a transferable numerical forecast.

## Historical tax preferences do not measure equitable access

[Knittel's review of 1970s U.S. gasoline-tax polling](../findings/access/us-1970s-gasoline-tax-polling.md)
adds stated policy preferences, not another household-cost or commuting measure. A reported 1974 survey found
13% support for an unqualified ten-cent increase, rising to 51% when the question combined a tax credit with
ending Middle East oil dependence. The promised outcome was hypothetical, not an evaluated tax design.

Historical opponents cited low incomes and car-dependent commutes. These concerns complement the distinction
between observed travel and genuinely available alternatives, but do not demonstrate actual tax incidence or
show that rationing was fairer. Separate polls favored vehicle taxes or rationing; support varied with time,
prices and wording. Neither opposition nor conditional support establishes current attitudes, affordable
alternatives or a cycling benefit. Revenue use and access to alternatives require evidence beyond opinion polls.

## Purchase assistance is not an evaluated access gain

[ACEEE's March 2025 e-bike incentive descriptions](../findings/access/aceee-2025-reported-e-bike-incentives.md)
add reported policy designs: Colorado's $450 point-of-sale credit and Rhode Island's higher income-qualified
rebate cap. Unlike the household-cost estimates, these describe assistance toward a bicycle purchase;
unlike user surveys, they do not measure participation. Program availability, eligibility, take-up and
additional purchases are separate questions.

The report provides no evaluation of car replacement or benefit distribution. Its affordability rationale
therefore does not show that income-based assistance closed access gaps, just as NABSA's discount-program
presence does not explain its user composition. These historical terms also leave current funding and
eligibility unresolved. Neither a subsidy nor legal permission guarantees safe routes to destinations.

## Incentive-user surveys do not establish lasting mode shift

Purchase assistance describes a policy offer; SIAMUS's [Pin Bike participant survey](../findings/networks/pin-bike-reported-use-mode-shift-limits.md)
adds reported experience with riding incentives and gamification. Among 517 respondents across nine Italian
cities, Chart 10 reports 29% using Pin Bike daily and 45% four or more days weekly. Yet 43% already
cycled before participation, and 25% reporting prior car use does not measure displaced car trips.
Reported commute-time distributions were almost unchanged. Frequent service use and changed motivations
therefore do not establish additional cycling, faster journeys or a population-wide mode shift.

[Willingness to continue without incentives](../findings/networks/pin-bike-post-incentive-intentions.md)
averaged 4.2/5, but this is a hypothetical intention, not observed post-payment retention. Projects still
running and already ended were pooled; roughly 7.4% of invited users responded. Selection, recall and
missing item-level denominators limit both results. Unlike household commute surveys, these describe
selected program participants; unlike a controlled evaluation, they cannot separate incentives,
gamification and infrastructure. Favorable intentions do not demonstrate lasting or equitable access gains.

## Legal eligibility is distinct from usable access

[New Jersey's reported June 2025 e-bike requirements](../findings/access/new-jersey-2025-e-bike-requirements.md)
add a different access consideration: NorthJersey.com describes low-speed Class 1/2 bicycles as exempt from
licensing, registration and insurance, unlike Class 3 motorized bicycles. Bicycle laws, helmet requirements and
municipal location or age restrictions still qualify that exemption. A state vehicle classification alone cannot
establish permission to ride a particular route.

This historical secondary account does not establish current legal requirements; the cited primary rules and later
changes were not reviewed. Alongside car-cost estimates and library proximity, it shows why administrative
requirements, affordability and route availability must remain distinct. It measures neither household savings nor
whether the reported exemptions increased commuting, equitable access or safety.

## Post-crash benefits are a separate access question

The primary [2024 Goyco opinion](../findings/access/new-jersey-goyco-scooter-pip.md) shows that bicycle-like
operating rules do not necessarily confer pedestrian insurance benefits. New Jersey's Supreme Court denied
PIP benefits under the policy and statute at issue to a low-speed electric-scooter rider: his motor-powered,
road-designed scooter placed him outside the No-Fault Act's pedestrian definition, unlike an ordinary bicycle.
This is a dated holding about that scooter and policy, not a ruling on every pedal-assist bicycle or proof of
current coverage. It measures neither treatment access nor premium effects.

[KYW's coverage of the same ruling](../sources/2024-kyw-new-jersey-scooter-pip.md) adds an insurance-industry
representative's prediction of premiums in the hundreds of dollars per month if eligibility expanded,
without pricing evidence or specified policy terms. His suggested medical-insurance or at-fault-driver
coverage alternatives are conditional, not guaranteed recovery. This interview adds a stakeholder perspective,
not an independent legal result or evidence that broader benefits would cause those costs. The article's
broad e-bike framing should not extend the scooter-specific holding to every pedal-assist configuration.

Epstein's August 2026 commentary on [pedestrian PIP coverage in Boone](../findings/access/new-jersey-boone-pedestrian-pip.md)
reports required medical benefits for an uninsured pedestrian struck by a Lyft driver carrying a passenger.
Unlike e-bike operating requirements, this concerns access to benefits after a crash, not permission to travel.
The author's argument for prompt treatment is a policy position, not a measured improvement in care, affordability
or safety. The underlying ruling was not reviewed, and the account cannot establish coverage for cyclists or all
rideshare activity states. Neither legal account measures whether people can reach destinations safely or affordably.

## Central-city shares do not describe the whole region

[Euronews's Paris survey coverage](../findings/networks/paris-reported-travel-shares.md) reports
11.2% cycling versus 4.3% driving for central-city trips, with walking and transit still dominant.
But cars accounted for 61% of trips within more distant suburbs, while transit led journeys between
those suburbs and Paris. A central-city cycling headline therefore does not describe region-wide
transport choices. These are trip shares from reported GPS/journal research, not commute-only shares
or shares of residents who cycle. Unlike the Copenhagen denominator contrasts below, this account
also distinguishes journeys within suburbs from journeys linking them to the core. Missing fieldwork
dates, precise boundaries and reviewed primary methods limit comparison; concurrent investment and
car restrictions do not establish a causal policy effect or equitable access gains.

## Count the travel relevant to the question

[ACS main-mode commute shares](../findings/networks/acs-active-commuting-measurement-limits.md) leave out non-work
travel and shorter walking or cycling legs in multimodal commutes. City and metropolitan-area shares also describe
different populations. Use matching trip purposes, geographic boundaries and denominators before comparing rates;
do not treat a commute share as an all-trip cycling share.

PBIC's [2024 youth planning brief](../sources/2024-creating-accessible-equitable-safe-complete-networks-young-pedestrians.md)
adds a population exclusion: worker commute data for ages 16 and older cannot describe younger children's travel.
The brief's broad wording about “U.S. Census data” concerns this worker-travel measure, not every Census dataset.
Children's destinations include parks, friends and activities, often outside adult peak periods; school counts alone
would also leave those journeys unmeasured. PBIC recommends youth surveys, appropriately timed counts and participatory
mapping. Its Boston example prioritized low-income youth, youth of color and underserved neighborhoods, but reports
no numerical equity improvement. Gathering youth input is not itself evidence of improved access.[^youth2024]

The same population distinction affects safety targeting: [Philadelphia's reported youth injury-crash share outside
its high-injury network](../findings/safety/philadelphia-youth-crash-network-coverage.md) shows why an all-user
priority map need not cover most youth injury locations. It is a crash-location measure, not proof that off-network
walking is riskier or that recommended youth-focused infrastructure has delivered safer access.

Copenhagen's [reported 43% work-cycling share in 2017](../findings/networks/copenhagen-2017-cycle-commute-share.md)
adds a different setting, but the news account leaves the denominator and mode definition unspecified. Its “up 2
percent” wording does not distinguish relative growth from percentage points. Unlike the ACS evidence, it cannot
be assumed to use a main-mode rule or a resident-worker population. Neither the U.S. comparisons nor this municipal
headline establishes causal infrastructure effects.

WEF's [Copenhagen travel shares](../findings/networks/copenhagen-wef-cycling-shares.md) make the denominator issue
concrete: 29% of all journeys, 41% of work/study commutes and 62% for those both living and working/studying in
the city are different measures. The article does not explicitly date those shares or supply mode-assignment
rules. Its 41% cannot safely be combined with the Copenhagen Post's 43% to infer a trend, nor does high cycling
use establish safe or equitable access.

Washington's [2017 mileage-method comparison](../findings/networks/king-county-travel-mile-estimation.md) shows
that moving beyond commute shares does not automatically yield reliable all-purpose travel totals. For King County
within the Puget Lowlands, national survey rates, count expansion and a demographic model produced widely different
annual miles. The authors explicitly reject using these demonstrations as actual-travel baselines. Unlike ACS,
NHTS includes recreational out-and-back trips and transit-access legs, but omits some very short travel; national
rates also need not represent Washington.

[Nonrandom count sites and borrowed temporal factors](../findings/networks/washington-count-expansion-limits.md)
create a different problem: extrapolating busy-site counts can overstate network-wide use, while one commuter bridge's
bicycle patterns cannot reliably annualize pedestrian or other-region counts. The two count-based methods share
these inputs, so their estimates are not independent confirmations. The report recommends improved surveys for
statewide totals and representative, appropriately adjusted counts for finer geography, not a universal correction
from commute share or corridor counts to total miles.

[Streetsblog's 2024 League coverage](../findings/networks/us-2024-state-bicycle-counting-gaps.md) adds a
national program-coverage gap: only 22 states reportedly operate or fund bicycle surveys/counts. Its claimed
four-state decline since 2022 lacks explained survey comparability. Unlike the Washington methods demonstration,
this is secondary reporting about program presence, not count accuracy or riding exposure. Neither program
presence nor a high bicycle-friendly ranking supplies the denominator needed to interpret rising cycling deaths.

TfL's [2017 London indicators](../findings/networks/london-2017-cycling-indicator-differences.md) further show that
trip counts, distance and boundary crossings can move differently: cycle trips fell 0.3% while daily cycle-kilometres
rose 4.3%. Central London's 24% daytime cycle share in 2016 is a share of counted vehicles, not all person trips.
Quarterly central counts also reveal winter troughs about 20% below summer peaks. Unlike Washington's rejected
baseline demonstrations, these are agency monitoring estimates, but they still cannot substitute for each other
or identify an infrastructure effect.

The [2022 recovery estimates](../findings/networks/london-2022-cycling-recovery-measurement.md) add a second
comparability problem: TfL revised absolute cycle-distance levels retrospectively, so older published levels
cannot be spliced onto the new series. Spring 2022 weekday distance was 18% above 2019, versus 14% growth
in estimated seven-day trips. Unrepresentative 2021 area-count timing prevents interpreting that observation as
a full-year decline. These later observations extend related TfL monitoring, rather than independently replicating
Report 11. They support recovery, not a causal lane effect or measured reduction in driving.

## Shared trips are not equivalent to displaced driving

NABSA's [2021 recovery estimates](../findings/networks/nabsa-2021-ridership-recovery.md) put North American
shared-micromobility trips at 128 million, above 2020 but below 2019 despite more vehicles and city coverage.
Unlike commute shares or corridor counts, these are all-purpose shared-fleet trip estimates, with incomplete
smaller-system data. They do not measure total cycling or establish an infrastructure effect.

Its [mode-replacement compilation](../findings/networks/nabsa-mode-replacement-survey-limits.md) reports 37%
car replacement, but also 37% walking and 10% transit replacement, using mixed 2018–2021 surveys. Transit
replacement does not preclude transit access: the [same report](../sources/2021-shared-micromobility-state-of-the-industry-report.md)
reports 63% of riders using micromobility to connect to transit and 18% of trips serving that purpose. Rider
prevalence, trip purpose and hypothetical alternatives are different measures, not a net change in transit use.

The [modeled emissions benefit](../findings/nabsa-2021-emissions-accounting-limits.md) depends on auto replacement
and excludes operations and lifecycle effects. Neither total ridership nor the estimated 54 million pounds of
avoided CO2 demonstrates net lifecycle savings or an environmental benefit from every trip.

[ACEEE's state VMT comparison](../findings/networks/aceee-vmt-overlapping-period-limits.md) adds aggregate
motor-vehicle distance per person, not another micromobility replacement survey. New Jersey's reported
−2.7% average combines three overlapping long-period changes, including +1.5% for 2014–2023. The
negative average cannot establish continuous decline, reduced total driving or a cycling substitution
effect. Credited targets coexist with positive average changes in Colorado and Massachusetts; policy
adoption is distinct from outcomes, without that contrast identifying policy success or failure.

The evidence supports historical travel descriptions, not a standardized international ranking. Current patterns,
unmet travel needs and route safety remain unresolved.
